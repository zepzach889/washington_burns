First Mexican War

The First Mexican War (1835–1837), known in Mexico as the First Mexican Civil War, was fought between the liberal Republic of Mexico — joined from June 1835 by the United States — and the conservative Empire of Mexico proclaimed by counter-coup in Mexico City. The war ended in stalemate and partition under the Treaty of New Orleans of March 3, 1837, leaving two Mexicos on the continent. It was the young republic's first foreign war since 1815, the occasion of its first presidential assassination, and — in the resentments it left behind — a formative wound in American politics, whose unresolved Texas question would return as the Second Mexican War less than a decade later.

Background

The liberal coup of 1833 had established a republican government in Mexico; in January 1835, conservatives struck back, launching a counter-coup in Mexico City and proclaiming the Empire of Mexico. What the plotters had hoped would be a swift change of government collapsed into full civil war between Liberals and Imperialists, with the northern provinces — Texas included — siding with the constitutionalist Republic. Inside Texas, the crisis forced a truce between the province's rival factions: the independence movement of Stephen Austin, which had been planning a revolt of its own, and the pro-Mexican bloc of Governor Anthony Benton, author of the province's new constitution. Austin proposed that the separatists fight alongside the constitutionalists against the Empire, with the future of Texas to be seriously discussed once the war was won. Benton agreed.

American Entry (1835)

The Constitutionalist victory at the Battle of Chihuahua on May 11, 1835, convinced many observers that President Manuel Ortega's government — relocated to Santa Fe — could win the war or at least sustain a viable state. On May 30, allies of President John C. Calhoun proposed that the United States declare war on the Imperialist faction. Federalists and many Democrats balked, but impassioned pleas from representatives of Santa Fe and Texas — and back-room bargaining over Indian removal and the postwar fate of Texas — swung enough votes. On June 21, 1835, Congress declared war on the "Imperialist Rebels" and authorized a draft of more than 50,000 men.

The War and the Home Front

By the end of 1835 American soldiers were fighting alongside Ortega's forces, pushing slowly south against the Imperialists under General Raul Guerra, the future Emperor Raul I — at a cost in casualties higher than anyone had promised. A second draft order in December, calling up 50,000 more men, touched off the first of many draft riots in New England, where the war was widely seen as a Southern war of expansion despite official assurances that the United States would not annex Texas. Through early 1836 the country split along the fault line that would define the era: Southern enthusiasm, Northern ambivalence shading into open hostility, and major anti-draft protests in Boston, New York, Philadelphia — and even Richmond.

The Assassination of President Calhoun

On May 1, 1836, while delivering a pro-war speech in Philadelphia, President Calhoun was shot by an anti-war protestor named Hiram Polk. He died eight days later, and Vice President Giles Brandon became the first vice president to ascend to the presidency — tragically, not the last. The assassination transformed the politics of the war overnight: the anti-war movement collapsed, New England's Federalists scrambled to disavow the act, recruiting stations across the South and West saw a surge of volunteers, and in June Congress established the Presidential Guard, the elite protective unit that endures to this day. Brandon, invoking the slain president's memory and a "just cause to further the light of liberty," won increased funding and another draft — but the combined American–Constitutionalist victories along the factional frontier remained so costly that many felt like draws, and by year's end war fatigue had crept back into communities across the country.

The Treaty of New Orleans

The exhaustion was greater still in both halves of Mexico, and the ceasefire agreed by Ortega and Guerra in February 1837 came as a relief to all parties. The following month, delegates from the United States, the Republic of Mexico, the Empire of Mexico, the Province of Texas, and the self-styled Texas Free State met at New Orleans. The settlement was pragmatic: each Mexican faction kept the territory it held, dropping all claims on the other; the Texas Free State remained within the Republic in exchange for semi-autonomous status — distinct from Benton's Province of Texas around Franklin-on-the-Brazos and San Antonio — and the Empire's price for agreement was an American pledge never to annex the Free State or any other Mexican territory, which the Brandon administration readily gave. The Treaty of New Orleans was signed on March 3, 1837, and swiftly approved by the Senate.

Legacy

The war left two Mexicos, a half-answered Texas question, and a bitter taste in much of the United States. Outside the South, the conflict was widely resented and blamed on the Republican Party that had driven the intervention — resentment that helped cost President Brandon the election of 1838 and shadowed Republican politics for a generation. The precedents it set proved durable in other ways: the Presidential Guard, born of the Calhoun assassination, still protects the executive today, and the American non-annexation pledge of 1837 became the template repeated at New Orleans in 1847 and Copenhagen in 1906. The peace itself lasted six years; when the Republic it had preserved collapsed in 1843, the unfinished business of the First Mexican War became the Second.
