Hartford Resolution
Adopted January 5, 1815 · Hartford, Connecticut

The Hartford Resolution was the concluding document of the Hartford Convention, adopted on January 5, 1815 and presented to Congress at Philadelphia the following month. It comprised a statement of the New England states' grievances against the federal government together with seven proposed amendments to the Constitution of 1787.

Congress took no action on it, and President James Madison refused to receive the delegates who brought it. The Resolution nonetheless became the defining statement of Federalist constitutional aims for a generation, and four of its seven proposals were enacted in substance through the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments as part of the Grand Compromise of 1817.

Background

The Convention had been called to address both the immediate question of New England's defence and the longer grievance that the constitutional structure of the republic systematically disadvantaged the commercial states. The Resolution addressed itself almost entirely to the second. Its drafters proceeded from the position that the War of 1812 had been made possible by three defects: a presidency insufficiently constrained by the states, a Congress able to declare war on a bare majority, and a southern voting weight inflated by the counting of persons held in bondage.

Provisions

The Resolution proposed that the three-fifths clause be repealed, and representation and direct taxation apportioned upon free persons only; that a declaration of war require a two-thirds vote of both houses of Congress; that the admission of a new state require the same; that the imposition of any commercial embargo require a two-thirds vote, and that no embargo remain in force beyond sixty days; that the president be limited to a single term; that no president be succeeded by a citizen of his own state; and that no person not a natural-born citizen be eligible to any federal office.

The order was not accidental. The three-fifths repeal stood first because the drafters held every other grievance to follow from it, and the two-thirds requirements were framed as remedies for a majority the clause had manufactured.

Reception

The Resolution was denounced across the South and West as an attempt to extract by threat what New England could not obtain by votes, and the secrecy of the Convention's proceedings was used to suggest that its published demands were the moderate remnant of something worse. The provision excluding naturalised citizens from office was attacked in the middle states, where it was correctly identified as directed at particular officeholders of foreign birth rather than at any principle.

Madison's refusal to receive the committee closed the matter for the remainder of his term. But the arrival of the peace terms in the same weeks removed the possibility of dismissing the document as the work of defeatists, and Federalist candidates campaigned on its provisions in every subsequent election of the period.

Enactment

The Resolution's provisions were realised in 1817, when Federalist members of Congress made them the price of their cooperation with President William Crawford. Crawford, needing an amendment to resolve the vacancy in the vice presidency and a Senate willing to dispose of the Wisconsin Treaty, opened negotiations at the executive residence in Philadelphia in May of that year.

The Fourteenth Amendment carried the single presidential term, fixed at six years, together with the bar on successive presidents from the same state. The Fifteenth carried the two-thirds requirements for declarations of war and for the admission of new states. Both were packaged with Crawford's own succession amendment and announced as components of the Grand Compromise on May 31, 1817. The embargo provisions and the exclusion of naturalised citizens were dropped in negotiation and never seriously revived.

The three-fifths demand

The repeal of the three-fifths clause was the Resolution's first proposal and the only one its authors regarded as indispensable. It was refused absolutely in 1817. Crawford's southern support was conditioned on its exclusion, and the Federalist negotiators accepted its loss in order to secure the remainder — a decision defended at the time as realism and attacked within their own party for a generation as the abandonment of the principle for which the Convention had been called.

The clause survived every subsequent attempt at amendment and was removed only with the Constitution of 1861, which abolished the institution it had been written to accommodate. That the demand went unmet for forty-six years, while the rest of the Hartford programme was law within three, was cited repeatedly in the decades before the crisis of 1857 as evidence that the constitutional order could accommodate every New England grievance except the one that mattered.

Legacy

The Resolution is the earliest document in American constitutional history to propose the reform of the executive that became settled American practice, and the single six-year term it demanded has governed the presidency without interruption since 1821.

Its opponents' characterisation of it as a sectional ultimatum has largely receded. Its provisions are now more often read as the first coherent argument that the Constitution of 1787 had defects requiring structural correction rather than interpretation — an argument that the crisis of 1857 and the convention that followed it would carry to its conclusion.
