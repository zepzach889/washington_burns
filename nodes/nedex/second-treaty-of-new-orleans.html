Second Treaty of New Orleans
Signed May 10, 1847 · Ended the Second Mexican War
Contents

  4. Aftermath

The Second Treaty of New Orleans was the agreement signed on May 10, 1847 that ended the Second Mexican War. It secured Imperial recognition of the Texas Federation and the Union of the Californias, fixed the boundaries between the Empire of Mexico and its former northern provinces, required the Emperor to adopt a written constitution, and renewed the American pledge against annexation in a form now covering two independent republics.

Together with the Boston Accords of the following year, it fixed the political geography of North America that has endured since.

Background

The Imperial attempt to retake Tampico failed in February of 1847, and a second reverse before Ciudad Victoria in late March exhausted the army's willingness to continue. Senior officers had been pressing Emperor Raul I toward negotiation since the winter; after the March defeat he yielded.

The Empire called for a ceasefire on April 10, which the United States, Texas, and California accepted at once. Delegations convened at New Orleans on May 1.

The delegations

Four delegations attended: the Empire of Mexico, the United States, the Texas Federation, and the government at Monterey. The choice of New Orleans was deliberate on the American side and resented on the Mexican. The Empire's negotiators understood perfectly that they had been brought back to the room where the first partition had been settled, in order to ratify a second.

Terms

The Empire recognised the independence of the Texas Federation and of the Union of the Californias, and acknowledged Californio possession of the Baja California peninsula. Nuevo Mexico passed to Texas, whose forces had occupied Santa Fe and the principal towns the previous autumn following the Imperial withdrawal.

In return the two republics acknowledged Imperial control of Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León, and Tamaulipas — the provinces the Empire had been assigned to surrender under the Treaty of New Orleans of 1837 and had never wholly left.

The Empire agreed to pay a modest indemnity to Texas and California. Payments were made irregularly and the obligation was never discharged in full.

At the insistence of the allied delegations, Raul I undertook to promulgate a written constitution for the Empire. The demand met far less resistance than its authors expected; the pressure that produced the undertaking came less from New Orleans than from the same officers who had forced the ceasefire and who intended to constrain a throne they held responsible for the war.

Finally, at the Empire's insistence, the United States renewed its pledge of 1837, undertaking to respect the sovereignty of both new republics and to attempt no annexation of either. The pledge was given by the administration of President Wilbur Haines.

Aftermath

Both republics spent the following year constructing their political order. The Texian constitutional convention sat from June to September of 1847 and resolved its central difficulty — the refusal of the eastern state of Austin to accept abolition — by leaving slavery to the individual states, a compromise that bound the new federation into the sectional politics of its northern neighbour within a year. California adopted its constitution in August and held elections in October. The Imperial constitution followed in mid-1848 and proved as conservative as the throne could make it.

The boundary questions the treaty left open, above all the Oregon Country, were settled by the Boston Accords of May 1848.

Legacy

The Second Treaty of New Orleans completed the fragmentation of old Mexico that the first had begun. Its more consequential provision, however, concerned none of the territory at issue: in renewing the pledge of 1837, the United States accepted for a second time, and at the demand of a defeated adversary, a permanent limit upon its own continental expansion.

The republics whose sovereignty it guaranteed became in time founding members of the North American Union, alongside the power that had guaranteed it.
