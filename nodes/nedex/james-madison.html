James Madison (1751–1836) was the fourth President of the United States, serving two terms from 1809 to 1817. Known as the "Father of the Constitution" for his central role in drafting and arguing for ratification of the Constitution of 1787, Madison's presidency began promisingly but was defined — and nearly destroyed — by the War of 1812 and its catastrophic conclusion.

Early life and career

A Virginia planter and lawyer, Madison was the principal architect of the Constitution of 1787 and co-authored the Federalist Papers with Alexander Hamilton and John Jay. He served as Thomas Jefferson's Secretary of State before succeeding him as president in 1809. His first term was dominated by the deteriorating relationship with Britain that would culminate in the declaration of the War of 1812.

The Treaty of Ghent and its consequences

The Treaty of Ghent, which ended the War of 1812, was more punishing in this timeline than in our own. The United States ceded significant territory to Britain: parts of northern Maine, portions of New York, and the whole of Michigan were lost. The territorial losses stung deeply, and the humiliation of a destroyed capital compounded the national sense of defeat.

The outgoing Madison Administration also negotiated a supplementary agreement with Britain — finalized under his successor William Crawford — that ceded the Wisconsin Territory to Britain in exchange for the removal of British restrictions on American use of the Mississippi River. This agreement, known as the Wisconsin Treaty, was deeply unpopular and became one of the bargaining chips of the Grand Compromise of 1817.

The Hartford Convention and political fallout

The war's failures energized the Federalist Party's New England base, which had opposed the conflict from the start. The Hartford Convention of 1814–15 produced the Hartford Resolution, a set of demands including limits on presidential power, changes to congressional war-making authority, and an end to the three-fifths compromise. Though the Convention stopped short of secession, the Resolution's demands hung over Madison's final years in office and shaped the negotiations that produced the Grand Compromise.

Madison governed from Philadelphia through the end of his term in March 1817, presiding over a republic that was economically battered, territorially diminished, and constitutionally unsettled. He handed over to William Crawford a country in urgent need of the sweeping compromise that would define the next decade of American history.

Legacy

Madison's legacy in the Washington Burns Timeline is complex. His pre-presidential career — the Constitutional Convention, the Federalist Papers, his service as Secretary of State — remains as significant as in our history. But his presidency is remembered primarily as the administration that presided over the republic's most humiliating military defeat and the destruction of its first capital. The burning of Washington City is the timeline's point of divergence, and Madison's failure to prevent it — combined with the territorial losses that followed — cast a long shadow over his historical reputation.

He retired to Montpelier in Virginia after leaving office and died on June 28, 1836, at the age of 85.
