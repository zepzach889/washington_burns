Matanzas War
Also known as the Caribbean War · 1905–1906 · United States and allied independence forces vs. the Kingdom of Spain

The Matanzas War (1905–1906), known contemporaneously and still widely as the Caribbean War, was fought between the United States — together with Cuban and Puerto Rican independence forces — and the Kingdom of Spain, over the slaveholding American exile enclave at Matanzas Bay, Cuba, and its decades-long campaign of gun-running to anti-government radicals in the American South. The war ended Spanish rule in the Caribbean, established the United Provinces of Cuba and the Republic of Puerto Rico, and — in the words of President Cornelius Roosevelt II's victory address — "bound the final wounds that remained from the War Between the States." It was the republic's first major conflict in half a century.

Background: The Matanzas Exiles

In the years after the Free State victory of 1860, an estimated fifteen to twenty thousand members of the defeated planter elite fled the United States for Spanish Cuba rather than submit to abolition, settling around the town of Matanzas and founding the enclave communities of Port Hawthorne and New Charleston, where slavery remained legal under Spanish law. In 1864 the exile families signed the Matanzas Bay Compact, a mutual-aid pact to preserve their old order under a loose legislature they called "The Assembly." The Brandt administration, aware of the migration, chose to permit it — "Let Cuba be an outlet for the most radical, so they might not be a scourge to us," as the Secretary of War put it — over the objections of the Exterior Secretary. For four decades the Compact communities festered under Spanish protection, maintaining intermittent contact with separatist cells in the Coastal South that federal reports long dismissed as "essentially harmless."

The Road to War (1902–1904)

That assessment collapsed on August 23, 1902, when the USS Philadelphia intercepted the SS Belle of Matanzas off South Carolina, laden with guns, ammunition, and explosives bound for anti-Union radicals — the Belle of Matanzas affair. President Alexander Fleak, unwilling to risk war with Spain while the Great European War raged, refused military action; his successor Peter Landon held the same line through mounting provocations: the November 1903 San Angelo incident, in which gun-runners fired on a boarding party from the USS Manhattan and killed Ensign Thomas M. Keeler; the beaching of the schooner Spirit of 1861 near Key Largo, its hold full of Spanish-made rifles and letters to extremists in three states; and Spain's persistent refusal to let American investigators near the Matanzas communities. The crisis consumed the election of 1904. When the patrol boat USS Harrington was lost with all hands off Perdido Key that October — its wreck, found a week before the vote, showing signs of attack — Landon's restraint became politically fatal. Cornelius Roosevelt II, campaigning on bringing the "Matanzas Bay clique" to heel, won 366 electoral votes to Landon's 238. In his Georgetown Address of December 21, delivered at the site of the old federal capital, the president-elect gave Spain until April 1 to surrender the Harrington's attackers — or face war.

Outbreak (1905)

Three days before the deadline, Spain's ambassador announced that the court of Francisco VIII rejected the ultimatum. On April 2, 1905, Roosevelt addressed a joint session of Congress, asking it "to authorize, without delay, the use of force against Spanish authorities in the Caribbean to compel the surrender of the Matanzas Bay Clique." Congress complied, and the Navy — prepared in advance — announced the blockade of Cuba on April 10, sweeping the token Spanish squadrons from the waters around both islands within the month.

The Cuban Campaign

The first landings struck Matanzas Bay itself on May 1, 1905. Against ferocious resistance from Compact militia stiffened by Spanish regulars, American forces took Matanzas by May 6, Port Hawthorne on May 19, and New Charleston on May 30, capturing part of the Compact leadership and scattering the rest into the hills. After a summer's interruption on the mainland, the Havana Landings of August 29 — preceded by forty-eight hours of naval bombardment — opened the war's largest battle; the colonial capital fell on October 29 after two months of fighting. Through the winter, hampered by yellow fever, the army secured western Cuba while Spanish forces withdrew to make their stand at Camagüey.

The Coastal Rebellion

On May 27, 1905, "Old South" diehards rose in Virginia, the Carolinas, Georgia, and western Florida, proclaiming a "Restored American Republic" pledged to the pre-1861 Constitution. The rebels were crushed within ten days in Virginia and North Carolina, but held varying degrees of control over a corridor from Charleston to Tallahassee through much of the summer, forcing the diversion of forces from the Caribbean. The rebellion enjoyed no support from any state government — and its investigation by the newly minted Federal Investigative Service uncovered links to members of the Republican Party that would destroy that party within the decade, clearing the ground for the rise of the Jeffersonian Constitutional Alliance.

Puerto Rico and Victory (1906)

The war's sharpest reverse came at sea: on March 7, 1906, a Spanish fleet surprised the weakened blockade at San Juan, sinking four American ships and reinforcing the garrison. The setback proved temporary. By April 20 San Juan was encircled, and on May 2 the governor of Puerto Rico surrendered; the island's rebels proclaimed the Republic of Puerto Rico the next day, with immediate American recognition and a pledge of protection. In Cuba, American diplomats had spent the spring organizing local leaders, and the United Provinces of Cuba was proclaimed on May 7. The final campaign closed on Camagüey from north and south; American forces entered the city on June 7, the governor of Cuba surrendered on June 9, and the last Compact holdouts were run down by June 16. Days later, Roosevelt informed Congress — in an address filmed for the nation's new cinemagraphic halls — that "Spanish authority in Cuba and Puerto Rico has ceased."

Aftermath and Legacy

The war produced a surge of national feeling unmatched since the War Between the States — flags on every house, patriotic anthems at every gathering, and the propaganda flowering that gave the republic both the "Firm, United" poster series and Goddess Columbia as its symbolic embodiment. The anthem debate it ignited ended with the designation of "Hail, Columbia!" as the national anthem. Strategically, the war extinguished the last armed remnant of the old slave power, created two independent Caribbean republics under American protection, and set the stage for the hemispheric order of the Treaty of Managua era. Politically, it made Cornelius Roosevelt II, destroyed the Republican Party, and demonstrated — for the first time since 1861 — that the reunited republic could act, in Roosevelt's words, "as one people, victorious abroad and renewed at home."
