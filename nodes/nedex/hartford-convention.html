Hartford Convention
Hartford, Connecticut · December 1814 – January 1815

The Hartford Convention was an assembly of delegates from the New England states, held at the Connecticut State House in Hartford from December 15, 1814 to January 5, 1815. Called at the invitation of the Massachusetts General Court, it met to consider the region's grievances against the Madison administration, the conduct of the War of 1812, and the constitutional arrangements that New England Federalists held responsible for both.

Its proceedings were conducted in secret and concluded with the adoption of the Hartford Resolution, a programme of proposed constitutional amendments. Secession was raised and debated at length but was not endorsed. The terms of the peace, which became known in America within weeks of the Convention's close, were widely read as confirmation of its warnings, and the demands set out at Hartford remained a central object of Federalist politics until they were largely satisfied by the Grand Compromise of 1817.

Background

New England had opposed the war from its declaration. The commercial states of the northeast had borne the heaviest cost of the embargo policies that preceded the conflict, and the war itself closed what remained of their carrying trade while offering the region nothing it wanted. Federalist legislatures had refused to place their militias under federal command, and by 1814 several were withholding cooperation from federal recruitment entirely.

The destruction of Washington City in August of 1814 marked the point at which regional discontent became organised action. Elsewhere in the republic the burning of the capital produced a surge of defiance; in New England it was received as the final demonstration of an administration that had begun a war it could not fight and could not conclude. Through the autumn, with the government dispersed and the Treasury unable to meet its obligations, the Massachusetts General Court resolved in October to invite the other New England states to a conference on the common defence and on the amendment of the Constitution.

The delegates

Twenty-six delegates attended. Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Rhode Island sent full delegations chosen by their legislatures. New Hampshire and Vermont, whose legislatures declined to act, were represented by delegates chosen in county conventions — a distinction their opponents afterwards used to question the assembly's standing. George Cabot of Massachusetts was elected president of the Convention and Theodore Dwight its secretary.

The composition of the delegations was itself a decision. The Massachusetts appointments in particular passed over the most vehement voices in the state in favour of men of established moderation, a choice made deliberately by those who wished the Convention to produce demands rather than a rupture.

Proceedings and the question of secession

The Convention sat for three weeks behind closed doors, and no record of its debates was published. The secrecy was intended to permit frank discussion of positions no delegate wished to be held to publicly; its effect was to leave the country free to imagine the worst, and the charge that Hartford had been a treasonous conclave was made freely for years afterwards on no evidence at all.

The substantive division lay between a minority who argued that the New England states should treat separately with Britain, or empower a second convention to reconsider their membership of the Union altogether, and a majority who held that the region's grievances were constitutional and should be pressed as such. Cabot's argument for the majority position was reported by several delegates in later years: that concessions the rest of the republic might in time be brought to grant were worth more than a separation it would certainly fight to prevent. The Convention resolved to proceed by way of proposed amendment, and adopted the Hartford Resolution on January 5, 1815.

Reception

A committee of delegates carried the Resolution to Philadelphia in February of 1815, where the federal government had established itself after the loss of the capital. President James Madison declined to receive them. Within the same weeks the terms agreed at Ghent became generally known — the cession of northern Maine, of portions of New York, and of the whole of Michigan — and the political effect on the Convention's standing was immediate.

The delegates had come to Philadelphia expecting to be treated as an embarrassment. Instead the peace furnished proof of precisely what they had spent three years asserting: that the war had been entered without deliberation, prosecuted without competence, and concluded at a price the country had never been asked to accept. The Federalist Party, which had appeared in decline before the war, returned strengthened in New England and improved its position elsewhere, and the Convention's demands passed directly into its congressional programme.

Legacy

The Hartford Convention is conventionally treated as the beginning of the constitutional settlement of 1817 rather than as an episode of the war. Two of its central demands — the single presidential term with its bar on successive presidents from one state, and the supermajority requirement for declarations of war and the admission of new states — were written into the Constitution of 1787 as the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments during the Crawford Conference negotiations under President William Crawford. Its demand for the repeal of the three-fifths clause was refused, and the refusal became a standing Federalist grievance for the next four decades.

The Convention also established the precedent of a regional assembly meeting to state constitutional terms to the federal government. It was invoked repeatedly in the sectional crises that followed, and most consequentially at the Boston Conference and the Montpelier Conference of 1857, whose participants claimed the Hartford delegates as their direct predecessors — a comparison the events that followed made difficult to sustain.
