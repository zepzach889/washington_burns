The Kingdom of Spain (Spanish: Reino de España) is a constitutional monarchy occupying the Iberian Peninsula in southwestern Europe, bordered to the northeast by the Union of European Republics and to the south by the Strait of Gibraltar. One of the oldest monarchies in the world, Spain entered the twentieth century as a diminished imperial power and emerged from the Global War (1932–1939) as a nation confronting the total dissolution of its overseas empire and a deeply uncertain political future.

Spain fought as an ally of Prussia in the Global War, declaring the Mediterranean a closed sea in 1933 — a provocation that drew the United States into the conflict in early 1934 and activated anger still raw from the Matanzas War of 1905–1906, in which living veterans of that earlier conflict remembered Spain's role. A separate armistice in 1938 ended Spanish participation before the European settlement was complete, on terms that formalized the loss of all remaining colonial possessions. The subsequent political future of the monarchy, and of Spain itself, remains an open question in the post-armistice years.

The Restoration and the Long Reign of Isabella II

Following the death of King Ferdinand VII in 1837 without a male heir — the king's brother, Don Carlos, having died in a carriage accident earlier that decade — the crown passed to Ferdinand's infant daughter, Isabella. Her mother, Queen Maria Christina, served as regent until Isabella came of age in 1848.

Isabella II ruled Spain for over four decades, presiding over a period of cautious but genuine liberalisation. Press freedoms expanded, the franchise broadened, and the Cortes Generales assumed increasing authority over national affairs. The Catholic Church retained considerable social influence, and regionalist movements in Catalonia and the Basque Country remained persistent undercurrents. Isabella's reign established the constitutional framework that would define Spanish politics through the following century, though its limitations — endemic corruption, elite-dominated parties, and deferred reform — planted the seeds of later instability.

Ferdinand VIII and the Matanzas War (1905–1906)

Isabella II died around 1890 and was succeeded by her son, Ferdinand VIII. His reign inherited a functioning but hollow constitutional order, in which the Liberal and Conservative parties rotated power without meaningfully addressing Spain's growing social and economic pressures. The Philippines and scattered Atlantic possessions remained under Spanish rule, relics of a once-vast empire.

The defining catastrophe of Ferdinand VIII's reign was the Matanzas War (1905–1906; also called the Caribbean War). Spain's long tolerance of the Matanzas Bay Clique — a community of former American slaveholders who had established themselves in western Cuba following their defeat in the War Between the States — provoked an ultimatum from President Cornelius Roosevelt II. When Ferdinand VIII's government refused to extradite the exile leadership, the United States declared war in April 1905.

The war was swift and decisive. American forces blockaded Cuba and Puerto Rico, linked up with local independence movements, and overwhelmed Spanish colonial garrisons. Despite a brief Spanish naval counteroffensive at San Juan in early 1906 — in which Spain's fleet temporarily regained control of the harbor before being swept aside in the Second Battle of San Juan — the outcome was never seriously in doubt. Crown Prince Alejandro represented his ailing father at the Treaty of Copenhagen (1906), where Spain formally relinquished all claims to Cuba and Puerto Rico and agreed to pay substantial war indemnities. Ferdinand VIII died in 1907, less than a year after the treaty was signed.

The Reign of Alejandro I

Crown Prince Alejandro ascended to the throne in 1907 as Alejandro I, inheriting a country humiliated, financially strained, and politically fractured. The Caribbean War had discredited the old Liberal-Conservative rotation, energised the republican movement, and emboldened Catalan and Basque regionalists who saw central weakness as an opportunity.

The Montilla Years (1907–1919)

The early years of Alejandro I's reign were stabilised by Prime Minister Rodrigo Montilla y Vega, a veteran Liberal who had spent the war years in opposition and was thus untainted by Copenhagen. Montilla governed from roughly 1907 to 1919, pursuing cautious modernisation — modest military reform, limited autonomy concessions to regionalists, and incremental economic development.

His tenure was overshadowed, however, by the Philippine War (1907–1915), in which a major nationalist uprising following the Caribbean War drew Spain into a costly and ultimately unwinnable colonial conflict. Unable politically to accept a third humiliation and unwilling to bear the expense indefinitely, Montilla's government eventually negotiated Philippine independence — adding the archipelago to the growing list of losses the Nationalist movement would lay at liberalism's door.

The Rise of the Nationalist Movement

The formation of the Union of European Republics in 1904 — a communist state born from revolution in France and the South German states, sitting directly across the Pyrenees — profoundly alarmed the Spanish political class. Under the radical leadership of Holtz from 1916 onward, the UER suppressed religion, dissolved national identities, and purged dissidents. For Catholic, monarchist Spain, the UER was not merely an ideological rival but an existential threat on its doorstep.

Through the 1920s, a new Nationalist movement absorbed and eventually displaced the old Conservative establishment, drawing on military officers embittered by the Caribbean War, on the Catholic hierarchy's fears of the UER, and on a younger generation that regarded Montilla-era liberalism as fatally weak. By the early 1930s, civilian Nationalist leader Ramón Calvo y Serrano had consolidated a parliamentary majority and formed government in genuine partnership with Alejandro I.

The Global War and the Armistice of 1938

Spain's alignment with Prussia in the early 1930s reflected a shared conviction that the UER represented a civilisational threat requiring a strong monarchist-nationalist counterweight. When Prussia invaded formerly German UER territories in 1932 and the European war began, Spain moved steadily into the Prussian column.

In 1933, Spain declared the Mediterranean a closed sea and began attacking Allied-bound shipping, including American vessels — actions that sank several ships with significant casualties. The declaration and attacks triggered a congressional declaration of war in the United States in early 1934. The political resonance in the NAU was acute: the Matanzas War was within living memory, its veterans still alive, its political scars still fresh. "Spain again" was not merely a political slogan but a genuine emotional catalyst that unified public opinion behind the war declaration.

Spain was originally scheduled to host the 1938 World Olympic Games in Madrid — a sign of the international standing the Nationalist government had cultivated in the early 1930s. The games were cancelled as the war consumed Europe.

Spain fought as a Prussian ally through the middle years of the Global War, but the military situation deteriorated sharply after 1937. The Prussian democratic revolution, the collapse of the Nationalist parliamentary coalition in Berlin, and the proclamation of the United German Republic in early 1938 left Spain and Austria-Hungary exposed. With Allied forces making gains in the Mediterranean — Italy having entered the war against Spain in 1935–36 following Spanish violations of Italian sovereignty — Calvo y Serrano's government negotiated a separate armistice in mid-1938. The terms were punishing: Spain renounced all remaining colonial pretensions, paid reparations to the families of Allied sailors killed by Spanish action, and accepted GPC membership as a regular — not founding — member.

Ramón Calvo y Serrano resigned as Prime Minister shortly after the armistice was signed. The formal European armistice followed in autumn 1938, with the Treaty of Geneva signed in spring 1939.

The Post-Armistice Period

Spain emerged from the Global War in a condition of profound institutional uncertainty. The Nationalist government had led the country into a catastrophic alignment that cost it its last overseas possessions, triggered a second war with the United States within a generation, and ended with occupation by Allied forces in the Mediterranean theater. The monarchy's association with the Nationalist program — Alejandro I's genuine partnership with Calvo y Serrano — implicated the crown in the war's disastrous outcome in ways that the post-armistice political settlement would be forced to reckon with.

The republican movement, which had never governed but had persisted as a significant political tendency throughout the constitutional era, found new energy in the post-armistice environment. Catalan and Basque regionalists similarly pressed their long-standing claims in the context of a weakened central authority. The fate of the monarchy, the character of the postwar constitutional settlement, and Spain's path toward whatever political order the post-armistice years would produce remained, in the years immediately following 1938, genuinely open questions.

Spain joined the Global Peace Council as a regular member under the armistice terms, participating in the postwar international framework from a position of diminished standing and under sustained international scrutiny of its domestic political evolution.

Government and Politics

Spain is a constitutional monarchy. Legislative authority rests with the bicameral Cortes Generales. The monarch serves as head of state; executive authority is exercised by the Prime Minister and cabinet, who must maintain the confidence of the Cortes.

The principal political tendencies of the twentieth century have been the Liberal Monarchists, the Conservative Monarchists (largely absorbed by the Nationalists by the 1930s), the Nationalist movement, the Republican movement, and the Regionalist blocs of Catalonia and the Basque Country. The Republican movement, while a persistent force, has never held national governing power. The post-armistice political landscape, in which the Nationalist movement is discredited and the monarchy's future is uncertain, represents the most significant challenge to Spain's constitutional order since the Restoration.
