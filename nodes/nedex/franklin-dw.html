Franklin is the capital city of the United States of America, located in the District of Washington on both banks of the Ohio River. It serves as the seat of all three branches of the federal government and is home to Washington House, Congress Hall, and the Supreme Court of the United States. Franklin is the most politically significant city in the republic and one of its most recognisable — a planned capital built from the ground up on the banks of the Ohio River, it has been the centre of American civic life since the federal government relocated there from Philadelphia in 1827.

The city takes its name from Benjamin Franklin, the elder statesman and founding father, and was built on the site of the former city of Cincinnati, Ohio. The District of Washington encompasses land on both the north bank (formerly Ohio) and the south bank (formerly Kentucky) of the Ohio River, making it one of the few political jurisdictions in the United States that straddles a major river.

History

Selection of the Site (1817)

The decision to build a new federal capital was one of the central outcomes of the Grand Compromise of 1817, reached under President William Crawford in the aftermath of the War of 1812. The burning of Washington City by British forces had left the original capital a smouldering ruin, and the federal government had been operating from Philadelphia since 1814. The question of where to permanently relocate became one of the most contentious of the Crawford administration.

Throughout the summer of 1817, government surveyors travelled the Ohio River looking for a suitable location. The Joint Committee for the Selection of a New Capital ultimately narrowed the choice to two sites: Louisville, Kentucky, and Cincinnati, Ohio. Both states viewed the selection as a mixed blessing — the prestige of hosting the national capital came with the loss of the chosen city from the state's jurisdiction, as the plan called for a federal district on the model of the old District of Columbia.

Henry Clay, then Speaker of the House and a Kentucky native, used his considerable influence to keep Louisville within Kentucky's borders. On October 12, 1817, the Joint Committee announced its selection of Cincinnati as the site of the new capital. The city would be renamed Franklin in honour of Benjamin Franklin, and a new federal district — the District of Washington — would be carved out of both Ohio and Kentucky, encompassing land on both sides of the river. The existing District of Columbia was dissolved, and its land returned to Maryland and Virginia.

Planning and Construction (1817–1827)

In the spring of 1818, the Congressional Committee for the District of Washington held a design competition for the new city and its federal buildings. The winning plan was an amalgamation of proposals from Josiah Kemp of New York and Phillip Ogden of South Carolina, unveiled on March 15, 1818, as the "Plan for the City of Franklin."

The plan called for a city built on both banks of the Ohio River. On the north bank — the legislative bank — the new capitol building would be constructed. On the south bank — the executive bank — the presidential mansion and executive departments would be built. A grand central avenue, running north-south from Congress to the presidency, would connect the two sides. This avenue — today known as Union Avenue — became the spine of the new capital. The planners acknowledged that no engineer had yet successfully bridged the Ohio River, but expressed confidence that the problem would be solved in time. It would not be until 1877, nearly fifty years later, that the George Washington Bridge finally linked the two banks of Union Avenue.

President Crawford broke ground in May 1818, personally clearing the first section of what would become Union Avenue. Construction proceeded throughout the 1820s under the active oversight of Henry Clay, who as president from 1821 made the completion of Franklin a personal priority. It was Clay's idea to name the executive buildings after their first officeholders — Washington House for the presidential mansion, the Jefferson Building for the Department of the Exterior, the Hamilton Building for the Treasury, and the Knox Building for the Department of War.

When Alexander Hamilton, Jr. took the oath of office on the steps of the nearly completed Congress Hall on March 4, 1827 — the first inauguration in the new capital — much of the city was still under construction. Roads had been laid out, private lots sold, and some buildings finished, but most of the city remained a worksite. Union Avenue was cleared on both sides of the river, but paving existed only near Congress Hall and Washington House, and the promised bridge across the Ohio was still decades away.

Early Growth (1827–1860)

Through the 1830s, 1840s, and 1850s, Franklin grew steadily as the federal government expanded and the surrounding region developed. The University of the United States, chartered by Congress in 1825 and opened in 1828, established itself northeast of Congress Hall and became one of the country's premier institutions of higher learning. Its campus anchors what came to be known as the National Arts and Sciences District — a concentration of museums, research institutions, and the National Theater that developed in the blocks surrounding the university.

The city's growth was uneven in its early decades, with the legislative bank developing faster than the executive bank due to the concentration of congressional business and the surrounding commercial activity it generated. The executive bank remained more residential and administrative in character, centred on Washington House and the cluster of departmental buildings along Union Avenue.

The War Between the States (1857–1860)

Franklin's most dramatic moment came during the War Between the States. When the conflict began in 1857, the city found itself in the unusual position of being the capital of the slaver Union government under James Hawthorne, while also being the symbolic heart of the republic that the Free States were fighting to reclaim.

The assault on Franklin began on March 11, 1859, when General Alexander Bloom of Indiana led the Free Army of the West against the recently built Fort Lee, near the village of Reading on the boundary between Ohio and the District of Washington. After two days of fighting, Union forces retreated into Franklin proper, with the Free Staters in pursuit; by March 14, Union forces had largely withdrawn to the south side of the Ohio, leaving the legislative bank in Bloom's hands while the Union's Second Army retained Washington House and the executive bank. With the bridges cut, the Free Army of the West paused its advance while army engineers prepared a crossing. Bloom then sent a force east to cross the river secretly, attacking the Union's southern position on March 22. After a day of fighting, the Union Army retreated southward toward Frankfort, Kentucky, and Franklin fell entirely to the Free States.

Acting President Hugo Brandt, exercising characteristic caution, declined to immediately move the Free State government to Franklin — he wanted Kentucky more fully secured before committing. The Free State capital remained in Philadelphia until the war's end. Franklin sat under military occupation through the remainder of the conflict, suffering relatively minor physical damage — the main federal buildings came through largely intact — before Brandt's government formally returned in 1863 in preparation for the new constitutional order.

Reconstruction and Expansion (1863–1910)

The return of the elected federal government to Franklin under President Hugo Brandt in 1863 opened a new era of growth for the capital. The city expanded rapidly in the post-war decades as the federal government grew and the surrounding region industrialised. Franklin Crossroads Station, dedicated in 1875 during the Littlefield Administration, became the first unified rail depot for both national and private lines serving the capital — a symbol of the city's growing importance as a transportation hub.

The George Washington Bridge, connecting the two banks of Union Avenue, finally opened in 1877 after decades of engineering effort. Its completion transformed the city, allowing for the first time the seamless movement of people, goods, and traffic between the legislative and executive banks. The bridge quickly became one of Franklin's most recognised landmarks.

The Museum of the United States, opened by President Littlefield on February 22, 1879, established Franklin as the custodian of the republic's historical memory. Its core exhibits included original copies of the Declaration of Independence and both constitutions, works of patriotic art by John Trumbull, and artifacts from the founding era and the War Between the States. The museum was built opposite the University of the United States, just east of Congress Hall, and worked closely with the UUS to promote historical research.

By the early twentieth century, architect Samuel L. Bingham — already well known for his redesign of the unified city of Calumet — had relocated to Franklin in 1904 and begun a broader vision for the capital's civic spaces. Bingham envisioned a Federal Green — a swath of parkland running east-west from the Supreme Court Building to Union Avenue — as a counteraxis to the city's north-south spine. The Federal Green gained official support during the Roosevelt Administration (1905–1911) and was planned alongside a proposed Brandt Memorial at its western anchor, conceived to commemorate the wartime president as the fiftieth anniversary of the war's end approached.

Bingham met with the Daughters of the Republic in 1906 and agreed to incorporate their long-sought monument to Brandt into his plans. President Roosevelt secured matching congressional funds to supplement money the DoR had already raised, and ground was broken on the Brandt Memorial in 1907. The completed structure — a marble rotunda encircled by forty Corinthian columns, thirty-nine inscribed with the names of the states then in the Union and the fortieth left blank to represent the Republic itself, crowned by a glass-oculus dome that illuminated a statue of Brandt with one hand resting on the 1861 Constitution, his head and shoulders turned to align with the window facing Congress Hall — was formally dedicated on Victory Day, October 17, 1910.

Geography and Layout

Franklin is built on both banks of the Ohio River, with the District of Washington forming a roughly square federal territory carved from both Ohio and Kentucky. The city is organised around Union Avenue, the grand central thoroughfare that runs north-south from Congress Hall on the legislative bank to Washington House on the executive bank.

The Legislative Bank (North Bank)

The north bank of the Ohio contains the principal organs of the legislative branch and the federal judiciary:

  • Congress Hall — seat of the House of Representatives and Senate; twin-domed neoclassical structure with a central dome over the connecting rotunda; first used for the inauguration of Alexander Hamilton, Jr. in 1827
  • Supreme Court Building — seat of the federal judiciary
  • University of the United States — premier federal institution of higher learning, chartered 1825, opened 1828
  • Museum of the United States — national history museum, opened 1879
  • National Arts and Sciences District — cultural and academic precinct surrounding the UUS campus, home to numerous museums and the National Theater
  • Franklin Crossroads Station — unified rail terminus, dedicated 1875

The Executive Bank (South Bank)

The south bank contains the executive branch and its supporting departments:

  • Washington House — official residence and workplace of the president; three-story neoclassical structure fronting Union Avenue
  • Jefferson Building — headquarters of the Department of the Exterior
  • Hamilton Building — headquarters of the Department of the Treasury
  • Knox Building — headquarters of the Department of War

Union Avenue and the George Washington Bridge

Union Avenue is the symbolic and physical spine of the capital, running from Congress Hall on the north bank to Washington House on the south. The avenue's most prominent feature is the George Washington Bridge, opened in 1877, which connects the two banks at the avenue's midpoint. The bridge's opening after nearly fifty years of the two banks being connected only by ferry transformed daily life in the capital and is regarded as one of the great engineering achievements of the nineteenth century.

Government and Representation

Franklin is governed as a federal district, not a state. The District of Washington is administered by a federally appointed governor, though residents elect a local council for municipal matters.

For much of its history, residents of Franklin had no representation in Congress and no vote in presidential elections — a source of persistent political controversy, particularly as the city's population grew. This was addressed by the First Amendment to the Constitution of 1861, which passed both houses of Congress on July 1, 1890, under President Leeland Rowling and was fully ratified by the requisite thirty of thirty-nine states by 1892, extending congressional representation and presidential voting rights to the residents of both the District of Washington and the District of Manhattan in time for the 1892 election.
