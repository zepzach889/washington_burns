Sixteenth Amendment to the Constitution (1833)
Electoral college plurality amendment
Contents
  1. Background
  2. Provisions
  3. Ratification
  4. Legacy
  5. See also

The Sixteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States, passed by Congress on April 18, 1833, and ratified in early 1834, abolished the requirement that a presidential candidate secure a majority of the electoral college. Under the amendment, the candidate receiving the most electoral votes was declared the winner outright, ending the practice of contingent elections in the House of Representatives. It was the final amendment adopted under the Constitution of 1787, and its perceived failures over the following decades became one of the grievances that fed the crisis of 1857.

Background

The amendment answered the structural dysfunction of three-party politics. With the Federalist, Democratic, and Republican parties each commanding a regional base, no candidate could reliably assemble an electoral-college majority, and for two consecutive elections the choice of the chief executive had fallen to the House of Representatives. The Election of 1826 ended in a near three-way tie; the House elevated Alexander Hamilton, Jr. over John C. Calhoun, who had won the most electoral votes — a result Republicans denounced for six years as the "Devil's Bargain." In 1832, Calhoun again led the field with 161 electoral votes, 43 short of a majority, and the House — under intense pressure to respect the electoral-college leader this time — elected him on the first ballot in February 1833.

By then, members of all three parties had concluded that the majority requirement was unworkable. President Calhoun, himself twice a victim and once a beneficiary of the contingent system, supported the change.

Provisions

The amendment's single operative provision dropped the constitutional requirement for an electoral-college majority, providing instead that the candidate with the greatest number of electoral votes be declared the winner of the presidential election. The contingent election in the House of Representatives was abolished.

Ratification

The amendment enjoyed wide support across all three parties, passed both houses of Congress on April 18, 1833, and received the approval of the requisite number of states by early 1834.

Legacy

The plurality rule faced its first great test in the Election of 1838, when Vice President Wilbur Haines defeated Senator Rutherford by a margin of 164 electoral votes to 163, with 82 for a third candidate — a one-vote victory that left much of the South embittered, though calls to challenge the result went unanswered. Over the following two decades, the rule's tendency to produce presidents without popular mandates became a standing grievance of national politics; the demand to repair the presidential election system was among the formal demands of the crisis of 1857 that preceded the War Between the States. The Constitution of 1861 resolved the question by abolishing the electoral college altogether in favour of direct popular election — the system that, as later refined by the runoff mechanism of 1911, remains in force today.
