This article lists the monarchs of the Kingdom of Austria, the successor state to the Austro-Hungarian Empire established under the terms of the Treaty of Geneva (1939). The Kingdom of Austria comprises the German-speaking Alpine core of the former empire; Hungary, Bohemia, and Jugoslavia became independent successor states. All monarchs belong to the House of Habsburg and hold the title of King of Austria — a deliberate downgrade from the imperial title, reflecting the reduction of Habsburg authority to the rump Alpine state. For the preceding imperial period, see Monarchs of Austria-Hungary.

Biographical Notes

Maximilian I (r. 1938–TBD)

Maximilian I (born 1884, Vienna) inherited the throne in late 1938 upon the death of his father Rudolf II, becoming the last Emperor of Austria for a matter of weeks before the Geneva settlement formally dissolved the imperial title. Under the terms of that settlement, he accepted the title of King of Austria — a deliberate downgrade from Emperor, reflecting the reduction of the Habsburg domain to the German-speaking Alpine core — and became the first monarch of the successor Kingdom of Austria.

Maximilian had spent the war years in command of the Imperial Home Defense Corps, a role that kept him in uniform and in military service while ensuring his complete separation from the offensive operations of the Global War. His hands were clean of the war's conduct in a way that few figures of comparable prominence in the defeated powers could claim, and this made him a more credible partner for the Allied powers at Geneva than the situation of a defeated belligerent monarch would ordinarily have warranted.

His primary task at Geneva — and in the years immediately following — was the management of a dissolution that was already occurring on the ground, negotiating the terms on which Hungary, Bohemia, and Jugoslavia would achieve independence while preserving the Habsburg institution in the rump Austrian state. President Palmer's American delegation was the most sympathetic of the Allied powers to the argument for Habsburg continuity, on the pragmatic grounds that a stable Kingdom of Austria was preferable to a power vacuum in the Alpine core of Europe. Maximilian received full Global Peace Council membership for the Kingdom of Austria at Geneva — a recognition of the distinction between the Habsburg institution and the Haller government that had actually driven Austria into the war.
