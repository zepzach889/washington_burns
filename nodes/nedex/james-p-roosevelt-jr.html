James Perry "JP" Roosevelt Jr. (1852-1921) was the twenty-fifth President of the United States, serving from January 1917 until his death in January 1921. A career officer in the United States Army, he spent more than three decades in uniform — on the frontier in Washingtonia, as military attaché in Canada, Britain, and France, and finally as Military Governor of Cuba following the Matanzas War, which earned him national fame as the "Hero of Havana." He was the elder brother of Cornelius Roosevelt II, the twenty-third President, making the Roosevelts the only siblings ever elected to the presidency.

Roosevelt resigned his commission in 1911 after aligning himself with the newly formed Jeffersonian Constitutional Alliance, whose politics stood well to the right of his brother's. Appointed to the Senate by the Virginia legislature in 1912, he became the party's standard-bearer four years later and won the 1916 election outright, becoming the first President of the United States inaugurated in January under the Fourth Amendment and the first Jeffersonian Constitutionalist to hold the office. His administration pursued a programme of federal retrenchment — reopening the firearms registry question, partially deregulating the United States Railway Company, withdrawing from the international peace councils, and cutting funding to the parks, broadcasting, and improvement agencies. He died of a heart attack in his sleep on January 3, 1921, less than two weeks before the end of his term. He remains the last sitting president to die in office.

Early life and military career

James Perry Roosevelt Jr. was born on April 11, 1852, in Manhattan, the first child of James Perry Roosevelt and his wife Cassandra. During the War Between the States the family kept largely to its estate on Long Island, away from the fighting that ravaged nearby New York City. In the post-war years the Roosevelts invested heavily in the rebuilding of Manhattan, but the eldest son showed little interest in the family's commercial affairs; he was drawn instead to sport, the outdoors and, as he approached adulthood, the military. He was accepted to West Point in 1870.

Service in the West

Roosevelt graduated from West Point in 1874 as a second lieutenant and was posted to Fort Niobara, in the territory of Washingtonia, where the garrison was charged with protecting traffic on the transcontinental railroad. The work involved patrolling for bandits and keeping the peace between white settlers and the Native nations still living in the region. It was the era of negotiations, in which the federal and territorial governments were working with tribal leaders to determine how they would coexist in the West.

The most persistent flashpoint in that corner of southeastern Washingtonia was the Black Hills. Sacred to the Lakota people, the majority of the range lay within Washingtonia, with a small sliver inside the territory of Lakota, and settler encroachment produced friction that Roosevelt and the Army were repeatedly called on to resolve. In 1879 the Department of the Interior placed the whole of the Black Hills under "Federal Reserve Protection," ordering all settlers evicted; the land was not turned over directly to the Lakota, but it was closed to settlement.

Roosevelt earned a reputation at Fort Niobara as a steady and trustworthy officer who dealt fairly with all parties, and a handful of sharp encounters handled with a cool head are generally credited with keeping the peace in a region that might easily have seen its first serious fighting in years. He was promoted to captain by 1880. In September 1881 he was tasked with escorting delegates to President Drake's Native Nations Conference in Franklin, where the administration hoped to settle the final questions of tribal settlement and jurisdiction and clear the path to statehood for Lakota, Washingtonia, and Clark; Roosevelt personally conducted the delegations of the Assiniboine and Arikara nations to the federal capital, making an impression on several senior officers that would serve his career well.

Following the conference he oversaw the voluntary relocation of the Assiniboine people from Washingtonia to Lakota between 1881 and 1883, and was promoted to major on its completion. In 1884 he was transferred to New Boston, Oregon, to help oversee cross-border traffic with the Dominion of Canada across the Columbia River and to assist in settler-Native negotiations in the state, where squatters had trespassed onto land in Kalapua County in the southwestern Willamette Valley. His work there earned him a further promotion and a posting to the General Staff in Franklin in 1889.

Foreign postings

Roosevelt received his first international assignment in 1891, spending two years as military attaché to the American legation in Canada, followed by two years in the same capacity in London beginning in 1894. In 1898 he was posted to the American Embassy in Paris, where he remained throughout the Great European War as both attaché and military observer — an experience that would later be cited by the General Staff as qualifying him to help direct the invasion of Cuba.

The Flight to La Rochelle

When the communalist revolt broke out across France in 1904, Roosevelt helped turn the American Embassy in Paris into both refuge and fortress. He organised the Marine guard and the veterans among the embassy staff to secure the compound and to bring in Americans stranded in the city, along with a number of locals. On learning that King Henri VI was preparing to abandon Paris for Corsica, Roosevelt and Ambassador William Gladston began planning an evacuation of their own — westward to ships waiting at La Rochelle on the Atlantic coast.

The first convoy departed on September 10, the day the king fled the capital, and the movement continued for a week. Roosevelt and Gladston were the last to leave, on September 18. They reached Manhattan on October 15, 1904, arriving to find the country on the eve of its own war — and in time for Roosevelt to watch his middle brother, Cornelius, elected the twenty-third President. For his conduct in what the press christened the "Flight to La Rochelle," Roosevelt was promoted to brigadier general in October 1904.

The Matanzas War and the Military Governorship of Cuba

When the United States declared war on Spain the following spring, the General Staff tapped Roosevelt to help oversee the invasion of Cuba, citing his firsthand observation of modern European fighting techniques. He was dispatched to Florida to assist with preparations and was present at the initial landings at Matanzas on May 1, 1905. With the outbreak of the Coastal Rebellion at home, the decision was made to keep the president's brother in Cuba to prepare for the Army's eventual return in force. That came in September, and on October 29, 1905, Havana fell to U.S. forces.

Havana became Roosevelt's headquarters for the remainder of the Matanzas War, and he was named Military Governor of Cuba. Over the winter he coordinated the pacification of the western half of the island and began working with local anti-Spanish leaders to organise a civil administration. The instruction from Franklin was unambiguous: this was not a war of conquest, and Cuba would emerge from it as its own nation. When the Spanish governor surrendered at Camagüey on June 7, 1906, Roosevelt was on hand to accept the capitulation.

The "Hero of Havana" remained in Cuba as Military Governor until 1910, helping local leaders establish the United Provinces of Cuba while rooting out remnant Spanish loyalists and Matanzas Clique diehards. The American military presence was formally ended on May 1, 1910, in a handover ceremony in Havana at which Roosevelt, by then a major general, and Vice President Patrick Hannah transferred control of the island to the Cuban government.

Break with the administration and the Jeffersonian turn

Roosevelt followed political developments at home closely during his governorship and found himself increasingly at odds with his brother; the surviving correspondence between the two is dense with policy argument. The elder Roosevelt was particularly outraged by the crackdown on gun ownership in the Coastal South, which he regarded as a dangerous precedent, and — a conservative Democrat who had more than once voted for Republican candidates — he considered the Federal Investigative Service's accusations against the Republican leadership questionable. In 1909 he formally aligned himself with the nascent Jeffersonian Constitutional Alliance and began a private correspondence with several of its leaders, urging them to keep up the fight for private liberties.

Months after his term as Military Governor ended, the new party attempted to draft him as its vice-presidential candidate for the 1910 election. He declined, citing his active commission and a desire to remain formally neutral. Historians have long speculated that a Paxton-Roosevelt ticket might have finished second in that year's general election; whether that would have positioned the JCA to win the contingent election that followed is harder to establish, but it would certainly have weakened Patrick Hannah's hand.

The refusal did not save his career. His new political reputation put his prospects in the Army on ice, and in the summer of 1911 he announced his resignation. He purchased a home in Arlington, Virginia, and took over some of the Roosevelt family holdings in the area — chiefly the popular hotel in nearby Georgetown, Maryland, built inside the shell of what had once been the President's House of old Washington City.

Senate career (1913-1917)

In 1912 the JCA-dominated Virginia legislature selected Roosevelt as the state's new senator, and he took his seat with the 63rd Congress in 1913. That Congress saw the Jeffersonian Constitutionalists gain seven Senate seats — still third place, but narrowly enough to unsettle both Democrats and Federalists. The 1915 midterms produced a similar jump and left the JCA the second-largest party in Congress, and speculation about the 1916 elections began almost immediately.

It was clear early that Roosevelt would be the party's preferred choice to contest Washington House. He publicly accepted the role in March 1916 as the local conventions were getting underway.

The Election of 1916

The JCA's national convention met in St. Louis in August 1916, where the former general won the nomination quickly and advocated for Alden Gunther, the Governor of Florida — whom he had met while working in Cuba — as his running mate. The Democrats nominated Vice President Benjamin Woodlark with Representative Lucas Grant of Pennsylvania; the Federalists ran Governor Horace Camden of New York with Josiah Willis of Iowa; and the Socialist Progressive Alliance renominated its chairman Oliver Lowden alongside Cameron Mercer of Manhattan.

Despite extensive preparations for what many expected would be the nation's first presidential run-off under the newly ratified Fourth Amendment, none proved necessary. Roosevelt took 367 electoral votes — one more than the majority required — to Camden's 182, Woodlark's 94, and Lowden's 88. It was one of only a handful of occasions since the Fourth Amendment's passage on which no runoff has been needed.

Presidency

Inauguration

Under the rules of the new amendment, Roosevelt took the oath of office on January 15, 1917, becoming the first President inaugurated in January. It was a cold morning in Franklin, with fresh snow from the previous day, but his supporters filled the square in front of Congress Hall and lined the route down Union Avenue to Washington House. Thanks to the rapid growth of radio over the preceding decade, hundreds of thousands of Americans heard the inaugural address live — carried by the public broadcaster NBS and the commercial American Broadcasting Network — and millions more heard it rebroadcast on local stations or watched it in newsreels in the new cinema halls over the following weeks. The president told his listeners:

The past decade has forced rapid change and modernization upon our republic, and we have borne it bravely. But now is the time for clear-eyed restraint. To take the new tools we've created, be patient, and find the best ways to continue into this modern world without steamrolling our traditions. Our federal government has bloated over the past two administrations, and it is time to place power back in local communities, back in the hands of ordinary people and local businesses.

He also made clear that the Federal Gun Registry, one of his largest campaign targets, would be his first priority, calling the level of restriction placed on American gun owners by the previous two administrations "cowardly and un-American" and promising to roll the policies back.

The Federal Firearms Regulatory Reform Bill

The Jeffersonian Constitutionalists swept the 1916 election and became the largest party in the 65th Congress, installing Representative Horatio Spencer of Missouri as Speaker and Representative Isaac Potter of Alabama as Secretary General. The question was how far they were willing to go on the registry, with at least moderate support available from Federalists and some Democrats.

On March 1, 1917, Kentucky Representative Timothy Eastman introduced a bill that would preserve the registry itself — arguing that the "well regulated militia" language of the Constitution required the government to keep track of firearm ownership — while abolishing the licensing system and the caps on private ownership. As with most attempts at compromise, it was attacked from both directions. The president and his closest allies held that the registry had to go, and perhaps the whole apparatus of the Federal Investigative Service with it, having campaigned on shrinking the federal government; Democrats and Socialist Progressives insisted on retaining some form of licence and cap, if only to ensure that owners were trained in firearm safety.

Eastman's proposal nonetheless gained the most traction. Enough Federalists supported a basic registry that abolishing it outright was a non-starter, and the resulting Federal Firearms Regulatory Reform Bill passed both houses in May 1917 with roughly two-thirds support. Roosevelt signed it into law on May 20, 1917. The registry remained; citizens were required to apply for a licence, issued free of charge, and to complete a firearm safety course at no cost. Funding for the licences and classes was drawn from the FIS's main budget, effectively shrinking the agency and curtailing several of its other programmes — precisely the "administrative bloat" the Jeffersonian Constitutionalists had campaigned against.

Railway reform and the Railway Competitive Regulation Act

The next target of the president's campaign against federal spending was more contentious: the United States Railway Company. No one in the JCA proposed dissolving it — the USRC was near-universally regarded as the lifeblood of the nation — but many conservatives wanted the government out of the rolling-stock business, leaving it as builder of rail while private industry built and operated the trains. Serious discussion began in Congress through the spring and summer of 1917, centring on a sell-off of the government's rolling stock.

Unlike the registry, the proposal enjoyed no broad cross-party support. The Federalists in particular defended their party's legacy as a builder of roads and rail and counted the USRC, alongside the Federal Bureau of Improvements, among its signature achievements. Senator Lance Hawkins of Iowa emerged as one of the fiercest critics, predicting sharply higher shipping rates and passenger fares should the JCA be permitted to proceed with what he called the gutting of a core national institution.

What ultimately reached the president's desk was the Railway Competitive Regulation Act, which opened most of the USRC network to private operators in both freight and passenger service, where previously such firms had been confined to small branch lines and modest regional runs. Private companies would pay to use the lines and operate under guidelines set by the newly created Federal Railway Commission, which assumed the regulatory role formerly held by the USRC on the principle that a single body could not act as both competitor and regulator. A modest reduction in USRC funding followed, intended to give private firms room to enter the national market. As Hawkins had predicted, the result was rising costs and, in places, poorer service — a talking point the JCA's rivals used to good effect in the 1918 and 1920 midterms.

Foreign relations and the Union of European Republics

The Roosevelt administration was markedly more withdrawn in foreign affairs than the two Democratic administrations preceding it. The president directed his Exterior Secretary — his old friend Ambassador Gladston — to reduce the American presence at international gatherings as a cost-saving measure. To the dismay of the internationalist community, the United States sent no official representatives to the International Peace Council meetings at Madrid in 1917, Copenhagen in 1919, or Edinburgh in 1921. The withdrawal formed part of a broader decline of the movement, which had never recovered from the failure of the 1915 proposal to make the IPC a permanent institution; by the mid-1920s the councils drew fewer and fewer official delegations.

At the same time Gladston, at the president's urging, used the Exterior Department to isolate the Union of European Republics. Roosevelt's antipathy to the UER dated to his years as attaché in Paris, and he refused any official relationship with the communalist state. The policy coincided with the rise of Mathias Holtz, whose increasingly radical form of communalism made him a bogeyman across much of the western world. The president and his party regularly sought to tie the excesses of the Holtz regime to the Socialist Progressives, deepening the enmity between the SPA and the JCA.

"The Long March" and the rise of American cinema

On September 19, 1917, Roosevelt hosted an unusual event at Washington House: the premiere of The Long March, a new film from New World Studios. Starring the up-and-coming actor Edmund Farr, it was among the first "long-form" pictures, running 103 minutes, and followed the soldier Gary Thornton from his enlistment in the Free State Army in Ohio in 1858 through to the Fall of Atlanta in 1860. Billed as one of the first patriotic epics of American cinema, it was given full ceremony at Washington House: members of the National Orchestra performed the score, and Farr, director Paul Haymacker, and New World Studios founder Quincy Palmer all attended. Like radio, cinematography was a fast-growing industry, with cinema halls opening across the country and film production taking root in St. Augustine, Florida.

The Third World Olympic Games

The Olympic Games returned in 1918, and despite the administration's official aloofness toward international events the American public followed the competition closely — thanks in no small part to the president's brother. Former President Cornelius Roosevelt II now chaired the United States Olympic Committee, and as he had in 1914 he organised the "America Games" as the official qualifiers for American athletes, holding them again in Philadelphia across April and May and drawing coverage from the print press, the growing radio press, and the cinema newsreels.

The Third World Olympic Games opened in Rome on September 5, 1918, with Italian Emperor Ferdinand I presiding over the ceremonies. Over three weeks of competition Great Britain finished on top with 46 medals to the United States' 42 and Russia's 25 — but for the first time the United States took the most golds, sixteen to Britain's fifteen. At the closing ceremonies the head of the World Olympic Committee made two major announcements: that from 1922 the Games would be divided into separate summer and winter editions, and that both 1922 Games would be held in the United States, the summer in Boston and the winter in Hugosville, capital of the state of Brandt. It was a considerable victory for Cornelius Roosevelt II, a supporter of the Olympic movement since his own time in Washington House. Despite the brothers' deep political differences, the current and former presidents largely agreed on what the newspapers called "Olympic Politics," and Cornelius was in regular contact with his brother's administration as planning got underway.

Retrenchment and the Department of the Interior

The midterm elections of 1918 returned a Congress almost unchanged: the JCA dropped just thirteen seats in the House and remained the largest party, ahead of the Federalists, Democrats, and Socialist Progressives. Some Federalists floated replacing Speaker Spencer, but enough of their own colleagues opposed the idea that it died before it began.

In the spring of 1919 the 66th Congress carried the president's programme of budget cuts further, reducing funding for the Federal Parks Service, the Federal Bureau of Improvements, and several other Department of the Interior offices. The administration supported the maintenance of existing parks and infrastructure but resisted nearly all proposals for expansion. Roosevelt additionally directed the Department of Agriculture and its Office of Forestry Management Services to examine placing more publicly held land under private commercial contract as a source of new revenue — a move that drew sustained objection from conservationists.

Broadcasting consolidation and the "Big Five"

While the reductions to the Parks Service and the Bureau of Improvements were modest, the cuts to the Federal Broadcasting Authority and the National Broadcasting Service were far deeper. The administration effectively ended federal funding for the expansion of broadcasting infrastructure, arguing that private companies should bear the cost of building new stations and extending service. Many smaller broadcasters failed or were absorbed by larger firms, and some communities temporarily lost local radio service altogether; the broader result was the consolidation of the industry under five regional networks.

The American Broadcasting Network, headquartered in Manhattan, became dominant across New England, New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, and into the Upper South. The Southern Broadcasting Company of Atlanta held the Coastal South, Alabama, and most of Kentucky and Tennessee. The Meridian Broadcasting Company, based in New Orleans, served Louisiana, Mississippi, Gigadohi, and much of the Mississippi valley. The Calumet-based Continental Broadcasting Company covered Indiana, Illinois, Minnesota, Iowa, Jefferson, Kanasaw, Platte, Mesopotamia, and Lakota. In the West, the Pacific Broadcasting Network operated from New Boston, Oregon, serving Oregon, Clark, and Washingtonia, and reaching parts of Alta California and Columbia. Known collectively as the "Big Five," these companies became the senior partners of the FBA in setting federal broadcasting policy — an arrangement the president and his allies in Congress were happy to encourage.

There had been discussion at Washington House of defunding the National Broadcasting Service entirely. Its leadership persuaded the president instead to preserve it as a non-partisan education and public information service. No new NBS stations were chartered during this period and some stand-alone stations closed, but the institution survived.

The recording industry

The expansion of radio coincided with the emergence of several record companies of lasting influence on American popular music. Crescent Records of New Orleans became the leading label for Blaze music, a blend of brass, ragtime, and Black and Creole musical traditions; although Blaze had been regionally popular since the 1870s and 1880s, records and radio carried it to a national audience. Macon Records, founded in Richmond and later relocated to Pittsburgh, grew out of the Macon Audiography Company, an early pioneer of sound-recording technology. Other significant firms included the Republic Audiograph Company of Philadelphia and Continental Records of Florida, which frequently partnered with the motion-picture studios developing in St. Augustine. Together these companies created a national market for recorded music and contributed to the emergence of a distinct commercial youth culture through the 1910s and 1920s.

The 1920 midterms and the divided 67th Congress

The 1920 elections proved a sterner test than 1918, and one the president's allies did not pass. Both Federalists and Democrats gained seats in the House, leaving the Federalists and the Jeffersonian Constitutionalists tied at 210 seats each — roughly 32 percent of the chamber apiece — with neither able to organise the House without the other. The Federalists offered to continue supporting Isaac Potter of Alabama as Secretary General in exchange for a Federalist in the Speaker's chair. The JCA reluctantly agreed, and George McKnight of Pennsylvania replaced Horatio Spencer.

The negotiations concluded in December, several weeks before the 67th Congress was due to convene in mid-January. The arrangement preserved Potter's cabinet position while acknowledging Federalist gains, and though the JCA had lost ground the administration still appeared likely to retain considerable influence over the new Congress. By the end of the year the principal questions surrounding the transfer of congressional leadership seemed settled.

Death

On the morning of January 3, 1921, staff at Washington House entered the president's bedroom and found James P. Roosevelt Jr. dead in his bed. He had suffered a heart attack in his sleep, less than two weeks before the end of his term. His wife Julia was in Manhattan visiting her sister; Vice President Gunther was on an extended New Year's holiday with his family in Florida. Gunther was woken shortly after eight that morning and informed of the president's death.

With both the Roosevelt family and the senior members of the administration scattered across the country, the immediate priority was an orderly transfer of power. A federal judge was summoned from Tallahassee to the private residence outside the city where Gunther was staying, and shortly after ten o'clock Eastern Time the Vice President took the oath of office as the twenty-sixth President of the United States. He then began arrangements to return to Franklin, where he would assume control of the government and join the Roosevelt family in preparing the state funeral. The congressional agreements reached in December remained in force, but it now fell to Gunther, rather than Roosevelt, to work with the closely divided 67th Congress. Roosevelt was buried in the family plot in Manhattan.

Legacy

Roosevelt was the first Jeffersonian Constitutionalist president and, in many respects, the party's entire public identity. His four years in office marked the high-water mark of the early JCA programme: the licensing compromise of 1917, which preserved the firearms registry while stripping out the ownership caps and shrinking the Federal Investigative Service; the partial opening of the national rail network to private operators under the Federal Railway Commission; the withdrawal of the United States from the international peace councils; and a broad retrenchment across the Interior Department, the parks service, and federal broadcasting that reshaped American radio into the five-network structure that would define it for decades. Several of these measures proved more durable than their critics expected, though the rail reforms in particular produced the rising fares and uneven service that fed Federalist gains through the early 1920s.

His death left the party without its central figure. Alden Gunther governed as a steward for the remaining two years and declined to seek a term of his own, and the JCA — which had never developed an identity independent of Roosevelt — entered the 1922 election without an obvious successor. Roosevelt's death was also the last occasion on which a sitting President of the United States has died in office.
