George Washington Bridge
Franklin, District of Washington · Opened 1873
Contents

The George Washington Bridge is the suspension bridge carrying Union Avenue across the Ohio River at Franklin, District of Washington, linking the capital's legislative and executive banks. Begun in 1866 and opened in 1873, it was the first permanent bridge across the Ohio at Franklin.

It remains the ceremonial crossing of the inaugural parade. Its towers bear relief sculptures of George Washington, for whom it is named.

Before the bridge

The capital was designed around an axis that crossed a river nobody knew how to bridge. The original planners sited Congress Hall on the north bank and the executive establishment on the south, connected them with a grand central avenue, and recorded their confidence that the engineering would follow. It did not follow for fifty-six years.

In the interval the capital made do. Ferries carried the ordinary traffic between the banks, supplemented by timber trestles and pontoon spans thrown across at low water and rebuilt after nearly every flood. None was intended to last, and none did. When the fighting reached Franklin in 1857 the crossings were cut, leaving the legislative bank in one army's hands and the executive bank in another's, and obliging the Free Army of the West to halt while its engineers improvised a crossing of their own.

That the two halves of the federal government could be severed from one another in an afternoon was not a lesson the reunified republic cared to learn twice.

Construction

Work began in 1866, under the administration of President Hugo Brandt, and continued for seven years. The bridge was formally opened in 1873.

It was the largest public works undertaking the District had attempted, and the first in which the reunified government built something in the capital that was neither a repair nor a replacement of what the war had destroyed.

Design

The bridge is a suspension structure of two masonry towers carrying the main cables, with the roadway hung from the span between them. The towers are pierced by paired pointed arches through which the roadway passes, and each bears on its avenue-facing elevation a relief sculpture of the first president.

The deck is aligned precisely on the Union Avenue axis. The effect, which was deliberate, is that a traveller crossing from either bank sees the avenue continue unbroken ahead — Congress Hall closing the view to the north, Washington House to the south — so that the river reads as an interruption of the axis rather than the axis as an accommodation to the river.

Ceremonial role

The bridge carries the inaugural parade from Congress Hall, where the oath is administered, to Presidents' Square and Washington House, where the new president delivers the inaugural address from the north balcony. The crossing is closed to other traffic for the occasion, and the walk across it is the portion of the ceremony most familiar to the public.

Legacy

The bridge completed a capital that had been functionally unfinished since its founding. It is also the oldest major structure in Franklin to have been built by the government that emerged from the War Between the States rather than the one that preceded it, a distinction its builders were conscious of and its dedication speeches laboured somewhat heavily.
