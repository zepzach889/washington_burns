Brandt Memorial
Federal Green, Franklin, District of Washington · Dedicated 1910

The Brandt Memorial is the monument to President Hugo Brandt standing at the western anchor of the Federal Green in Franklin, District of Washington, on the west side of Union Avenue.

Designed as part of Samuel L. Bingham's plan for the capital's civic spaces and built with subscription funds raised by the Daughters of the Republic alongside matching congressional appropriations secured by President Cornelius Roosevelt II, it was begun in 1907 and dedicated on Victory Day, October 17, 1910 — the fiftieth anniversary of the end of the War Between the States.

Origins

The Daughters of the Republic had campaigned for a monument to the wartime president for a generation without securing a site, a design, or sufficient money, and by the turn of the century the subscription had become something of a standing joke in the capital press.

What rescued it was an unrelated project. The architect Samuel L. Bingham, already known for his redesign of the unified city of Calumet, relocated to Franklin in 1904 and began developing a broader vision for the capital's civic spaces, centred on a Federal Green running east to west from the Supreme Court Building to Union Avenue as a counteraxis to the city's north–south spine. The scheme gained official backing under the Roosevelt administration, and it required an anchor at its western end.

Bingham met the Daughters of the Republic in 1906 and agreed to incorporate their monument into his plan. Each party got what it had lacked: the Daughters acquired a site at the most prominent intersection in the District, and Bingham acquired a terminal feature with a subscription fund already attached to it.

Funding and construction

President Roosevelt secured matching congressional funds to supplement the money the Daughters had raised, and ground was broken in 1907. The timing was set by the approaching fiftieth anniversary of the war's end, and the work was carried out against that deadline.

Design

The memorial is a marble rotunda encircled by forty Corinthian columns. Thirty-nine bear the names of the states then in the Union. The fortieth was left blank, to represent the Republic itself.

The rotunda is crowned by a dome with a glass oculus, positioned so that daylight falls on the statue beneath — a figure of Brandt with one hand resting on the Constitution of 1861, his head and shoulders turned to align with the window facing Congress Hall across the Green.

That last decision has attracted more commentary than any other element of the design. Brandt does not face the visitor, and does not face the avenue; he faces the legislature, across the parkland, on the far bank of the axis he is standing beside. Bingham left no written explanation, and every generation of critics since has supplied its own.

Dedication

The memorial was formally dedicated on Victory Day, October 17, 1910, fifty years to the day after the surrender at Atlanta ended the war. The ceremony was among the largest public gatherings the capital had then seen, and was attended by surviving veterans of both the Union and the Free States armies.

The memorial today

The Brandt Memorial is among the most visited sites in the District and the customary setting for observances on Victory Day. Its position at the Union Avenue crossing of the Federal Green places it on the inaugural parade route, and it is passed by every incoming president between the oath and the address.

Since the 1960s it has faced the Global War Memorial across the avenue. Before that construction the Green lay open on the eastern side, and the view from the rotunda ran uninterrupted several blocks to the Supreme Court Building.
