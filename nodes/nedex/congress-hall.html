Congress Hall
Seat of the United States Congress · Franklin, District of Washington

Congress Hall is the seat of the legislature of the United States, located at the northern end of Union Avenue in Franklin, District of Washington. Home to the House of Representatives and the Senate, it is the republic's most recognisable public building and one of the defining landmarks of the capital city. Along with Washington House across the Ohio River, it anchors the two ends of Union Avenue and gives Franklin its symbolic heart.

Design and Architecture

Congress Hall was designed by architects Josiah Kemp of New York and Phillip Ogden of South Carolina as part of the Plan for the City of Franklin, unveiled on March 15, 1818, following a competition organised by the Congressional Committee for the District of Washington. The building occupies a commanding position on the north bank of the Ohio River, facing south down Union Avenue toward the river and, beyond it, Washington House.

The building's most distinctive feature is its twin copper-clad domes — one rising above the House of Representatives chamber in the west wing, and one above the Senate chamber in the east wing. Between them, the two wings are connected by a lower central block fronted by a grand colonnade of Corinthian columns supporting a classical pediment. A broad staircase descends from the central entrance toward Union Avenue. An American flag flies from a flagpole atop the central block. The overall effect — symmetrical, imposing, and unmistakably civic — was designed to project the permanence and authority of the republic's legislative branch.

The building is constructed primarily of pale limestone, giving it a gleaming appearance in direct sunlight that struck early visitors to the new capital. The interior houses the two legislative chambers, committee rooms, offices, and the large House Chamber which doubles as the venue for joint sessions of Congress.

Construction and Early History

Ground was broken on Congress Hall during the Crawford Administration, as part of the broader construction effort to establish Franklin as the republic's new capital following the Grand Compromise of 1817. The building was designed to be completed in time for the government's relocation to Franklin by 1827 — the deadline set by the New Capital Act of 1817.

Construction proceeded under the oversight of the Congressional Board for the District of Washington, with Phillip Ogden serving as chief planner after Josiah Kemp's reduced involvement in later years. President Henry Clay, deeply invested in meeting the 1827 deadline, made regular visits to the construction site throughout his term and was present at the groundbreakings of every major federal building under construction.

When Alexander Hamilton, Jr. took the oath of office on the steps of Congress Hall on March 4, 1827 — becoming the first president inaugurated in the new capital — the building's exterior was largely complete and its gleaming facade suitably impressive. The interiors, however, were still largely the domain of workmen; the crowds marvelling at the twin domes and the columned central block were looking at a building that would not be fully finished for some years yet.

Role in National Life

Inaugurations

Every president of the United States since Alexander Hamilton, Jr. has been inaugurated on the steps of Congress Hall, following the traditional sequence: the oath of office administered at the building, a parade down Union Avenue, across the George Washington Bridge to the south bank, and an address from the north balcony of Washington House. The broad southern staircase and the columned facade provide the ceremonial backdrop for one of the republic's most repeated rituals.

Joint Sessions

The House Chamber serves as the venue for joint sessions of Congress, at which the president may address the full legislature. Notable joint sessions have included Cornelius Roosevelt II's war address of April 2, 1905 — the first presidential address to Congress to be filmed — and the contingent election of February 6, 1911, when 650 representatives and 81 senators gathered to select the 24th president. The chamber's gallery fills with press and public on such occasions, and the building has been the scene of some of the republic's most consequential political moments.

Contingent Elections

Under the original Constitution of 1787 and later the Constitution of 1861, Congress Hall has been the site of four contingent elections — occasions when no presidential candidate achieved an electoral majority and the legislature was called upon to decide the outcome. Under the original constitution, the House of Representatives selected Alexander Hamilton, Jr. in 1827 and John C. Calhoun in 1833. Under the Second Amendment to the Constitution of 1861, joint sessions subsequently resolved the contingent elections of 1893 and 1911.

Surroundings

Congress Hall sits at the northern terminus of Union Avenue, the capital's central thoroughfare. To its northeast, the campus of the University of the United States extends through what is known as the National Arts and Sciences District — home to the Museum of the United States, numerous research institutions, and the National Theater. To the west, the Federal Green — the parkland planned by architect Samuel L. Bingham in the early twentieth century — runs westward from the nearby Supreme Court Building to Union Avenue, with the Brandt Memorial anchoring its far end.
