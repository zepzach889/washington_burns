First Capital Federal Park
Federal Park · Georgetown, Maryland · Site of the original U.S. Capitol and President's House

First Capital Park is a public historic site in Georgetown, Maryland, preserving the ruins of the original Capitol Building of the United States — the seat of government of the early republic before the War of 1812 and the burning of Washington City in August 1814. Opened to the public on August 24, 1889, on the 75th anniversary of the British burning of the city, the park was formally incorporated into the Federal Parks Service in 1914 and is today one of the republic's most visited historic landmarks — a place where Americans can stand among the physical remains of the nation's first capital and reflect on the moment that changed its history forever.

Background: Washington City and the Burning of 1814

When British forces entered Washington City on August 24, 1814, they burned the federal buildings with exceptional thoroughness. The Capitol Building — still under construction, with only its north and south wings complete — was gutted by fire, leaving only portions of its stone exterior walls standing. The President's House, the Treasury, and most other federal structures were similarly destroyed or severely damaged. The scale of the destruction made rebuilding in place impractical, and the subsequent Grand Compromise of 1817 relocated the capital westward to Franklin, District of Washington, on the Ohio River.

The ruins of Washington City were left largely in place as the federal government departed. The land reverted gradually to private and state ownership, with Maryland and Virginia reclaiming the former federal district's territory as stipulated by the New Capital Act.

The Claymore Era

In 1819, the Claymore family of Georgetown purchased a large tract that encompassed much of the former federal city's core, including the ruins of the Capitol Building and the shell of the President's House. They converted the stone shell of the former President's House into a plantation manor — known as the Claymore Capital Plantation — while preserving the Capitol ruins largely as they stood, with initial plans for a grand mansion on the site that never materialized. The Claymores became one of the most prominent landowning families in the Georgetown area and held the property for over six decades.

The family's fortunes declined significantly during the economic depression triggered by the Drake Administration's budget cuts in the early 1880s. In October 1883, the Claymore Capital Plantation announced it was folding, its owners financially ruined by their ties to railroad ventures badly damaged by Drake's cuts to the Federal Bureau of Improvements and United States Railway Commission.

Acquisition and Establishment of the Park

When news of the Claymore family's financial collapse became public, the Potomac Preservation Society — already known for its work preserving George Washington's Mount Vernon plantation nearby — stepped forward to try to secure the entire property for public trust. Funding proved insufficient to acquire both parcels. The more commercially valuable former presidential mansion was purchased in 1885 by the Roosevelt family of New York, who announced plans to convert it into a hotel. In a compromise arrangement, the Roosevelts agreed to sell the land containing the Capitol ruins to the PPS in 1886, separating the two historic sites — for the time being.

The capitol ruins, today.

The PPS immediately set about preparing the Capitol ruins for public access, working in partnership with the State of Maryland. The ruins were cleared of overgrowth, stabilized, and carefully landscaped. The surviving stone walls of the north and south wings — all that remained of the incomplete building — were preserved and made safe for visitors. In the place where the central rotunda would have stood — never built before the burning — the PPS paved a plaza with a large flagpole at its centre, flying the stars and stripes over the site where the republic's original legislature once intended to stand.

A modest museum was constructed on the north side of the property, housing artifacts from the Battle of Washington (1814), architectural drawings and models showing what the Capitol Building would have looked like had it been completed, and documents and objects from the early republic's Washington City years.

First Capital Park was formally dedicated and opened to the public on August 24, 1889 — the 75th anniversary of the burning of Washington City. Vice President Jefferson Young attended the dedication ceremony on behalf of President Leeland Rowling.

The Roosevelt Hotel and Its Return to the Park

The former Claymore plantation manor — built around the stone shell of the original President's House — opened as the Roosevelt Hotel in 1888, operated by the Roosevelt family of New York. Situated adjacent to the park, the hotel quickly became a popular destination for visitors to the historic site and for travellers passing through the Georgetown area. For nearly half a century, the juxtaposition of the grand hotel and the adjacent ruins created one of the more unusual streetscapes in the mid-Atlantic states — a working commercial establishment built into the bones of the republic's first executive residence, standing beside the carefully preserved wreckage of its first legislature.

The hotel closed permanently in 1937 amid the disruptions of the Global War, and the Federal Parks Service acquired the property the following year, completing a basic stabilisation and restoration of the primary building by 1941, along with the removal of the hotel expansion wings that had added extra rooms to the mansion, and folding it formally into the grounds of First Capital Park — reuniting, for the first time since 1886, the two halves of the original federal city under a single historic site.

The President's House today.

The mansion's most significant restoration came decades later, undertaken in the early 1960s under President Georgina Lincoln. This work was much more thorough than the 1940s restoration, with historians working with local preservationists to restore the mansion to the state it had been in the 1810s just prior to the British attack in the War of 1812.

Federalization (1914)

In 1914, on the centennial of the burning of Washington City, First Capital Park was formally dedicated as a unit of the Federal Parks Service, superseding the Potomac Preservation Society's direct administration of the site, though the PPS has continued to play an advisory and historical role in the decades since. The centennial dedication ceremony was attended by the Canadian Prime Minister and Crown Princess Charlotte — the future Charlotte III of the United Kingdom — marking the occasion as one of genuine international significance rather than a purely domestic commemoration.

Description and Visitor Experience

The park occupies a roughly rectangular plot centred on the Capitol ruins. The surviving walls of the north and south wings — roofless, stabilised, their window openings open to the sky — are the park's central feature, giving visitors an immediate and visceral sense of the building's scale. The stone is weathered but solid, its survival over decades of exposure and neglect a testament to the quality of construction that had been underway before the burning.

The central plaza, where the rotunda would have been, is a simple paved space with its large flagpole. The effect — a flag flying over an absence, marking a thing that was planned but never built — has been noted by many visitors as unexpectedly affecting.

The museum on the north edge of the property is small but well regarded. Its models of the planned Capitol have been particularly popular, allowing visitors to imagine the building as its designers intended — a grander, more complete structure than the ruins suggest. The restored mansion, formerly the Roosevelt Hotel, now houses additional exhibits on the early republic's executive branch and is open for guided tours.

Significance

First Capital Park occupies a unique position in the republic's historical consciousness. It is simultaneously a monument to destruction and a monument to consequence — the place where the burning happened, and the place that made Franklin necessary. For most Americans it is their most direct encounter with the physical evidence of the events of August 1814 that shaped the nation they live in.

President Cornelius Roosevelt II visited the site on December 21, 1904, to deliver the Georgetown Address — choosing the paved courtyard between the two ruined wings as the backdrop for his announcement that he would seek war with Spain over the Matanzas Bay exile community. The choice of location was deliberate: a president announcing the defence of the republic from the place where its first capital had been destroyed.
