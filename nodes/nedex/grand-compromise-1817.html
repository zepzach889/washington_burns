Grand Compromise of 1817
Crawford Conference · Philadelphia · Spring–Autumn 1817

The Grand Compromise of 1817 was a package of political agreements negotiated in Philadelphia during the spring and summer of 1817 that resolved several interconnected crises facing the young United States in the aftermath of the War of 1812. Brokered by President William Crawford at what came to be known as the Crawford Conference, the Compromise produced three amendments to the Constitution, secured ratification of the Wisconsin Treaty with Great Britain, and authorised the relocation of the federal capital to a new city on the Ohio River — the future Franklin, District of Washington.

The Grand Compromise is widely regarded as one of the most consequential political settlements in American history, reshaping the structure of the presidency, the relationship between the branches of government, and the physical and symbolic centre of the republic.

Background

When William Crawford took office in March of 1817, the United States was in a state of profound strain. The War of 1812 had left the country with territorial losses in Maine, New York, and the whole of the Michigan Territory, and the former capital of Washington City lay largely abandoned and in ruin following the British raid of 1814. The economy was weakened, sectional tensions were running high, and the Democratic-Republican Party — then the dominant force in national politics — was visibly fracturing along Southern and Western lines.

Crawford faced three immediate political crises that individually might have been manageable but together threatened to paralyse his administration. First, the death of his vice president, Daniel Tompkins, in a carriage accident in December 1816 had left the office vacant with no constitutional mechanism to fill it. Second, Federalist members of Congress — especially from New England — had campaigned on enacting elements of the Hartford Resolution, including limits on the presidential term and a higher threshold for declarations of war, and were conditioning their cooperation on progress toward those goals. Third, the outgoing Madison Administration had negotiated the Wisconsin Treaty with Great Britain, which ceded the Michigan and Wisconsin Territories to the British in exchange for the removal of trade restrictions on the Mississippi River. Many senators regarded the territorial concessions as deeply objectionable and were threatening to block ratification.

After months of congressional deadlock, Crawford invited leaders from across the political factions to the executive residence in Philadelphia in May of 1817 to negotiate a comprehensive settlement. The resulting agreements were announced publicly on May 31st.

Terms of the Compromise

The Grand Compromise consisted of four interlocking elements, each satisfying a different political constituency.

Thirteenth Amendment

Crawford's primary legislative priority was establishing a constitutional mechanism to fill a vacant vice presidency. The proposed Thirteenth Amendment created a formal procedure for replacing a vice president who had died or resigned, and also clarified the ambiguous succession language of the original Constitution, explicitly confirming that the vice president became president — not merely acting president — upon a presidential vacancy. The amendment passed Congress on September 17, 1817 and was ratified on November 19, 1817, when Pennsylvania became the fourteenth state to approve it. Crawford immediately nominated James Monroe as vice president; the Senate confirmed the appointment on November 24th.

Fourteenth Amendment

In exchange for Federalist support for the vice-presidential succession amendment and the Wisconsin Treaty, Crawford agreed to back two Federalist constitutional priorities. The first was the Fourteenth Amendment, which limited the presidency to a single term of six years and prohibited any person from serving as president from the same state as the immediately preceding president. Crawford pledged personally not to seek reelection in 1820 as a condition of the amendment's passage. It passed Congress on September 22, 1817 and was ratified on December 20, 1817.

Fifteenth Amendment

The second Federalist constitutional priority was the Fifteenth Amendment, which required a two-thirds majority vote of both houses of Congress to declare war, and applied the same threshold to the admission of new states to the Union. The amendment was more contentious than its companions and passed Congress by a narrow margin on October 1, 1817, receiving final ratification on September 12, 1818.

Wisconsin Treaty

In exchange for the constitutional concessions secured by the Federalists, opposition to the Wisconsin Treaty was withdrawn, allowing the Senate to ratify the agreement. The treaty formalised the cession of the Michigan and Wisconsin Territories to Great Britain, settled the northern boundary between the United States and Canada, and secured the removal of British restrictions on American navigation of the Mississippi River that had been embedded in the Treaty of Ghent.

Relocation of the Capital

The final element of the Compromise addressed the longstanding question of where the permanent federal capital should be located. With Washington City effectively abandoned and no consensus on whether to rebuild it or relocate the government elsewhere, the Crawford Conference delegates agreed to establish a new capital on the Ohio River — the Western wing of the Democratic-Republicans' preferred outcome. Throughout the summer of 1817, government surveyors examined potential sites along the river. The Joint Committee for the Selection of a New Capital ultimately narrowed the choice to two locations: Louisville, Kentucky, and Cincinnati, Ohio. Speaker of the House Henry Clay used his influence to preserve Louisville as part of Kentucky, and on October 12th the Committee announced its selection of Cincinnati as the site of the new federal district.

Legislation authorising the new capital passed the House on October 31st and the Senate on November 10th, and was signed into law by Crawford shortly thereafter. The bill dissolved the existing District of Columbia, returning its land to Maryland and Virginia, and established the District of Washington at Cincinnati. The town was to be renamed Franklin in honour of Benjamin Franklin. A Congressional board was created to oversee the city's funding, planning, and construction, with a ten-year deadline for completion. In the spring of 1818, design competitions were held; the winning plan, an amalgamation of proposals by Josiah Kemp of New York and Phillip Ogden of South Carolina, was unveiled on March 15, 1818. President Crawford broke ground on what would become Union Avenue in May of 1818.

Legacy

The Grand Compromise stabilised American political life at a moment of genuine crisis and set the country on a new constitutional and geographic course. The three amendments it produced — governing presidential succession, term limits, and the war powers threshold — remained in force until they were superseded or modified by the Constitution of 1861. The relocation of the capital to Franklin, District of Washington proved transformative for the development of the American interior, anchoring federal identity to the Ohio Valley and reinforcing the westward orientation of the republic for generations.

Crawford's personal pledge not to seek reelection, made as a condition of the Fourteenth Amendment's passage, became a source of controversy when he reversed course in 1820 and announced his candidacy — a decision widely criticised as a breach of faith and one that significantly damaged his historical reputation. The controversy also formalised the divide already building within the Democratic-Republican Party, with its Western faction organising as the new Democratic Party and its Southern faction organising as the Republican Party in time for the 1820 election.
