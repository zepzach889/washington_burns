Alden Gunther (1848-1925) was the twenty-sixth President of the United States, serving from January 1921 to February 1923. A Florida lawyer's son turned legislator, he sat in both chambers of the Florida legislature, served two terms as Governor of Florida, and was elected Vice President on the Jeffersonian Constitutional Alliance ticket in 1916. He succeeded to the presidency on the death of James P. Roosevelt Jr. on January 3, 1921, and was the last President of the United States born before the War Between the States.

Gunther governed explicitly as a caretaker. Within days of taking the oath he announced that he would not seek a term of his own, and his two years in office were defined less by an agenda than by inheritance: the enforcement fight over the Boiler Safety and Certification Act, a closely divided 67th Congress in which the Jeffersonian Constitutionalists no longer held the whip hand, and the two great set-pieces of 1922 — the Fourth World Olympic Games in Boston and the first presidential run-off held under the Fourth Amendment. He handed the office to Lance Hawkins on February 12, 1923, and died of pneumonia at his home outside Tallahassee two years later.

Early life and family

Alden Gunther was born on June 19, 1848, in Tallahassee, Florida. His father, Orville Gunther, served in the Union Army from 1857 to 1860 during the War Between the States, and after Florida was readmitted to the Union in 1869 was appointed to represent the state in the United States Senate.

The younger Gunther was sent north to the University of Virginia, graduating in 1872. He returned to Florida to take up the management of the family's business affairs, and after handing that responsibility to his younger brother, Brandon, joined his father in Franklin to help run the senator's office. The arrangement gave him nearly a decade of close observation of the federal legislature before he ever stood for office himself — an apprenticeship later remarked upon by biographers, who noted that Gunther entered national politics with an unusually low estimate of what the government in Franklin was capable of doing well.

Florida politics

State legislature

The elder Gunther retired from the Senate in 1884, and in the same year Alden ran successfully for the Florida State House. He remained in the lower chamber until 1900, when he was elected to the State Senate. His legislative career through these years was that of a conventional Southern Republican of the post-Reconstruction settlement: attentive to commercial and agricultural interests, sceptical of federal direction, and largely uninterested in national controversy.

The Coastal Rebellion and the Jeffersonian turn

The Coastal Rebellion changed that. Gunther was adamantly opposed to the anti-Federal rebels who rose in his state, and said so publicly at a point when a good many Floridian officeholders preferred to say nothing at all. In the aftermath, however, he judged the party he had belonged to all his life to be irretrievably compromised, and he became one of the first prominent Florida politicians to renounce his Republican membership. He then set about gathering like-minded states-rights conservatives from across Florida into the movement that would become the Jeffersonian Constitutional Alliance, and his organising work in the state is generally counted among the party's founding efforts in the Coastal South.

Governor of Florida (1910-1917)

Gunther ran for governor of Florida in 1909 and was returned for a second term in 1913. Both campaigns were fought principally against Franklin rather than against his opponents at home. He attacked the Democratic and Federalist parties for their support of the firearms registry established under the Federal Firearm Safety and Regulation Act, and as governor directed state resources toward challenging the law in the federal courts — an approach that made him one of the more visible governors in the country on an issue that was, by the middle of the decade, the defining question of Jeffersonian politics.

Vice Presidency (1917-1921)

It was this record on the registry that brought Gunther to national attention as the 1916 presidential race got underway, and his name was soon being floated as a running mate for James P. Roosevelt Jr. The two men had crossed paths several times during the Coastal Rebellion and the Matanzas War, when Roosevelt was Military Governor of Cuba and Gunther a Florida state senator; their dealings had been cordial, and Roosevelt threw his weight behind the faction of the party pushing Gunther for the vice presidency. During the campaign Gunther travelled throughout the South holding rallies, urging voters to back the JCA ticket and "return sanity to Washington House."

As Vice President he kept a low profile. His principal function was to stand in for the president at events where Roosevelt was needed elsewhere — a duty Gunther privately described as "surrogate ribbon-cutting, gladhanding and baby kissing." The one area in which he took a genuine and sustained interest was the Olympic movement, which the JCA as a whole regarded with the same suspicion it directed at every other form of internationalism. He attended the America Games in 1918, and after it was announced that Boston would host the 1922 World Olympic Games he volunteered to serve as the administration's liaison with the United States Olympic Committee and with local officials in Boston.

Accession to the presidency

On January 3, 1921, Gunther was at the residence of his brother Brandon outside Tallahassee, where he had gone for an extended New Year's holiday, when word reached him that President Roosevelt had died in his sleep at Washington House. Judge Andrew Bannon, who presided over the federal court in Tallahassee, was rushed out to the Gunther residence, and just after ten o'clock that morning Alden Gunther was sworn in as the twenty-sixth President of the United States.

He and his wife, Tabitha, were on the evening train out of Tallahassee and reached Franklin by the evening of the following day. There had been talk of putting the new president on an aeroplane; he refused, not trusting the machine to carry himself and his wife safely to the capital, and unwilling to let the Republic risk losing two presidents in the space of a day. The decision was reported approvingly at the time and has been cited since as characteristic of the man.

Presidency

The Roosevelt state funeral

Roosevelt's body had lain in state in Congress Hall since January 6. On January 10, 1921, tens of thousands of people lined Union Avenue to watch as the remains of the Hero of Havana were carried from the Hall up to the still-incomplete Cathedral of St. Michael the Archangel, on Mount Ida on the eastern side of the city — today generally referred to simply as the National Cathedral. It was the first presidential funeral service held at the Cathedral, which had been under construction since 1891, and it established a precedent that later administrations followed.

The caretaker announcement and the Carmichael nomination

Two days after the funeral the president made two announcements. The first was that he would not stand in the following year's presidential election. He had never intended to become president, he said, and regarded himself as a caretaker for the Roosevelt administration. The second was his choice of a new vice president: Senator Matthew Carmichael of Jefferson, whose nomination the Senate approved on January 30.

The renunciation, made barely a week into the term, shaped everything that followed. It cost Gunther whatever leverage a president ordinarily holds over his own party in an election year, and it opened the JCA nomination contest eighteen months early.

The Boiler Act and Tennessee v. United States

Congressional leadership was by this point split between the Federalist Speaker, George McKnight, and the Jeffersonian Secretary General, Isaac Potter, an arrangement negotiated in December 1920 that left Gunther without the free hand Roosevelt had enjoyed with the federal legislature. He also inherited the enforcement fight over the Boiler Safety and Certification Act of 1919.

The law had been passed in the aftermath of the 1917 St. Louis omnibus boiler disaster, in which thirty-two people were killed when the boiler of a poorly built and badly maintained steam-powered city bus exploded. The vehicle had been manufactured by Henderson Steamworks, a competitor of Lowry Steamer. Public outrage was strong enough that the JCA ultimately relented and allowed the act to pass, establishing federal minimum safety standards for steam-powered automobiles and requiring the states to certify that vehicles met them. Several states balked at the enforcement mandate and took the government to court.

Gunther and Attorney General Oliver Jacobson thus found themselves defending in court a law both privately disliked — the alternative being to abandon it and answer for the next boiler explosion. Tennessee v. the United States was argued before the Supreme Court in late April 1921, and on June 18 the court ruled seven to two that the act was constitutional: vehicles, and particularly buses of the sort involved in the St. Louis disaster, travelled across state lines, and the federal government was entitled to set safety standards for the good of interstate commerce.

The divided 67th Congress

The ruling emboldened the Federalists and a number of Democrats. Through the autumn of 1921 and the following spring, Congress saw repeated attempts to extend safety regulation to other modes of transport, and the Jeffersonians dug in. The Boiler Act had been a necessary evil, its members argued, but "regulation for regulation's sake" would not do. The argument moved from the floor of the House onto the campaign trail as the 1922 primaries got underway, and it framed much of the year's domestic debate.

The Fourth World Olympic Games

Before the general election contest could properly begin, attention across the country — and much of the world — shifted to Boston, Massachusetts, where the Fourth World Olympic Games opened on Friday, August 11, 1922, with 1,945 athletes from 44 nations in attendance. President Gunther presided over the opening ceremonies at the newly built Boston Liberty Stadium.

The presiding genius of the Games, however, was former President Cornelius Roosevelt II, head of the United States Olympic Committee. Three months earlier Roosevelt had overseen the Third America Games in Philadelphia, where American athletes competed to qualify for Boston, and had announced that Philadelphia and the USOC would build a new stadium in time for the 1926 competition, making the city the permanent home of the America Games. He had also presided over a smaller America Winter Games at Hugosville in January. Having attended the London Games of 1914 and the Rome Games of 1918, the former president was determined that Boston should set the standard for the Olympic movement thereafter — in organisation and preparation, but above all in style and spectacle.

Liberty Stadium, though small by later standards, seated 45,000, more than double the capacity of the Rome stadium of 1918. Military and civilian marching bands opened the ceremony, with flag dancers on the field forming the new Olympic logo that Roosevelt had developed with the World Olympic Committee for these Games. The bands then gathered to play the Olympic Hymn as the new Olympic Flag was raised. A further innovation followed: a gold-medal athlete from Italy, the previous host, entered the stadium bearing a torch and met an American gold medallist halfway across the field, who carried it on to light a waiting cauldron — the first Olympic Flame. As the cauldron was lit, the airship USAS Jefferson passed over the stadium. President Gunther then stepped to the central podium and delivered the line for which he is now best remembered: "Ladies and Gentlemen, athletes of the world, I declare open the fourth Olympiad of our modern era, here in the city of Boston!" A burst of fireworks followed. The sequence set the pattern for Olympic opening ceremonies ever since.

Over the two weeks of competition the United States took first place in both the total medal count, with 36, and the gold medal count, with 14; Great Britain followed with 34 and 11, and Prussia with 22 and 9. At the close of the Games it was announced that Prussia would host in 1926, at Hamburg. The Boston Games were widely judged the greatest success the Olympic movement had yet achieved. Across the United States and much of North America, events were followed live on the radio in real time — a first for the Olympics and still a novelty in sports reporting generally. Results were relayed to Europe and beyond by wireless telegraph for broadcast there, and the opening ceremony itself was carried live by shortwave to London and Berlin as an experiment, with mixed results.

The Election of 1922

Gunther's refusal to stand had left the Jeffersonian field wide open. Vice President Carmichael entered early and was challenged by Senator Frederick Stark of Platte, Representative Gene Dempsey of Ohio, Mayor Ulysses Brannock of St. Louis, and Attorney General Jacobson. Gunther declined to endorse anyone during the state convention season, a neutrality that reportedly soured relations between the president and his vice president. Stark pulled ahead through the spring, and when the party gathered at Atlanta in mid-July he was confirmed as the nominee with Dempsey as his running mate. Stark promised to "continue the work of reducing the federal government and restoring power to the states," with the clear implication that Gunther had let that mandate slip. But the convention had exposed a problem the party could not talk its way past: James P. Roosevelt Jr. had been the JCA's entire public personality, and no one in Atlanta could replace him. Stark left as the nominee of a party that had spent the summer arguing with itself, and the campaign never recovered its energy.

The Democratic field was narrower. Governor Andrew Kent of Ohio was an early favourite alongside Senator Stephen Sexton, until 1920 the popular mayor of Calumet, and Derrick Shauck, a Manhattan businessman. Kent and Sexton ran close through the early primaries until April, when the governor's youngest child was killed in a riding accident; Kent withdrew within the week. Shauck, who had never built support outside Manhattan, followed him out in May. Sexton took the nomination and successfully advocated for Harold Bride, the Governor of Oregon, as his running mate. Addressing the convention at Pittsburgh in late June, Sexton promised to bring "common sense judgment" back to Washington House and to reverse the "worst excesses of JCA rule" in the size of government, adding: "The American people do not want the government in every aspect of their lives. However, we do expect the trains to run on time, the roads to be safe and easy to drive, and to have adequate access to communication and power — these are things the government can and should be taking care of."

The Federalist contest turned on Senator Lance Hawkins of Iowa, a vocal opponent of the Roosevelt and Gunther administrations — particularly of Roosevelt's moves against the Federal Investigative Service and the United States Railway Company — and Governor Gabriel Lockhart of Pennsylvania, who had been a prominent supporter of the Boiler Act. Hawkins made heavy use of the growing radio network, giving interviews heard by tens of thousands across the country and buying advertising time on stations in several major markets, a first for a United States presidential campaign. The strategy gave him an insurmountable lead by the spring of 1922. With the nomination settled before a vote was cast, the Boston convention turned on the choice of a running mate, and an early favourite electrified the delegates: Representative Lynne Powell of New Jersey. Six years earlier an attempt to place Representative Eleanor Clampton of Oregon on the ticket had been blocked by the party's own presidential nominee; Hawkins took the opposite view, judging that Powell would energise the electorate, and guided her nomination through the convention himself. Announced on the last day of June, Lynne Powell became the first woman nominated to a major-party national ticket, and the news dominated the press for days.

The Socialist Progressives settled quickly on their party chairman, Senator Neil Brooks of the District of Manhattan, one of the first SPA senators in the country's history. The open question at the Calumet convention was the vice-presidential place. Brooks unofficially preferred the new mayor of Calumet, Peter Holbrook, but others feared Holbrook was too radical — and radical was the last thing the party wanted while the news from the Union of European Republics carried reports of the Holtz government curtailing freedom of expression and persecuting religious groups. The moderates favoured Governor Leonard Lincoln of Indiana, great-grandson of the former Chief Justice Abraham Lincoln. The moderates prevailed, and the ticket was Brooks and Lincoln.

The four candidates criss-crossed the country through September and October, drawing large crowds from St. Augustine to Astoria, and met in Calumet for a radio debate broadcast by the National Broadcasting Service and heard live by millions. The consensus in the following morning's papers was that Hawkins and Sexton had stood out, that Brooks had seemed disconnected, and that Stark had come across as simply angry. More clearly than in 1916, interviews with the public suggested that the broadcast had caused a substantial number of listeners either to reconsider their intended vote or to make up their minds at last.

The first run-off

Voters went to the polls on November 7, 1922, and for the first time since the passage of the Fourth Amendment no candidate carried an electoral majority. Hawkins led on both counts, with 226 electoral votes and roughly 30.5 per cent of the popular vote, some 8.6 million ballots. Sexton followed with 221 electoral votes and about 28 per cent, roughly 8 million votes, narrowly ahead of Stark, whose 193 electoral votes and approximately 26 per cent — some 7.4 million — placed the Jeffersonian ticket third. Brooks finished a distant fourth with 91 electoral votes and nearly 15 per cent, about 4.2 million votes. Election authorities and the Electoral College confirmed the result quickly, and a run-off between Hawkins and Sexton was set for December 5.

The press and the public followed the first run-off closely, fascinated by the novelty of voting twice in a month for the presidency — a procedure that has since become routine. It produced, as intended, a clear and undisputed winner. Hawkins took 376 electoral votes to Sexton's 355, ten more than the threshold required, with 52 per cent of the popular vote, some 13.2 million ballots, against 48 per cent and 12.2 million for the Democrats. Newspapers and radio commentators pronounced the Fourth Amendment a success. It was not, however, the biggest story of the day: for the first time in American history a woman would be vice president, a heartbeat away from the highest office in the land.

The run-off also had a consequence for the calendar that had been written into the amendment but never before tested. The ten-week transition ran from the deciding election rather than from the general, and because the presidency had been decided on December 5 rather than November 7, the inauguration of the twenty-seventh president fell in February rather than January — the first February inauguration under the amendment, and the pattern followed in every run-off year since.

The Hugosville Winter Games

The last major event of Gunther's presidency came days after the run-off, when the first Winter Olympic Games opened at Hugosville, Brandt, on Friday, December 8, 1922. A far more modest affair than Boston, Hugosville hosted 267 athletes from twelve nations: Austria-Hungary, California, Canada, Denmark, Great Britain, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway, Prussia, Sweden, Switzerland, and the United States. Norway swept the competition, taking the most medals overall with eleven and the most in each individual category; Sweden was second with six, and Canada and Prussia tied for third with four apiece. When the Games closed on December 16 after eight days of competition, it was announced that Turin, Italy, would host the 1926 Winter Games, Prussia having no location suitable for winter sport.

Final months and the transition

Gunther spent a quiet final Christmas at Washington House and delivered the New Year's Eve address. Twelve years after Cornelius Roosevelt II began the tradition it had become a fixed part of the rhythm of the year's end; where Roosevelt's words had reached a few hundred hobbyists, Gunther's reached millions. January passed in a blur of packing interspersed with meetings with the incoming administration.

Hawkins arrived in the capital a week before the inauguration and stayed at what is now known as Jefferson House, the medium-sized town home across the street from Washington House that has served as the presidential guest house since the 1890s. The outgoing and incoming presidents held several substantive meetings. Of particular concern was the news out of the Union of European Republics, where Mathais Holtz had begun a purge of the civil service, arresting and frequently executing thousands of officials he denounced as "wreckers." Scarcely less troubling was the rise of nationalist societies in Prussia demanding revenge against the UER and the overturning of the Great European War settlement. The Exterior Department assured the president-elect that King Karl-Albrecht I would keep the radicals at bay, though a few within the department noted privately that the king, then in his late sixties, was said to be suffering health difficulties the court was working to keep quiet. Should he die within a few years the crown would pass to his grandson, a young man of twenty with no experience of government and reported sympathies with the radicals. Prussia, and much of central Europe with it, could destabilise.

Retirement and death

At noon on February 12, 1923, Alden Gunther stood in the shadow of Congress Hall and watched Lance Hawkins of Iowa take the oath of office, ending an unplanned and — by the evidence of his private papers — unwanted tenure as chief executive. He left believing he had done what he had said he would do: kept the nation on the course Roosevelt had set, nothing more and nothing less. He and Tabitha left Franklin for Florida that same evening.

The retirement was brief. On November 11, 1925, two years after leaving Washington House, Gunther died of pneumonia at his home outside Tallahassee at the age of seventy-seven.

Legacy

Gunther is remembered less for what he did than for what he declined to do. He is the only president to have renounced a candidacy of his own within a fortnight of taking the oath, and historians have generally taken him at his word that he understood the office as a trust held on another man's behalf. The judgement on that choice has been mixed. It gave the country a stable and unremarkable transfer of power at a moment when the governing party had lost its central figure without warning, and it spared the 1922 election the distortion of an incumbent running on a mandate he had never sought. It also left the Jeffersonian Constitutional Alliance to discover, over eighteen public months, that it had no identity apart from James P. Roosevelt Jr. — a discovery that cost the party the presidency it had held for six years.

The substantive legacy of the term belongs largely to the courts. Tennessee v. the United States settled the constitutionality of federal transport safety regulation on interstate commerce grounds and became the foundation for the broader regulatory expansion of the following decades, an outcome Gunther and his attorney general had argued for in public and regretted in private. The 1922 election, meanwhile, gave the Fourth Amendment its first genuine test and established both the run-off and the February inauguration as working parts of the constitutional machinery.

He is also remembered, more warmly, for the Boston Games. His interest in the Olympic movement had been genuine and slightly eccentric within his own party, and his eleven words at the podium of Liberty Stadium remain the most quoted sentence he ever spoke. Alden Gunther was the last President of the United States born before the War Between the States, and the last to reach the office without ever having sought it.
