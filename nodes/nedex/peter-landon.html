Peter Landon (1844-1922) was the twenty-second President of the United States, serving from March 5, 1903 to March 1905. Born enslaved on a plantation in Lowndes County, Alabama, Landon joined the slave uprising of 1860 as a sixteen-year-old, served as a runner for Free State commanders, and emerged from the War Between the States as a free man with an education, a surname, and a determination to make good on the republic's promise of equality. He rose through the Freedmen's Party, the Alabama state legislature, the U.S. House of Representatives, and the U.S. Senate before being chosen as Alexander Fleak's vice-presidential running mate in 1898. When Fleak died on March 4, 1903, Landon became the first Black man to serve as President of the United States — forty-three years after the end of slavery.

His presidency was defined by two overlapping crises he had not sought and could not fully resolve: the Keyman v. Virginia ruling that gave constitutional sanction to segregated public accommodations under the "Separate AND Equal" doctrine, and a mounting confrontation with Spain over the Matanzas Bay exile community's gun-running operations against the republic. Landon navigated both with careful restraint that satisfied almost no one. He lost the 1904 Presidential Election decisively to Democrat Cornelius Roosevelt II, who promised more forceful action on both fronts. He was the first Black president, and the last for nearly four decades.

Early life and career

Peter Landon was born in 1844 on the Vancourt family plantation in Lowndes County, Alabama. His mother, Violette, was a house slave who secured a place in the household for her youngest son, sparing him from field work and giving him access to basic education in reading and writing. Had the war not come, he would likely have spent his life as a senior house servant.

The war did come. When the slave uprising led by Joshua Black swept through Lowndes County and central Alabama in 1860, the sixteen-year-old Landon joined the slave militia, serving first as a runner for local commanders and then as a liaison between the formerly enslaved and the Free State Army in the war's closing days. He chose his surname during this period — taking it from a Free State commander who had taken him under his wing and then died in action just before James Hawthorne's surrender in Atlanta.

When peace came, Landon threw himself into the work of reconstruction. He joined the Freedmen's Party and helped the new post-war administration — a mix of Northerners, educated freedmen, and local white moderates — begin to function. He enrolled in the Institute for Negro Advancement in Montgomery, building on his basic education. By the end of the 1860s he was working as a teacher and with the Freedmen's Bureau, helping integrate the formerly enslaved population into civic and economic life. Alabama, unlike Mississippi and Louisiana, remained near evenly split between Black and white residents by 1870 and was more politically volatile than its neighbors — a fact that shaped Landon's career-long instinct for coalition-building over confrontation.

The Freedmen's Party — by the 1870s effectively an affiliate of the Federalist Party — recognized him as an ideal candidate for state office. In 1872 he was elected to the Alabama state legislature, where he quickly earned a reputation as a bridge-builder respected by Federalists and Freedmen's Party members alike, and even grudgingly acknowledged by opposition Democrats and Republicans. In 1882 party leaders convinced him to run for the U.S. House of Representatives, where he served fourteen years — the maximum allowed under the term limits of the Constitution of 1861. In 1896 the Alabama legislature chose him as one of the state's senators in Franklin.

When Alexander Fleak began selecting a running mate in 1898, Landon was the leading candidate from the Freedmen's Party wing. Fleak wanted to signal his party's continued commitment to racial equality — a pointed message directed at the Coastal South. Landon confessed in a letter to a close friend that he was surprised by the offer, having imagined he would serve a term or two in the Senate before retiring or perhaps running for governor as his final act. He accepted nonetheless. In 1899 he was sworn in as the second Black Vice President of the United States, after Frederick Bailey.

Assumption of the presidency

When Fleak died on March 4, 1903, Landon was in Alabama attending to family matters following his elder brother's death. He rushed back to Franklin and was sworn in the following day — March 5, 1903 — in a simple ceremony in the House Chamber, with Chief Justice Matthew Hale Pemberton administering the oath. His brief inaugural remarks were measured and deliberate: he spoke of grief, of Fleak's steady example, and of his own intention to govern with moderation and restraint, remarking without overstatement that public office in the republic "belongs to all who would uphold the Constitution with diligence and honor."

Across the country, reactions were sharply divided. In the Freedmen States — Louisiana, Mississippi, and Alabama — celebrations erupted in cities, towns, and rural communities alike. In the Coastal South, large crowds gathered in Richmond, Charleston, Atlanta, and St. Augustine to protest. The rest of the country fell somewhere between uncertainty and cautious pride. Just 43 years after the end of slavery, a Black man resided in Washington House.

Within days of taking office, Landon appointed Zachary Lowell, former governor of Oregon and a moderate Federalist senator, as vice president under Article III of the Constitution of 1861. He chose Lowell deliberately to project stability and quiet fears that the republic's first Black president might govern as a radical.

Presidency

Keyman v. Virginia

The first major crisis of Landon's presidency came from the Supreme Court. Josiah Keyman, a 27-year-old Black lawyer and son of former slaves working with the National Equality Council, had been arrested in Richmond after refusing to use the Black "standing-only" section of the city's trolley system. He sued the state of Virginia, arguing that the Harmony Law requiring racial segregation on public transportation violated the Constitution of 1861's Article I citizenship protections.

On May 17, 1903, the Supreme Court announced a 6–3 decision upholding the Harmony Law's constitutionality — with significant conditions. Chief Justice Matthew Hale Pemberton, writing for the majority, held that racial separation in public accommodations did not inherently abridge the rights of citizenship, provided that accommodations were furnished in substantially equal measure and manner. He found that the Richmond Street & Rail Company had violated Article I by failing to provide equal accommodations to Black passengers and that Keyman's arrest had been unconstitutional — but then laid out how legally permissible separation could be structured in the future. The ruling gave birth to the doctrine of Separate AND Equal.

The dissent, written by Associate Justice Sherman P. Rutledge on behalf of three justices, was widely circulated by civil rights groups: Our Constitution recognizes no superior or inferior classes of citizens…to force a man to a separate place because of the color of his skin is to deny him the equal enjoyment of his citizenship, no matter how finely upholstered the compartment may be. In the eyes of our Constitution, there is only one class: the American people. A separate concurring opinion by Associate Justice Jeremiah L. Wexford — arguing that racial separation was a legitimate exercise of state police power, not a badge of inferiority — was taken up by the Coastal South as justification for further expansion of the Harmony Laws.

Landon waited three days before issuing a statement. When it came, on May 21, it satisfied no one:

The Supreme Court of our republic has issued its ruling in the Keyman vs. Virginia case. While it is not the ruling I and many of my fellow citizens would have preferred, it is nevertheless measured and fair. It neither condemns nor praises segregatory practices, and reaffirms the national commitment to equality. We call on the states wishing to pursue such practices to do so with the spirit of this ruling in mind, and ensure that the equal rights of all Americans are protected.

Coastal Southerners resented his meddling at all. Most Federalists were appalled at the implicit acceptance of any form of legalized segregation. Freedmen's Party members were outraged that the first Black president would not outright condemn the practice. Daily protests were held outside Washington House and the nearly completed Supreme Court Building in Franklin. Marches took place in cities across the country, in favor and in opposition, depending on the region.

Landon's restraint was calculated rather than indifferent. His private papers reveal that military intelligence and Justice Department investigators were warning of a possible insurrection attempt in the Coastal South, likely supported by gun-runners from the Matanzas Bay exile community. He was unwilling to add fuel to that fire.

The Cuba crisis

The gun-running threat Landon feared had been building since the Belle of Matanzas affair under Fleak. In November 1903 it escalated. U.S. Army intelligence warned that another run was planned for Florida. The Navy dispatched the USS Manhattan to intercept. On November 18, the Manhattan located the Cuban vessel San Angelo and launched a boarding party — whereupon the San Angelo's crew opened fire. Three sailors were wounded; Ensign Thomas M. Keeler was killed. The gun runners fled back toward Matanzas Bay.

The AMERICAN BLOOD SPILLED IN THE GULF! headline in the Franklin Observer captured the public mood. Spain denied knowledge of the incident; Cuban officials claimed the confrontation had occurred in international waters and that the vessel must have been confused with pirates. Exterior Secretary Harold Forbes pressed for a full investigation. Madrid agreed in principle but colonial authorities in Havana stonewalled, and Spain — caught in delicate negotiations with Cuba and Puerto Rico over autonomy — backed down. The press hammered Landon for inaction. Senator Cornelius Roosevelt II of Manhattan delivered a fiery attack in New York City: If President Landon does not have the backbone to defend our country from the sons of turncoat slavers, who won't avenge the death of an American sailor, then he needs to be thrown out of Washington House post haste.

In early 1904, a second incident occurred. The Coast Guard cutter Liberty spotted an unflagged schooner attempting to land boats near Miami, Florida. When the Liberty gave chase, the schooner ran aground near Key Largo. The crew had abandoned the vessel and tried to set it alight — but the Coast Guard recovered rifles of Spanish manufacture, crates with Cuban customs stamps, and letters connecting the crew to anti-government extremists in Florida, the Carolinas, and Virginia. The ship was named the Spirit of 1861. Spain and Cuba again denied any connection and refused to allow American investigators access to the Matanzas Bay communities.

Then, in October, the USS Harrington — a Navy patrol boat stationed in Mobile — went radio silent after reporting an unknown inbound vessel near the Florida panhandle. On October 2, debris washed ashore near Gulf Shores, Alabama, showing signs of fire or attack. The wreck was located on November 1, approximately four and a half miles offshore from Perdido Key, with bodies recovered and evidence of attack. The public assumed the Matanzas clique had sunk an American warship. Landon issued an ultimatum to the Spanish ambassador on November 4 — allow American naval forces into Matanzas Bay to investigate, or risk "deteriorating relations." Spain, preoccupied with the political upheaval in Europe as the Great European War wound down, barely responded.

The 1904 election and defeat

Landon announced his intention to seek his own full term in January 1904. Despite challenges from within the Federalist Party's primary, he secured the nomination, with Vice President Zachary Lowell agreeing to remain on the ticket. The Democrats held their first primary election and ultimately nominated Senator Cornelius Roosevelt II of Manhattan, with Platte Governor Patrick Hannah as his running mate. The Republicans nominated Virginia Governor Francis Periman with Jefferson Senator Joseph Chapius.

The campaign was dominated entirely by Cuba, the Harrington, and the Keyman ruling. Roosevelt promised direct action against the Matanzas Bay community and a muscular foreign policy. Periman promised states' rights and an end to federal interference in the Coastal South's internal affairs. Landon promised continued diplomacy. The Harrington wreck announcement a week before the election destroyed whatever chance he had. On November 8, Roosevelt won 366 electoral votes to Landon's 238 and Periman's 126.

On December 21, following the Electoral College's confirmation, President-Elect Roosevelt gave the Georgetown Address at the site of the old federal capital — promising that if Spain had not turned over those responsible for the Harrington sinking by April 1, he would order the Navy in regardless and ask Congress to declare war. The press cheered. The stage was set for the republic's first real conflict in half a century.

Legacy

Peter Landon's historical legacy is layered and contested. His presidency lasted barely two years, was defined by crises not of his making, and ended in a landslide defeat. By almost any conventional measure, it was not a successful administration.

And yet. He was the first Black man to serve as President of the United States — a fact of enormous symbolic weight in a republic that had abolished slavery barely four decades before. He chose restraint over theater in both the Keyman crisis and the Cuba confrontations — a choice his critics called weakness and historians have come to see more charitably as the calculation of a man acutely aware of how easily a Black president's forcefulness could be reframed as radicalism or instability. Whether greater boldness on Keyman v. Virginia might have altered the Harmony Laws' trajectory is one of the more debated counterfactuals in American history.

He left office in March 1905, returned to Alabama, and lived for another seventeen years — long enough to see Cornelius Roosevelt II's Caribbean War, the rise of the Social Progressive Alliance, and the republic's continued, uneven march toward the equality he had spent his life trying to help it achieve. He died on June 3, 1922.
