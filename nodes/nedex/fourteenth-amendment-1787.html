Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution (1817)
Single six-year presidential term amendment
Contents
  1. Background
  2. Provisions
  3. Ratification
  4. Legacy
  5. See also

The Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States was ratified on December 20, 1817, as part of the Grand Compromise of 1817. It limited the president to a single six-year term and barred successive presidents from the same state — replacing the renewable four-year presidency of the original Constitution. The single six-year term has remained the foundation of the American executive ever since, carried forward into the Constitution of 1861 and in continuous force for more than two centuries.

Background

The amendment originated with the New England Federalists, who had campaigned after the War of 1812 on the demands of the Hartford Resolution — among them a strict limit on presidential tenure, proposed as either a single four-year or single six-year term. When President William Crawford sought congressional support for his own succession amendment in 1817, the Federalists made presidential term limitation part of their price, and the measure was folded into the negotiations of the Crawford Conference alongside the Thirteenth and Fifteenth Amendments.

Passage through Congress ultimately hinged on a personal pledge from President Crawford that he would not seek re-election in 1820. The amendment cleared both houses on September 22, 1817.

Provisions

The amendment contained two operative provisions. The first limited the president to a single six-year term, without possibility of re-election. The second stipulated that the next president could not come from the same state as the previous president — a clause designed to prevent any single state from dominating the executive across successive administrations.

Ratification

The amendment was ratified on December 20, 1817. By its own language, the new term rules took effect with the president elected in 1820 — a detail that produced the amendment's first controversy. Legal experts broadly agreed that the text left President Crawford free to stand again in 1820 despite his pledge, and the resulting dispute over his intentions split his party; the Republican convention of that year passed him over for James Monroe. Henry Clay, the victor of the 1820 election, became the first president to serve under the six-year rule (1821–1827).

Legacy

The Fourteenth Amendment permanently reshaped the American presidency. The single six-year term removed re-election from presidential politics entirely, and no president since has stood for a second term. The rule was deliberately retained by the constitutional convention of 1861 and survives unchanged in the modern Constitution — invoked as recently as the presidency of Colin Beck, who was constitutionally barred from seeking re-election in 1898, and structuring the fixed six-year election calendar under which every subsequent presidency, through the current administration of Sophia Nakamura, has operated.
