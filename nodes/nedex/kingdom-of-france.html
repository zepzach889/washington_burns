France, formally the Kingdom of France, was a constitutional monarchy in western Europe that existed from the Bourbon Restoration of 1814 until the communalist revolution of 1904, which dissolved the kingdom and gave rise to the Union of European Republics. Through nine decades of constitutional monarchy under five kings, France emerged from the turbulence of the Napoleonic era as a major European power, building an overseas empire, developing its industry, and forging the Franco-British alliance that defined the continent's balance of power through the end of the nineteenth century. The kingdom's final years were consumed by the Great European War (1899–1904), and it was the strains of that conflict — combined with decades of unresolved social pressure — that ultimately brought the monarchy to its end.

The royal court of Henri VI escaped the revolution and reconstituted itself on the island of Corsica, where it maintained a claim to metropolitan France. Aligned with Prussia throughout the Global War (1932–1939), the Corsican Bourbon government placed itself permanently on the losing side of history. At the Treaty of Geneva (1939), the reigning Bourbon pretender formally renounced all claims to metropolitan France; the Bourbon dynastic claim was extinguished as a condition of international recognition, and the heir was subsequently recognised as King of Algeria. See Kingdom of Algeria.

The Bourbon Restoration (1814–1829)

For most of continental Europe, the conclusion of the Napoleonic Wars and the subsequent Congress of Vienna in 1815 marked the beginning of a new era of restored order. France was returned to the Bourbon dynasty in the person of Louis XVIII, who promulgated the Charter of 1814 at the behest of the Allied powers. On paper, the Charter was a document of compromise: it preserved the Napoleonic Code, guaranteed civil liberties, established a permanent legislature, and protected the property rights established since the Revolution. In practice, the new regime was pulled steadily rightward by the ultra-royalist faction that dominated the court.

Louis XVIII moved to compensate returning émigré nobles, purged Bonapartist officers from the military, and gradually restricted the free press. By 1820, wealth and property requirements had narrowed the voting franchise to fewer than one hundred thousand men. These retreats from the Charter's liberal promises created a slow accumulation of political frustration that the moderate king never fully reckoned with.

His brother and successor, Charles X (1824–1829), had no such moderation. Charles harbored ambitions of restoring the pre-revolutionary divine right of kings and set about advancing them as rapidly as political reality permitted. He reintroduced capital punishment for sacrilege in 1825, established a commission to restore confiscated properties to the Church, and compensated émigré nobles who had lost land since 1789.

The king's undoing came in the summer of 1829. After two successive elections returned a Chamber of Deputies hostile to the ultra-royalists, Charles X invoked Article 14 of the Charter on August 1st, suspending the government, imposing further press restrictions, narrowing the electorate, and calling for new elections under revised rules. Paris erupted. Barricades went up within days; the army was called in; atrocities committed by the most loyal units drove other soldiers to defect to the protesters' side. By August 15th the tide had shifted decisively, and the proscribed Chamber of Deputies met in defiance of the king's decree.

Charles X abdicated on August 20th. His attempt to pass the crown to his son, Louis Antoine, Duke of Angoulême, was rejected by the Chamber. On August 28th the legislators voted to offer the throne to Louis-Philippe, Duke of Orléans, styling the new ruler "King of the French." Charles X, his son, and his grandson Henri, Count of Chambord, departed for exile in Austria.

The August Monarchy (1829–1847)

Louis-Philippe entered his reign with genuine goodwill. He reaffirmed the Charter of 1814, restored press freedom, abolished the sacrilege law, and widened the electorate to nearly half a million Frenchmen. The early years of his reign were broadly peaceful, and France's international standing improved steadily.

By the late 1830s, however, the mood had soured. The advance of industrialization brought worsening conditions for urban workers, and a corresponding growth of leftist political organizations that the monarchy moved to suppress. The Legitimist faction — those who held that the main Bourbon line was the rightful dynasty — kept up a persistent campaign of legal agitation and occasional outright conspiracy, supporting in succession Charles X, then Louis Antoine, and then young Henri, Count of Chambord as the true king.

The immediate cause of the monarchy's fall was a series of poor harvests between 1844 and 1846. By 1847, hunger had become a visible and growing problem for the urban poor, and a broader economic contraction had eaten into the bourgeoisie's moderate prosperity. A reform movement gathering serious momentum was met with crackdowns: first in March, then more severely in May, following an assassination attempt in which the king escaped injury but his wife was wounded and two guards killed. The second crackdown ignited Paris. The local militia largely sided with the insurrection, and within days Louis-Philippe found himself surrounded. He abdicated on May 25th, 1847, and fled to Great Britain.

The Second Restoration and Henri V (1847–1886)

The weeks following Louis-Philippe's departure were politically treacherous, with the Chamber of Deputies and the newly assembled National People's Assembly competing for authority. It was at this moment that the Legitimists put forward their candidate: Henri, Count of Chambord, the grandson of Charles X, long in exile but possessed of an unexpected reformist reputation. After intensive negotiations, both bodies issued a joint invitation. Henri accepted, and entered Paris on August 1st, 1847, as Henri V, King of the French. He was twenty-six years old.

Henri V proved to be neither the reactionary the left feared nor the conservative the Legitimists had imagined. Working in concert with the leaders of both chambers, he guided the drafting of a Charter of 1848, ratified in February of that year, which enlarged the Chamber of People's Deputies, gave it substantially greater legislative authority, and reduced the Chamber of Peers to an advisory role. The franchise was widened, though it remained well short of universal.

The early years of Henri V's reign were marked by paternalist reform. State guilds were established to regulate industry and working conditions. Public housing construction expanded in the major cities, urban sanitation improved, and price controls were introduced on essential goods. A sustained public health initiative between 1848 and 1854 measurably reduced the incidence of cholera and typhoid fever in Paris, Lyon, and Marseille. France's rail network expanded dramatically between 1848 and 1880, transforming the domestic economy. Henri V financed conservative support through ambition rather than confrontation — major state-backed infrastructure programs created substantial wealth for industrialists and financiers who might otherwise have led opposition.

In foreign policy, Henri V was cautious but not passive. He quietly supported the emergence of the Imperial Federation of Italy in the early 1880s — a diplomatic achievement widely credited to his patient engagement and seen as cementing France's position as the patron of Catholic monarchies in Europe. The centrepiece of his foreign policy, however, was the relationship with Great Britain. In April 1859, Henri V became the first French monarch to make a state visit to Britain in nearly five centuries, meeting Queen Charlotte, her husband Prince Alexander, and Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli. Queen Charlotte returned the visit in June 1860. The two sovereigns developed a genuine personal warmth that outlasted the visit itself, and the Franco-British alignment became the cornerstone of Henri V's foreign policy for the remainder of his reign.

The 1860 visit coincided with the American War Between the States. Henri V, who admired Britain's opposition to slavery, had already curtailed French trade with the rebel southern Union. Following his conversations with Queen Charlotte, France expelled the ambassador representing the Hawthorne government and extended formal recognition to the Brandt government in Franklin — a significant diplomatic signal to the watching world. Henri V died in 1886 at the age of sixty-six, widely respected as the man who had stabilised France after decades of revolutionary turbulence without sacrificing either modernity or monarchy.

The Reign of Henri VI (1886–1904)

Henri V was succeeded by his son, Henri VI, a more idealistic and reformist figure than his father, though one who inherited a France that was wealthier, more restless, and more politically complicated than at any earlier point in the century. The growing urban middle class pressed for expanded suffrage and genuine civil liberties; a new generation of communalist organizers had established themselves in the industrial cities; and the conservative Chamber of Deputies remained deeply resistant to structural change.

Henri VI made modest concessions — expansions of the electorate, rural infrastructure investment, proposals for tax relief — but was hemmed in at every turn by the legislature, a bureaucracy formed in his father's image, and the monarchy's own structural limits. His popularity declined through the 1890s. The one bright moment was the birth of his son, Prince Louis-Charles, in 1887, born of Henri VI's marriage to Princess Adelheid of Bavaria. The boy, raised in both French and Bavarian traditions, was charismatic and beloved in both countries, and came to represent the hope of a new generation. That hope was shattered on April 26, 1899.

The Great European War and Revolution (1899–1904)

In February 1899, Adelheid's father, King Maximilian II of Bavaria, died unexpectedly. The Bavarian Diet named the twelve-year-old Prince Louis-Charles as king, with his uncle Prince Albrecht serving as regent (Albrecht himself being ineligible to rule due to a morganatic marriage). Prussia, whose Chancellor Leopold von Falkenhayn had long viewed a Franco-Bavarian dynastic union as a threat, declared the succession an act of dynastic aggression and advanced a rival Hohenzollern claimant. France, Bavaria, and the South German Confederation rejected the Prussian position.

Then came the assassination. While Prince Louis-Charles was visiting Munich with his mother on April 26th, a bomb was thrown at the royal procession. The blast killed Louis-Charles instantly and wounded both Queen Adelheid and Prince Albrecht. The attacker, a known radical named Johann Reiber, claimed under interrogation to have received funding from contacts in Prussian-aligned Frankfurt. Henri VI, devastated, issued a royal proclamation declaring the killing an act of war by stealth. Prussia denied any involvement, but the damage was irreparable.

By early May, demands had escalated into declarations. France declared war on Prussia on May 7th; Prussia mobilized the Union of German States in response. The Great European War had begun. France and its allies — the South German Confederation and Austria — fought Prussia and the Union of German States across multiple fronts. The conflict was brutal and exhausting, and it transformed France's political landscape irrevocably. The strains of total war — manpower losses, economic disruption, and years of sacrifice for a cause that seemed increasingly distant from ordinary people's lives — fed a radicalism that the Second Restoration's moderate reformism had never fully defused.

By 1904, with the war entering its final phase, revolutionary conditions had ripened across both France and the South German states simultaneously. Communalist uprisings spread through Paris, Lyon, and Marseille over the spring and summer. The army, exhausted and politically fractured, could not or would not suppress them with the force necessary. Henri VI and his court, unable to hold the capital, fled Paris and eventually established themselves on the island of Corsica, where they declared their intention to continue the legitimate government of France. They were not recognised as such by any major power for long.

In the autumn of 1904, revolutionary delegates from France and the South German states convened to establish the Union of European Republics, which declared itself the successor government of both nations. The Kingdom of France had ceased to exist on the continent. The new UER government additionally announced, in 1906, that it would not honour any debts held by its pre-revolutionary predecessors — a declaration that triggered a cascading financial crisis across European markets and added an economic dimension to the war's already devastating legacy.

Aftermath: The Corsican Court and the Kingdom of Algeria

The Corsican court maintained its claim to metropolitan France through the following decades, styling itself the government-in-exile and operating as a persistent challenge to the UER's legitimacy. Through the 1910s and 1920s, it occupied an uncomfortable diplomatic position — recognised by some conservative European courts as a matter of legitimist principle, ignored by the practical realities of the international order in which the UER was the operating government of France.

When the Global War began in 1932 with Prussia's invasion of UER territories, the Corsican Bourbon government never wavered — it aligned explicitly with the Prussian alliance from the outset, and when the United States entered the war, the Corsican court's formal Prussian alignment became still more explicit. In the history books, the Corsican Bourbon government would always be listed in the Prussian column. Allied forces occupied Corsica as part of the armistice terms in 1938; the Bourbon court fled to Algiers rather than accept capture.

At the Treaty of Geneva (1939), the reigning Bourbon pretender formally renounced all claims to metropolitan France as a strict condition of any international recognition for a successor Bourbon state. The dynastic claim to France was extinguished permanently. The UER was internationally recognised as the legitimate government of metropolitan France. The Bourbon heir was subsequently recognised as King of Algeria — a French settler state in all but name, built on the substantial European population of the Algerian territory. The indigenous Algerian population's relationship to the new kingdom, and the unresolved questions of sovereignty and self-determination that relationship entailed, became the defining political challenge of the Kingdom of Algeria's early decades. See Kingdom of Algeria.

Government

France was governed under a constitutional monarchy throughout its existence from 1814 to 1904, though the character of that monarchy shifted considerably across its constitutional instruments. The Charter of 1814, promulgated under Louis XVIII, established the basic structure of a bicameral legislature alongside a powerful crown. The Charter of 1848, negotiated during the early reign of Henri V, substantially strengthened the Chamber of People's Deputies and reduced the Chamber of Peers to an advisory role, while leaving the monarchy's executive authority largely intact. Genuine parliamentary government — in which the President of the Council required the confidence of the Chamber — developed in practice over the second half of the nineteenth century.

The Chamber of People's Deputies was the lower and more influential house, elected on a restricted but gradually expanding franchise. The Chamber of Peers served an advisory and ceremonial function under the Charter of 1848. The President of the Council served as head of government, managing day-to-day administration and responsible in practice to the Chamber, while the king retained significant influence over foreign policy, military command, and major appointments.
