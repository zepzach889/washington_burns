The history of Spain in the modern era is defined by three interlocking themes: the slow dissolution of empire, the persistent tension between monarchy and republic, and the deepening fear of revolutionary communism that ultimately drove Spain into catastrophic alignment with Prussia during the Global War. From the restoration of the Bourbon monarchy in 1814 to the armistice of 1938, Spain travelled from the largest empire in the Western world to a nation stripped of every overseas possession, confronting an uncertain future on the Iberian Peninsula.

The Bourbon Restoration and the Death of Don Carlos

The defeat of Napoleon Bonaparte and the restoration of the Bourbon monarchy in 1814 returned King Ferdinand VII to the Spanish throne. Ferdinand's reign was marked by the reimposition of absolute authority and the systematic rollback of the liberal reforms enacted during his absence. His government crushed liberal agitation with violence and imprisoned or exiled reformers by the thousands.

The critical divergence from the broader pattern of Bourbon history came in the mid-1820s, when Don Carlos — Ferdinand's younger brother and presumptive heir — died in a carriage accident. In the absence of a male claimant around whom traditionalist and clerical factions could rally, the political crisis that might otherwise have torn Spain apart through decades of civil conflict never fully materialised. The Carlist movement, lacking its namesake, dissolved before it could organise. Spain was spared a generation of internal war, but the underlying tensions between liberal constitutionalists and conservative traditionalists did not disappear — they simply found other channels of expression.

Ferdinand VII died in 1837 without a surviving male heir. The crown passed to his infant daughter, Isabella, with her mother, Queen Maria Christina, assuming the regency.

The Regency of Maria Christina (1837–1848)

Maria Christina governed as regent for eleven years, navigating between liberal constitutionalists who demanded an expanded Cortes and conservative traditionalists who viewed any concession to liberalism as the first step toward revolution. Her regency produced a functioning constitutional framework — the Cortes was restored, a constitutional monarchy formally established — but its provisions satisfied no one entirely. The liberals found it too timid; the conservatives found it too dangerous.

The regency period also saw the final collapse of Spain's American empire. One by one, the mainland colonies that had been in revolt since the 1810s achieved independence, a process essentially complete by the 1830s. Spain retained Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines, along with scattered Pacific holdings. These remnants of empire would define — and ultimately doom — Spanish foreign policy for the next seventy years.

The Long Reign of Isabella II (1848–c.1890)

Ferdinand VIII and the Caribbean War (c.1890–1907)

Isabella II died around 1890 and was succeeded by her son, who reigned as Ferdinand VIII. His reign inherited the full weight of his mother's unresolved contradictions and added catastrophe of its own.

The Hollow Rotation

Ferdinand VIII presided over a Liberal-Conservative rotation that had become fully hollowed out. Both parties were discredited by corruption and paralysis. A nascent Republican movement pressed at the margins of the Cortes. Regionalist blocs in Catalonia and the Basque Country extracted concessions in exchange for parliamentary support that neither major party could govern without.

The broader international situation added to Spain's anxiety. The Great European War (1899–c.1904) — triggered by the assassination of Bavaria's King Louis-Charles and fought across western and central Europe — ended with communist revolutions in France and the South German states. The resulting Union of European Republics, formed in 1904, placed a revolutionary state committed to the dissolution of national identities and the suppression of religion directly on Spain's northeastern border. Ferdinand VIII's government watched with alarm but could do nothing.

The Caribbean War (1905–1906)

The war that destroyed Spain's last Caribbean empire began with an ultimatum. President Cornelius Roosevelt II, elected in 1904 on an explicit platform of resolving the Cuban situation, demanded the extradition of the Matanzas Bay Clique's leadership. Ferdinand VIII's government — calculating that the Americans were bluffing, or that European powers would intervene diplomatically — refused. Three days before Roosevelt's April 1, 1905 deadline, Spanish Ambassador Rafael Martínez de Ibarra delivered the rejection.

The United States declared war in April 1905. The American strategy was straightforward: naval blockade of Cuba and Puerto Rico, support for local independence movements, and overwhelming ground force once the islands were isolated. The Spanish navy, outmatched in the Caribbean from the outset, was unable to prevent the blockade. The Havana Landings came on August 29, after forty-eight hours of continuous naval bombardment; the fight for the colonial capital — home to the largest Spanish garrison on the island — lasted into late October, with the city finally falling to American forces on October 29. American forces spent the remainder of the year securing Cuba's three western provinces: Pinar del Río, La Habana, and Matanzas, while Spanish forces prepared their last stands at Santiago de Cuba and Camagüey.

A brief Spanish naval counteroffensive at San Juan in March 1906 temporarily reversed American gains in Puerto Rico, but the Second Battle of San Juan in April decisively ended Spanish naval resistance in the theatre. On May 2, the governor of Puerto Rico, Joaquín Herrera-Valdés, surrendered alongside the last Spanish soldiers on the island; the following day, rebels declared the Republic of Puerto Rico, and President Roosevelt extended recognition and pledged protection.

The final Spanish position on Cuba — a redoubt around Camagüey — fell in June 1906, after a month of heavy fighting that began with the American march from Nuevitas in late May. Governor Hernando Ruiz de Peñalver surrendered on June 9. The United Provinces of Cuba had already been proclaimed on May 7, ahead of Camagüey's fall.

Denmark offered to host peace negotiations, and both sides accepted. Exterior Secretary Jonathan B. Fairchild dispatched career diplomats Charles E. Harrington and Samuel R. Whitlock to lay initial groundwork with Danish Foreign Minister Erik Rosenkrantz and former Spanish Ambassador Rafael Martínez de Ibarra. Formal talks opened in July at Christianborg Palace under King Frederick VIII of Denmark, with Fairchild and Spanish Foreign Minister Alejandro de Villamar y Cortés conducting the substantive negotiations. Crown Prince Alejandro represented his ailing father at the table.

The American position was firm: Spain would relinquish all claims to Cuba and Puerto Rico and pay war indemnities. Ferdinand VIII's representatives initially sought to retain Puerto Rico or reduce the indemnity, but the Americans made clear neither concession was available. Villamar y Cortés and Crown Prince Alejandro accepted the core terms and negotiated the particulars: Spanish citizens on the islands would have up to a year to settle their affairs and depart, or else become citizens of the new republics, with property held in absentia forfeit; prisoners of war would be exchanged and repatriated promptly; colonial debts were cancelled outright over Madrid's protests; and the United States pledged not to annex either island, while reserving the right to a temporary military occupation of no more than five years to help the new governments establish themselves. The Treaty of Copenhagen was signed on July 27, 1906.

Ferdinand VIII died in 1907. He had outlived his empire.

Political Aftermath

The Caribbean War shattered the Liberal-Conservative rotation. The Liberal government that had taken Spain to war — calculating that American resolve would falter — was finished. The Conservatives, who had criticised the government's management of Cuba for years, were briefly ascendant but offered no real answers to Spain's strategic situation. The Republican movement surged, drawing strength from the argument that the monarchy had demonstrably failed to protect Spanish honour or Spanish interests. Regionalists in Catalonia and the Basque Country extracted further concessions from a central government too weakened to refuse.

Most significantly, the war planted the seeds of a new political tendency: an explicitly nationalist right, drawing on military officers humiliated by defeat, on the Catholic hierarchy's intensifying fear of the UER, and on a younger generation of conservative intellectuals who concluded that the old establishment was constitutionally incapable of defending Spain. This tendency would not consolidate into formal political organisation for another decade, but its emotional and ideological foundations were laid in the ruins of Copenhagen.

The Reign of Alejandro I: Stabilization and Radicalization (1907–1932)

Alejandro I ascended to the throne in 1907 already marked by the experience of Copenhagen. He was a young king inheriting a discredited political class, a humiliated military, and a republican movement that had grown bold enough to openly contest the monarchy's legitimacy for the first time.

The Montilla Era (1907–1919)

The early years of Alejandro I's reign were dominated by Prime Minister Rodrigo Montilla y Vega, a veteran Liberal who had spent the war years in opposition. Montilla was not a reformer by temperament — he was a stabiliser, a pragmatist who understood that Spain needed a decade of quiet more than it needed transformation. His government pursued modest military reform, negotiated limited autonomy arrangements with Catalan regionalists in exchange for parliamentary support, and invested cautiously in infrastructure and industrial development.

Montilla's Spain remained formally neutral during the Great European War's final convulsions and watched the formation of the UER in 1904 with deep unease. Under Holtz, who consolidated power within the UER around 1916, the Union became something more alarming than merely a communist state: it was actively dismantling national identities, suppressing religious practice, purging dissidents, and constructing a totalising ideological system across the Pyrenees. For Montilla's government, the UER was an argument for stability and caution. For the nationalist right coalescing in the margins of Spanish politics, it was an argument for something more drastic.

Montilla governed until approximately 1919, when shifting political winds made his brand of cautious liberalism increasingly untenable. He stepped down and died in the mid-1920s. The nationalist right would spend the following decade retroactively blaming him for being too soft, too willing to accommodate regionalists, and too slow to prepare Spain for the dangers gathering on its borders.

The Covarrubias Bridge (1919–1924)

Montilla was succeeded by General Esteban Covarrubias, a military figure who had built political credibility through colonial administration and was broadly acceptable to the increasingly powerful nationalist tendency within the old Conservative establishment. Covarrubias was not yet the full nationalist ideologue that his successors would be — he worked within the Cortes rather than seeking to circumvent it — but he moved Spain's political centre of gravity decisively rightward. His government was openly hostile to labour organising, deeply suspicious of Catalan autonomy, and unambiguous about the threat posed by the UER.

Covarrubias represented the transitional moment when the Nationalist tendency moved from being a faction within the Conservative party to being the dominant force within it. By the end of his tenure, the old Conservative Monarchist establishment was rapidly losing its organisational independence, its leading figures being absorbed or marginalised by a movement that shared their monarchism but replaced their cautious constitutionalism with something harder.

Parliamentary Instability and Nationalist Consolidation (1924–1932)

Covarrubias's departure was followed by roughly eight years of parliamentary instability. No single party or coalition commanded a durable majority. Liberal Monarchists, their political identity increasingly incoherent, fragmented into reformist and nationalist wings. Republicans made gains in urban constituencies. Regionalists extracted concessions. The Cortes cycled through governments without producing leadership adequate to the moment.

The late 1920s added a new element of ideological complexity. Holtz was overthrown by a military coup within the UER around 1928, having attempted to purge the very army that ultimately removed him. The post-Holtz UER underwent a genuine constitutional crisis — three factions (Restorationists, Reformed Communalists, and Holtzian Loyalists) competed for the Union's future — before emerging reformed: genuine elections, civil liberties, restored co-official status for French and German, religious freedom nominally guaranteed. The UER that emerged from the Holtz crisis was considerably less frightening than the UER of Holtz's peak, but the Spanish right had spent over a decade constructing an ideological architecture around the image of the Holtzian monster, and was not inclined to revise that architecture based on the post-Holtz settlement.

Meanwhile, in Prussia, the Revanchist Nationalist Party was building toward electoral dominance, drawing on grievances about the formerly German territories absorbed by the UER and a broad conviction that the post-Great European War settlement had been unjust to German-speaking peoples. The elderly Prussian king died around 1930; his more aggressive son took the throne. The international situation was moving toward confrontation.

Ramón Calvo y Serrano and the Road to War (1932–1938)

The parliamentary instability of the late 1920s produced, in approximately 1930–1932, a consolidated Nationalist majority capable of governing. Its leader was Ramón Calvo y Serrano, a civilian ideologue who had built the Nationalist movement's organisational apparatus over the preceding decade. Calvo y Serrano was not a general trading on military prestige; he was a politician who had persuaded people, written manifestos, built coalitions, and won elections. That made him both more capable and more culpable than his predecessors.

The Prussian Alignment

Calvo y Serrano's government and Alejandro I shared a genuine conviction — not merely a political calculation — that the UER represented an existential civilisational threat and that the Prussian bloc offered the only credible counterweight. The alignment was not imposed on an unwilling monarchy by a nationalist parliament, nor was it a royal initiative pursued against parliamentary resistance. King and Prime Minister arrived at the same conclusion through the same logic and pursued it together.

When Prussia formally demanded referenda in formerly German UER territories in 1931 and the UER refused, Spain's alignment with the Prussian position was explicit and immediate. When Prussia invaded in 1932 and the UER declared war, Spain made its choice. The Corsican Bourbon government — hostile to the UER throughout — was always in the Prussian column; Spain joined them.

The Mediterranean War (1933–1938)

Spain's formal entry into active belligerence came in 1933–1934, when it declared the Mediterranean closed to Allied shipping and began attacking Allied vessels, including American ships. The United States Congress declared war on Spain in early 1934. For the second time in thirty years, Spain and the United States were at war — this time as part of a global conflict rather than a colonial dispute.

The early years of Spanish belligerence were not disastrous. Spain controlled significant Mediterranean coastline and could threaten Allied supply lines to the UER's southern front. But the military situation shifted decisively in 1937. The Prussian democratic revolution — mass protests, parliamentary collapse, the king's abdication, and the proclamation of the German Republic in early 1938 — left Spain and Austria-Hungary exposed without their principal ally. Austria-Hungary negotiated a separate armistice almost immediately. Spain followed in mid-1938.

The terms were punishing. Spain renounced all remaining colonial pretensions — a formal acknowledgment that the Philippines, lost to revolution during the war, would not be recovered. Spain paid reparations to the families of Allied sailors killed by Spanish naval action. Spain accepted membership in the newly formed Global Peace Council as a regular member, without the founding status accorded to the major Allied powers. Spain's planned hosting of the 1938 World Olympic Games in Madrid was cancelled outright, a casualty of the war that consumed the world.

Ramón Calvo y Serrano resigned as Prime Minister shortly after the armistice was signed. Alejandro I — who had reigned for thirty-one years, had personally sat at the Copenhagen table as a young crown prince, and had governed as a genuine partner in the decision that brought Spain into the Global War — remained on the throne. The future of the Spanish monarchy, and of Spain itself, was among the most consequential unresolved questions of the postwar world.
