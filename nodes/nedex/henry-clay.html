Henry Clay
6th President of the United States (1821-1827)

Henry Clay (1777–1852) was the sixth President of the United States, serving a single six-year term from 1821 to 1827. The founder and first president of the Democrat Party, Clay was one of the most consequential figures of the early republic — a Kentucky lawyer and long-serving Speaker of the House who had been the driving force behind the Western faction of the Democratic-Republican Party before leading it into independence as the Democrats. His presidency is remembered as the administration that built Franklin — laying the cornerstone of Washington House, establishing the Federal Bureau of Improvements, chartering the University of the United States, and delivering to his successor a functioning capital city on the banks of the Ohio River.

Clay was a man of paradoxes. He personally opposed slavery yet led a party deeply divided on the question. He believed in states' rights yet championed the largest federal infrastructure project in the republic's history. He was a Westerner who built the nation's capital, a planter who would eventually free his slaves, and a politician whose greatest achievement was not a law or a treaty but a city. He is buried in Lexington, Kentucky, but his most enduring monument is Franklin itself — every road, building, and institution of the capital bears some trace of his six years in office.

Early life and career

Born in Virginia in 1777 and raised in Kentucky, Clay trained as a lawyer and entered politics young, serving in the Kentucky legislature before moving to the U.S. Senate and then the House of Representatives. He became Speaker of the House in 1811 — one of the most powerful positions in the federal government — and held it with considerable distinction through the War of 1812 and its aftermath. He was a leading voice for Western development, internal improvements, and the expansion of American commerce, and his belief that a strong national infrastructure was essential to the republic's future made him a natural leader of the Western Democratic-Republican faction.

His most important pre-presidential act was his role in the Grand Compromise of 1817. As Speaker of the House, Clay used his influence to ensure that Louisville remained within Kentucky rather than becoming part of the new federal district, while simultaneously championing the Western faction's demand for a new capital on the Ohio River. His fingerprints were on the compromise from the beginning.

Presidency

Election of 1820

When William Crawford announced he would attempt seek reelection, the Democratic-Republican Party formally split. Clay led the Western faction — now calling itself the Democrat Party — into the 1820 election against the Southern Republicans, who ultimately rejected Crawford's bid for a second term, and nominated James Monroe, and the Federalists under John Cotton Smith of Connecticut. Clay won decisively with 147 electoral votes to Monroe's 74 and Smith's 53, carrying most of the West and making significant inroads in the mid-Atlantic states including New York.

Clay became the first president elected to the new six-year term established by the Grand Compromise of 1817, and the first to be inaugurated under the new constitutional framework. He also became the first Democratic president — the founder of a party that would go on to produce more presidents than any other.

Building Franklin

Clay's presidency was defined above all by the construction of Franklin. He made the completion of the new capital a personal priority, making regular trips to the fledgling city throughout his term and attending the groundbreaking of every major federal building under construction.

His first trip, in July 1821, allowed him to be present at the laying of the cornerstone of Washington House — the future home of the American presidency. It was Clay's idea to name the executive buildings after their first officeholders: Washington House for the presidential mansion, the Jefferson Building for the Department of the Exterior, the Hamilton Building for the Treasury, and the Knox Building for the Department of War.

By the time Clay left office in 1827, Franklin was ready to receive the federal government. The roads had been laid out, private lots sold, and the major federal buildings were complete or nearly so. His successor Alexander Hamilton, Jr. became the first president inaugurated in the new capital

The Federal Bureau of Improvements

Clay's most lasting domestic achievement beyond Franklin itself was the establishment of the Federal Bureau of Improvements in April 1821 — the agency charged with building and maintaining the national road and rail network. Clay signed the FBI Act into law on April 22, 1821, appointing Josiah Colfax as its first director. The agency was given authority over the existing National Road between Cumberland and Wheeling, and Colfax immediately announced its extension to Franklin — completed in time for the capital's opening in 1827.

By the end of Clay's administration, the FBI had applied for congressional approval to create the Second and Third National Roads, extending from Cumberland to Boston and from Cumberland to Charleston. The FBI would become one of the most enduring institutions of the American republic — eventually evolving into the infrastructure arm of the federal government and surviving every subsequent attempt to reduce or dismantle it

The University of the United States

Clay also championed the creation of the University of the United States, which received its official congressional charter in 1825. Though it did not open its doors to students until 1828 — after the government's move to Franklin — the UUS was Clay's vision of a federal institution of higher learning that would educate the republic's future leaders and anchor the cultural life of the new capital. Over the following half-century it expanded substantially northeast of Congress Hall, eventually becoming one of the country's premier institutions and anchoring the National Arts and Sciences District in Franklin.

Western expansion and territorial ogranization

No new states were added to the Union under Clay, but his administration made significant progress in organizing the western territories for eventual statehood. In 1823, the Arkansas, Kanasaw, and Iowa Territories were established with territorial governors and legislatures, laying the groundwork for statehood in the following decades.

Clay also encouraged the movement, beginning in Tennessee and spreading to several other states, of allocating electoral votes by congressional district rather than winner-takes-all — an early step toward the electoral reforms that would eventually be constitutionalized in the Fourth Amendment (1911).

Slavery

Clay personally opposed slavery — he would emancipate all of his own slaves by the 1840s — but his party was deeply divided on the question. The Democrat Party would neither endorse slavery as enthusiastically as the Republicans nor pursue abolition as consistently as the Federalists. In 1824, Massachusetts congressmen proposed the complete abolition of slavery in all federal territories. The bill failed badly in the House, but the fact that several Democrats voted for it signaled the growing fracture within the party that would deepen with every passing decade.

The 1826 Election

Clay supported his Vice President, William Jones, as the Democratic candidate for the 1826 election, but Jones was defeated at the party convention by Ohio Senator Thomas Worthington, who was seen as a fresher Western voice untainted by association with the outgoing administration. The Republicans nominated John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, and the Federalists ran Alexander Hamilton, Jr., of New York. Surprising no one, each candidate carried their respective "heartlands," (Federalists in the Northeast, Republicans in the Southeast, and Democrats in the West), resulting in no candidate with the 138 electoral votes that would have been required to win. Hamilton received 92 votes, Calhoun had 93, and Worthington had 89. For the second time in the nation’s history, this triggered the clause in Article II of the Constitution, which required the House of Representatives to pick the new president. Under this provision, each state delegation would have a single vote. Calhoun quickly tried to reach out to Western state delegations to vote for him since their candidate had the fewest electoral votes, and Calhoun had one more electoral vote than Hamilton. However, Worthington put his support behind Hamilton, and the House overwhelmingly voted to make the Federalist candidate the 7th President of the United States.

Legacy

Henry Clay left office in March 1827 with Franklin ready to receive the government and the republic on a more stable footing than he had found it. His presidency is remembered with genuine warmth in the American historical tradition — not as a transformative moral leader, but as the builder who turned the promise of the Grand Compromise of 1817 into a physical reality. He built the city, built the roads, built the university, and built the institutional infrastructure of a westward-looking republic.

He returned to Kentucky after leaving office and remained an active voice in Democratic politics through the 1830s and 1840s, eventually emancipating his slaves and becoming one of the elder statesmen of the party's antislavery wing. He died in June 1852.
