Fourth Amendment to the Constitution of 1861
The presidential runoff amendment · Passed 1911 · In force 1917
Contents

The Fourth Amendment to the Constitution of 1861, passed by Congress in 1911 and first applied in 1917, created the modern presidential runoff system and shortened the transition between election and inauguration. Unusually among American amendments, it never acquired a settled popular name; it is known simply as the Fourth Amendment, or informally as the runoff amendment.

Background

The amendment was born of the arrival of five-party politics. As the election season of 1910 opened with both the Jeffersonian Constitutional Alliance and the Social Progressive Alliance announcing candidates for Washington House, reformers warned that the electoral college's majority requirement — patched but not cured by the Moore Proposal — invited perpetual contingent elections in a five-way field. A second grievance joined the first: the four-month lame-duck period between November and a March inauguration, sensible in the eighteenth century, seemed absurd in the twentieth, and President Cornelius Roosevelt II lent his voice to shortening it. Progressives in several parties also pressed to make Election Day a national holiday, building on state "voting break" laws already adopted in Massachusetts, New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, and the District of Manhattan.

Provisions

The amendment provided that if no ticket won a majority, the top two candidates would proceed to a national runoff election held one month after the general election — abolishing the contingent election entirely. It further provided that the new president would take office ten weeks after the election: mid-January in an ordinary year, mid-February following a runoff. The proposal to designate Election Day a national holiday failed in the House and was dropped from the final text.

Ratification

Congress passed the amendment in 1911, in the opening months of the Hannah administration, and ratification was completed in time to govern the next presidential cycle. The election of 1916 was the first conducted under its rules, and J. P. Roosevelt Jr. became the first president inaugurated in January, in 1917.

Legacy

No amendment has shaped modern American politics more. The runoff entrenched the multi-party system by making every first round a free vote and every runoff a coalition negotiation — the arena of bloc endorsements, party conventions on runoff behavior, and the electoral arithmetic that has decided every close presidency since, down to the Green Party's 62–38 runoff victory in 2024. Its calendar provisions proved equally durable: the January inauguration and the ten-week transition remain the fixed rhythm of American political life.
