Robert Lee
12th President of the United States (1851-1856)

Robert Lee (1807–1856) was the twelfth President of the United States, serving from March 1851 until his death on January 6, 1856. A Virginia lawyer and longtime Republican senator who had served as vice president under Giles Brandon, Lee came to office at one of the most fraught moments in the republic's history — the slavery question had reached a new pitch of intensity following the Runaway Slave Act, and the country was fracturing along sectional lines that no amount of compromise seemed able to heal. He was shot by a radical abolitionist in New York City on November 20, 1855, and died six weeks later from his wounds. He was the second president to be assassinated, and his death placed his vice president James Hawthorne in Washington House — setting in motion the chain of events that would lead the republic to civil war.

Lee was not a villain. He was a Virginia slaveholder who genuinely hoped to keep the presidency above the sectional fray and find a lasting compromise that would hold the republic together. He failed — but the failure was less of character than of circumstance. The forces pulling the republic apart were beyond any one man's ability to contain, and the tools available to him — prosecution, negotiation, infrastructure investment — were inadequate to the scale of the crisis. His assassination cut short whatever remaining chance he had to prevent the worst.

Early life and career

Born in Westmoreland County, Virginia, on January 19, 1807, Lee trained as a lawyer and entered politics as a young man. He married Mary Curtis in 1829 and settled into the life of a Virginia planter-politician. He was elected to the U.S. Senate from Virginia in 1831, where he quickly established himself as a capable and moderate Republican voice — defending Southern interests on slavery while avoiding the most incendiary rhetoric of his colleagues. He served his first Senate term from 1831 to 1836, during which time he came to the attention of President John C. Calhoun and Vice President Giles Brandon.

When Giles Brandon assumed the presidency after Calhoun's assassination in 1836, he appointed Lee as his vice president under the 13th Amendment mechanism — choosing a fellow Virginian and Republican who could bring credibility and Southern support to a presidency that had assumed office under difficult circumstances. Lee served as vice president from 1836 to 1839, gaining national prominence and the experience of executive governance.

After leaving the vice presidency, Lee returned to the Senate in 1841, serving a second term from 1841 to 1851. He gave a strong challenge to William Rutherford at the 1844 Republican convention before withdrawing; by 1850 he was the party's clear presidential choice.

The Election of 1850

Lee won the 1850 Presidential Election with 141 electoral votes, defeating Democrat Hiram Calloway (132 electoral votes), Federalist Jacob North (79), and Liberty Party candidate Tim Goodman (59). The Democratic split between Calloway and Goodman was decisive — Calloway would almost certainly have won without Goodman in the race. Lee took office in March 1851 aware that he had won more because of his opponents' fracture than because of any broad national mandate.

Presidency

The Runaway Slave Act and the courts

The dominant issue of Lee's presidency was the legal battle over the Runaway Slave Act. In February 1851, barely weeks after Lee's inauguration, the Supreme Court heard a suit filed by Massachusetts and Rhode Island challenging the law's constitutionality. The ruling came on June 3, 1851 — a 4–3 decision upholding the Act. The Republican South rejoiced; the Federalist Northeast mourned and then mobilized.

Massachusetts Governor Upton Ellsworth responded by passing a state law forbidding Massachusetts law enforcement from aiding in slave hunts or the return of escaped slaves — a direct challenge to the federal statute. Lee's Attorney General, Trent Camden, filed suit against the Massachusetts law in September 1851. The Federal District Court in Boston initially ruled in Massachusetts' favor. The government appealed. The First Court of Appeals reversed that ruling on April 19, 1852. Massachusetts vowed to fight to the Supreme Court.

The final judgment came on May 10, 1853. In a 6–1 decision, the court struck down the Massachusetts law as unconstitutional. Governor Ellsworth's response the following day became one of the most quoted lines of the pre-war era: President Lee has received the judgment he wanted. Now he's free to enforce it, as I will not.

Lee was genuinely uncertain what to do. He was unwilling to send the army into New England to force compliance — a step that would have amounted to a declaration of war on his own citizens. Instead, he ordered the Attorney General to prosecute individual state officials throughout New England for non-compliance. The prosecutions produced protests, not compliance. Through the summer of 1853, major demonstrations erupted in Boston, Providence, Hartford, New York City, and Brooklyn. By fall, the most radical voices in Boston had organized the New Sons of Liberty — a movement dedicated to the nationwide abolition of slavery, racial equality, and what some of its members openly called a "Second Revolution."

Domestic achievements

Away from the slavery crisis, Lee pursued an ambitious infrastructure agenda. In the fall of 1851 he announced a major expansion of the Federal Bureau of Improvements and United States Railway Company networks. The Third National Road was to be extended to Savannah and eventually St. Augustine; the Fourth National Road would run from St. Augustine to New Orleans, creating the long-awaited Gulf Coast route. The USRC announced a rail line from Philadelphia to St. Augustine via Richmond, Charleston, and Atlanta — expected to be complete by 1861 — and a line from Franklin to St. Louis, expected to be finished before Lee left office.

These projects generated considerable excitement in the South, which had seen relatively little FBI investment in recent years. Federalist complaints that the Northeast was being neglected fell largely on deaf ears, given the region's already extensive private road and rail networks.

In early 1855, gold was discovered in the Union of the Californias, triggering a rush of fortune seekers. Some voices in Congress suggested the United States ought to "protect" the Californians — a thinly veiled argument for annexation. Lee refused to entertain the idea.

Assassination

On November 20, 1855, as Lee was delivering a speech in New York City, he was shot by a radical abolitionist — not a member of the New Sons of Liberty, though likely influenced by their rhetoric. The wound was not immediately fatal. For six weeks the president lingered as infection and complications slowly worsened. Vice President James Hawthorne took charge of day-to-day operations, though he refused to assume the title of Acting President while Lee still lived — an act of formal loyalty that carried a grim irony, given what his own presidency would bring.

On January 6, 1856, President Robert Lee died of his wounds. He was buried in Virginia. His death was the second presidential assassination in the republic's history. Hawthorne was sworn in within hours.

Legacy

Lee's legacy is one of the most melancholy in American presidential history — a man of genuine moderation and good intentions overwhelmed by forces larger than his presidency could contain. His infrastructure achievements were real and lasting. His handling of the Runaway Slave Act crisis was cautious to a fault — prosecuting individual officials rather than either enforcing the law vigorously or seeking a broader compromise — but it is difficult to see what bolder action would have achieved given the depth of the sectional divide.

What history remembers most about Robert Lee is not what he did but what his death made possible: the accession of James Hawthorne, whose escalating responses to New England resistance drove the republic from crisis to civil war in barely a year. Whether a living Lee could have prevented that outcome is one of the great unanswerable questions of history.
