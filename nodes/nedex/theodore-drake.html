Theodore Drake (1825–1902) was the eighteenth President of the United States, serving a single six-year term from 1881 to 1887. A Iowa lawyer and governor who came to office as the second president from a state west of the Mississippi, Drake won the 1880 Presidential Election on a platform of rolling back the Federalist expansion of federal power — cutting budgets, reducing tariffs, and returning authority to the states. He achieved all of that. The consequences were catastrophic.

Drake's presidency is among the most uniformly condemned in American history. His budget cuts to the United States Railway Commission, the Federal Bureau of Improvements, and the postal and telegraph services produced a labor crisis, an economic depression, and — when a deadly influenza epidemic struck in 1884 — a public health catastrophe that he refused to address at the federal level, citing states' rights. Nearly 400,000 Americans died in the Italian Flu epidemic. A rail disaster that killed over 100 people was traced directly to maintenance cuts his administration had imposed. His own two sons died of the flu. By the time he left office in 1887, his party had been routed at the midterms, congressional Federalists were governing around his vetoes, and his successor inherited one of the worst domestic situations any incoming president had ever faced.

Early life and career

Theodore Drake was born on June 2, 1825, in what was then the Iowa Territory, the third son of a farmer who grew up not far from Cedar Rapids. He became interested in the law as a teenager after a lawyer friend of the family helped his father avoid losing his land — an experience he cited throughout his career as formative. He passed the bar and joined a modest Cedar Rapids practice in the early 1850s.

When the War Between the States broke out, Drake volunteered when Iowa joined the fight in 1858, serving as a junior officer in the Iowa Militia before it was folded into the Free State Army. After the war he returned to his law practice but was persuaded by fellow Democrats to run for the Iowa House of Representatives in 1864, where he won his first election. He served in the state legislature until 1874, when he was elected Governor of Iowa at the age of 49, winning reelection in 1878. As governor he championed farmers and homesteaders, pushed for maximum local control, and chafed at USRC regulations he believed hindered private rail development — positions that put him in direct conflict with the Littlefield Administration and ultimately propelled him to the top of the Democratic ticket in 1880.

The Election of 1880

Drake won the 1880 Presidential Election with 368 of 721 electoral votes — a majority — defeating Federalist Jeremiah Nichols (234) and Republican Christopher Hampton (121). His running mate was Beauford Scott of Pennsylvania. The Democrats also retook Congress decisively, gaining 119 seats in the House and leaving the Federalists in the minority for the first time in six years.

Presidency

Economic agenda and the cuts

Drake's inaugural address promised to end "the era of imperial government." He would return the presidency to its humble constitutional role, end what he called the economic burden imposed by Federalist expansion, and open development to the private sector. "The federal government," he told the crowds at Congress Hall, "was never intended to be all-encompassing and touching the lives of ordinary Americans on a day-to-day basis."

Within a week, Washington House sent Congress a sweeping economic proposal: major cuts to tariffs and freight duties, elimination of the post-war federal income taxes that had been gradually built up since the Brandt Administration, and significant reductions to the budgets of the USRC, the Federal Bureau of Improvements, the Post Office, and the federal civil service. Maintenance of infrastructure was to be shifted primarily to the states; private enterprise was to fill the gaps left by reduced federal services.

Federalists fought the proposals through the summer of 1881 but were outnumbered. Secretary General Kenneth Baxter and Speaker Winston Heart worked to pass the president's agenda, and Drake signed the bills into law on June 30, 1881.

The labor crisis

The unintended consequences were immediate. The Railroad Workers Union and the Postal and Telegraph Workers Alliance saw a surge in membership following the announcement of cuts to the USRC and postal and telegraph services. On July 30, 1881, the leaders of both unions announced a joint nationwide strike. Train service and communications across the country were severely disrupted for most of August. Drake insisted the issue should be handled at the state level and declined federal intervention. The strike was eventually resolved, but the damage to both industries — and to Drake's standing — was substantial.

Western statehood

One of Drake's genuine successes was his pledge to bring the remaining western territories into the Union before he left office. During his term, Washingtonia, Clark, and Montana all achieved statehood, fulfilling a decades-long ambition of western settlement advocates and completing the continental republic's primary expansion.

The old capital and First Capital Park

In October 1883, the Claymore Capital Plantation in Georgetown, Maryland — built on the former site of Washington City, the republic's original capital — announced it was folding, its owners financially ruined by the economic downturn. The property included the stone shell of the former President's House, converted into a plantation manor, and the ruins of the old Capitol Building. The Potomac Preservation Society stepped forward to secure the property for public trust but could not fund the entire site. By 1885 the Roosevelt family of New York had purchased the former presidential mansion with plans to convert it into a hotel. In a compromise, the Roosevelts sold the land containing the old Capitol ruins to the PPS in 1886, laying the groundwork for the establishment of First Capital Park in 1889.

The Columbia River rail disaster (1884)

On May 22, 1884, following spring rains and snowmelt, a section of the 6th National Rail line gave way as a passenger train passed over, sending it into the Columbia River and killing over 100 people. The line had been in need of repair, but federal funding cuts had prevented maintenance, and the state of Oregon was not in a position to fill the gap. Drake told reporters the tragedy was "an unstoppable act of God." The subsequent investigation, published in early 1885, clearly identified the budget cuts as an underlying cause — the maintenance that would have prevented the failure had been eliminated under the austerity measures. The Federalist press pinned the disaster squarely on the Drake Administration.

The Italian Flu epidemic (1884-1885)

The catastrophe that defined Drake's presidency came in the fall of 1884, when a deadly strain of influenza arrived from Europe. Doctors urged the federal government to enact quarantines and safety measures immediately. Drake refused, stating that such powers belonged to the states. By the time states acted, the disease had spread to every state by Christmas 1884. By spring 1885, nearly 400,000 Americans had died in what became known as the Italian Flu epidemic.

Tragedy struck Drake directly. The epidemic claimed the lives of his 21-year-old son Andrew and his 17-year-old son Lewis. His wife Paulette was devastated and remained in deep mourning for the rest of his time in office. Washington House cancelled nearly all celebratory events for the remainder of Drake's term.

Economic depression

The budget cuts, the labor disruption, and the epidemic combined to produce a full economic depression. Workers laid off by cuts to the rail and infrastructure industries were left further in the lurch as breadwinners died of the flu. Drake had hoped to spur private railroad development by removing federal competition, but the result was a wave of speculative "paper railroads" — land purchases by speculators who expected private expansion that never materialized. When those ventures collapsed, small regional banks followed. The Central Union Bank prevented a total financial meltdown, but throughout much of the Midwest, small private banks were essentially wiped out, leaving the CUB and its regional affiliates to absorb their depositors. Labor union membership surged as workers sought protection against conditions that federal retrenchment had made worse.

The congressional reversal

The 1884 midterm elections were a rout. Democrats lost 101 House seats and 9 Senate seats. The Federalists secured 369 of 654 House seats — a clear working majority. When the 49th Congress convened in March 1885, it immediately ousted both Speaker Winston Cross and Secretary General Harold Cleveland. Timothy Richards of Iowa became the new Speaker; Derek Thorne of Pennsylvania became Secretary General. The Federalists now controlled the legislative agenda.

The Drake Administration spent its final two years in a running battle with a Congress determined to reverse its policies. The Federalists passed the Federal Health Bureau Act — establishing a national health agency — over Drake's opposition. Drake refused to appoint a director, leaving the agency in legal limbo; the Supreme Court ruled in June 1886 that a president could not leave a congressionally established position vacant for political purposes, forcing him to appoint his cousin Matthew Drake as director. Drake spent the rest of his term dragging his feet on any actual implementation.

Secretary General Thorne attempted to repeal Drake's tariffs and budget cuts in March 1886. The repeal passed the House but narrowly failed in the Senate, where Democrats still held just enough votes to block it. The Federalists folded the issue — and Democratic obstruction — into the 1886 election campaign.

The Election of 1886

The Democratic convention in Calumet was deeply divided. Drake loyalists pushed to draft Vice President Beauford Scott, but Scott declined. The party ultimately settled on Harrison Ford, the former senator from New York, with former Secretary General Kenneth Baxter as his running mate — a ticket that tried to distance itself from Drake's legacy while offering little clarity on what it would do differently. The Republicans nominated Tennessee Governor Benjamin Conroy with Georgia Senator Adlai Benton. The Federalists rallied behind Leeland Rowling of Pennsylvania — a constitutional scholar and reforming governor — with an energized base eager to reverse the Drake years.

Drake endorsed Ford publicly but his support was a liability more than an asset. Rowling won decisively, and the Federalists gained complete control of both houses of Congress. Drake left office in March 1887 as one of the most unpopular presidents in American history.

Legacy

Theodore Drake is remembered as the cautionary case study of what happens when ideology outstrips governance. His convictions were genuine — he sincerely believed that federal power had grown too large and that states and markets could do better. The evidence of his six years in office suggested otherwise. The budget cuts produced exactly the disasters their opponents had warned about. His refusal to act on the Italian Flu epidemic, citing states' rights as 400,000 Americans died, is the single act for which he is most condemned by historians.

His personal suffering during those years — the deaths of his two sons, his wife's lasting grief, the isolation of a presidency that had become politically toxic — was genuine. History has not found it sufficient to alter its verdict.

He returned to Iowa after leaving office and died there on August 13, 1902, at the age of 77.
