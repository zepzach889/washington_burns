Wilbur Haines
11th President of the United States (1845-1851)

Wilbur Haines (1801–1880) was the eleventh President of the United States, serving a single six-year term from 1845 to 1851. A Kentucky lawyer and longtime senator who had served as Victor Yates's vice president, Haines came to office inheriting a republic already straining under the weight of slavery and an unresolved situation in Mexico. The troubles that would lead the country to civil war a decade later began in earnest during his six years in Washington House.

Haines was not a weak president — he led the United States through the Second Mexican War, secured the independence of the Texas Federation and the Union of the Californias, and negotiated the Boston Accords that finally settled the Oregon boundary dispute. But his domestic legacy is shadowed by the Runaway Slave Act he championed, the fracturing of the Democrat Party at the 1850 convention, and the election of Robert Lee — events that set the republic on a direct course toward the War Between the States.

Early life and career

Wilbur Haines was born in Kentucky on January 6, 1801, and trained as a lawyer before entering politics. He served as a U.S. Senator from Kentucky from 1825 to 1839, becoming one of the Democrat Party's most prominent Western voices. He was a slaveholder himself — a fact that shaped his approach to the slavery question throughout his political career. When Victor Yates chose him as his vice-presidential running mate in 1838, Haines was seen as a natural complement — a Western Democrat with Southern sympathies to balance Yates's Pennsylvania base. He served as vice president from 1839 to 1845 and succeeded Yates as the Democratic presidential nominee in 1844.

The Election of 1844

Haines won the 1844 Presidential Election narrowly, with 164 electoral votes to Republican William Rutherford's 159 and Federalist Victor Lang's 82. The closeness of the result — Haines won a plurality under the 16th Amendment to the Constitution of 1787 but fell well short of a majority — unsettled the country. Southern Republicans called on Rutherford to challenge the results in states that allocated electoral votes by congressional district, but he declined. Haines took office in March 1845 with a thin mandate and a republic already under pressure.

Presidency

The Massachusetts Tariff ruling

Less than two months after Haines was inaugurated, the Supreme Court announced a 4–3 decision siding with the southern states over the Massachusetts Tariff. The Federalists were outraged. Within weeks the Massachusetts legislature passed a revised law — this time charging firms that sold goods made by slave labor or made products incorporating goods produced by slave labor a fee rather than a direct interstate tariff. The court had objected to taxes on imports between states; this new formulation fell outside those objections, at least in the Northern interpretation. New lawsuits were filed immediately. Other Federalist-controlled states quickly followed Massachusetts's lead, and by 1846 every New England state was placing fees on goods produced, directly or indirectly, by slave labor. The legal battle would grind on for years.

The Second Mexican War

The more immediate crisis came from Mexico. The Empire of Mexico had made significant advances against the Texas Federation and Union of the Californias through early 1845, retaking the provinces of Coahuila, Nuevo Leon, and Tamaulipas and threatening to cross the Rio Grande into Texas proper. On August 13, 1845, the Mexican Navy sank the S.S. Bostonian, an American merchant ship attempting to enter the port of Galvez Town in Texas.

The sinking shifted political winds rapidly. On September 2, 1845, Haines went to Congress and asked for a declaration of war on the Empire of Mexico. It passed with only a handful of dissenting votes from representatives from Massachusetts and New Hampshire. The navy was immediately ordered to clear Mexican ships from the Texian coast and raid Veracruz, which was conducted in November 1845.

In January 1846, before American soldiers could arrive in force, the Imperial Army launched its major assault into Texas, crossing the Rio Grande toward San Antonio. The Battle of San Antonio began February 3, 1846 — by which time several hundred American soldiers had arrived, with thousands more marching west from railheads in Arkansas and Louisiana. After three days of fighting, the Imperials were forced to retreat, and over the following month the Texian-American forces pushed them back to the Rio Grande.

In the west, however, the Imperial Army crossed from Sonora into Baja California, threatening to cut off the Californian forces. The American and Texian commanders in the east responded by marching on Mexico City and bombarding Gulf Coast ports. The Battle of Monterrey, fought October 3–6, 1846, was a costly American-Texian victory; momentum to push further south evaporated. The destruction of Veracruz as a functional port and the eastward pressure did have the desired effect — the Empire was forced to withdraw large numbers of men from California.

In March 1847, the Imperial Army attempted to retake Monterrey but was repelled. In April, a cadre of Mexican military officers forced Emperor Raul Guerra to agree to a ceasefire. Peace talks were held once again in New Orleans, and on May 10, 1847, the Second Treaty of New Orleans was signed, formally ending the war and recognizing the independence of the Texas Federation and the Union of the Californias. The United States pledged not to annex either republic. In the aftermath, the same military council that had forced the ceasefire compelled Emperor Raul to adopt a more liberal constitution in 1848.

Texas emerged from the war controlling the former provinces of Texas and Nuevo Mexico. The Texian capital Franklin-on-the-Brazos was renamed Texopolis the following year. The Texas Federation adopted a bicameral legislature — the National Assembly, consisting of a House of Delegates and a Senate — with an independently elected president and shared power between the federal government and four states: Austin, Brazos, Colorado, and Nuevo Mexico. The Union of the Californias established a unicameral Parliament that would elect a Premier. The question of slavery proved contentious in Texas — the state of Austin, dominated by American settlers, refused to abolish the institution, and the Texian constitution left slavery to individual states. Austin subsequently passed a law in 1848 requiring the return of runaway slaves to their owners.

The Boston Accords

With the Mexican question settled, attention turned to the Oregon Country — still a condominium between the United States and the British Empire. President Haines invited delegates from Britain, Texas, and California to Boston in April 1848 to negotiate a final border settlement. The Boston Accords, signed May 11, 1848, established the borders still in use today. The United States received all land south and east of the Columbia River up to the 49th Parallel, at which point the border followed that parallel to the continental divide and southward to the California border. This created Oregon Territory and the British Colony of British Columbia.

Statehood for Jefferson and Minnesota

During the war, both Jefferson and Minnesota — products of earlier territorial compromises — had seen sufficient population growth to petition for statehood. Congress approved both petitions on April 1, 1845, and Haines signed their enabling acts into law on April 4. Jefferson became the 28th state and Minnesota the 29th.

The Runaway Slave Act

In the fall of 1849, Georgia Senator James Hawthorne — considered a rising star in the Republican Party — introduced the Runaway Slave Act, modeled after a law recently passed in Texas's Austin state. The law would require runaway slaves captured in free states to be returned to their masters, allowing slaveholders to pursue runaways across state lines and compelling law enforcement and private citizens to assist in their capture.

Haines, a slaveholder himself, supported the bill but proposed a compromise: any slave brought into a free state by their master would automatically be granted freedom after thirty days on free soil. The Federalists refused to support even the modified version, but it won over enough Democrats, and the law passed in January 1850. It immediately became one of the most divisive pieces of legislation in the republic's history, triggering legal challenges across Federalist-controlled states and accelerating the collapse of Democratic unity on the slavery question.

The Election of 1850 and the Democratic fracture

The 1850 Presidential Election saw the Democrat Party tear itself apart. The Federalists officially declared their support for a federal abolition plan — the end of traditionalist resistance within the party — and nominated former Speaker Jacob North of Pennsylvania with Maine Governor Ulysses Portman as his running mate. The Republicans nominated former Vice President Robert Lee of Virginia with Senator James Hawthorne as his running mate.

At the Democratic convention in Columbus, Ohio, the party's two wings — moderates led by former Speaker Hiram Calloway of Illinois, who wanted to leave slavery as a state-by-state issue, and reformers led by New York Senator Tim Goodman, who wanted a federal sunset on slavery — reached an impasse. When a bare majority voted in favor of supporting the Runaway Slave Act, the northeastern delegates backing Goodman walked out. The convention nominated Calloway for president with Hugo Brandt, the current Speaker of the House from Ohio, as his running mate.

On August 29, Goodman and his supporters announced a breakaway candidacy under the name Liberty Party, nominating Goodman for president with New Jersey Representative Lewis Benton as his running mate. Calloway and his allies dismissed the Liberty Party as a stunt and ignored it. They underestimated the depth of Northern feeling against the Runaway Slave Act.

The result was catastrophic for the Democrats. Lee and Hawthorne won 141 electoral votes. Calloway and Brandt came second with 132. The Federalists won 79 and the Libertarians 59. Loyalist Democrats correctly noted that Calloway would have won without Goodman in the race. Instead, Robert Lee became the 12th President of the United States — and unbeknownst to anyone at the time, the country was less than seven years from civil war.

Legacy

Haines is a president of genuine achievement overshadowed by catastrophic political consequences. He won the Second Mexican War, secured the independence of Texas and California, settled the Oregon boundary, and added two states to the Union. None of it mattered as much as the Runaway Slave Act, which fractured his party, energized the abolitionist movement, and gave James Hawthorne the vice-presidential platform from which he would eventually drive the republic to war.

He returned to Kentucky after leaving office and lived for another three decades, dying on January 24, 1880, at the age of 79 — long enough to see the consequences of the Runaway Slave Act play out in full.
