The Global War (1932–1940) was the largest and most destructive conflict in world history to that point, involving the major powers of Europe, Asia, and the Americas across two interconnected but distinct theatres of war. It is regarded as the defining event of the twentieth century — reshaping the political map of Europe and Asia, accelerating social change across the Allied nations, and producing the institutional framework of the Global Peace Council that has governed international relations ever since.

The war grew from two simultaneous crises in 1932: Prussia's invasion of formerly German territories within the Union of European Republics in Europe, and China's opportunistic attack on Chosŏn and British treaty ports in Asia. Though these conflicts were not formally coordinated, they drew the same powers into opposition and produced a single global conflict that did not end until the Asian ceasefire of late 1940.

The United States entered the war in stages — first declaring war on Spain in early 1934 following Spanish attacks on American shipping, then entering the Pacific theatre under President Leon Palmer in spring 1935. American participation proved decisive in both theatres.

Background

The Union of European Republics

The Union of European Republics (UER) was formed in 1904 from the communist revolutions that swept France and several South German states during and after the Great European War (1899–1904). Under its founder — a French revolutionary leader of moderate temperament — the UER was a communalist state that was ideologically distinctive but diplomatically functional. The founder died around 1914; a protégé period followed before Mathias Holtz seized power around 1916.

Holtz's program was radical and destabilising: a constructed common language was imposed across the UER, national boundaries were dissolved, the capital was relocated to Strasbourg, religious practice was suppressed, and political purges eliminated opponents. The UER withdrew from the International Peace Council and the Olympic Games under Holtz. When Holtz attempted to purge the military in the late 1920s, he was overthrown by a military coup. The post-Holtz constitutional crisis pitted three factions — Restorationists, Reformed Communalists, and Holtzian Loyalists — against one another. The UER survived the crisis in reformed shape: genuine elections, civil liberties, and religious freedom were restored, though Holtzian administrative boundaries remained, and the constructed language was retained for official use alongside restored French and German.

Prussia and the Road to War

Prussia was a constitutional monarchy that had developed a powerful revanchist Nationalist Party through the 1920s. Prussia's core grievance was that Bavaria and the South German states — German-speaking peoples — had been absorbed into the UER, which Prussia regarded as a French-led state with no legitimate claim to govern Germans.

When Prussia's elderly king died around 1930, his more aggressive son took the throne. The Nationalist coalition won parliamentary elections, formed a government, and began demanding referenda in formerly German UER territories — a pretext for territorial expansion. The UER refused. Prussian military buildup began through 1931. The International Peace Council convened but went nowhere. Britain's Conservative government pushed accommodation. Spain aligned explicitly with Prussia. The Corsican Bourbon Government was hostile to the UER throughout, always aligned with the Prussian column in the historical record.

In 1932, Prussia invaded. The UER declared war. A Prussian U-boat's sinking of a British passenger vessel destroyed the Conservative government's position in London; the British People's Party returned to power and brought Britain into the war.

China and the Asian Dimension

Simultaneously, the Zhongxing Dynasty of China — a constitutional monarchy established in 1903, nationalist and expansionist in character — attacked Chosŏn and British treaty ports in East Asia. This was a deliberate opportunistic move, timed to expel Britain from East Asia while the empire was distracted in Europe. Hawaii, recognising China as an existential threat to Pacific trade, immediately opened its ports to Allied forces.

The War

Phase One — American Neutrality and Approach to War (1932–1934)

When war broke out in Europe in 1932, the United States under President Benjamin King maintained formal neutrality while providing material support to the Allied powers. The Preparation Act (1932), passed with Democratic-Federalist-SPA support over fierce JCA opposition, allowed the government to strengthen and modernise the armed forces. The Third North American Conference (1932) — convened as an emergency session — produced the North American Mutual Defense Pact, an "attack on one is attack on all" agreement with deliberately vague wording that set the minimum obligation at monetary and humanitarian aid.

In 1933, Spain declared the Mediterranean a closed sea and attacked Allied-bound shipping, including American vessels. Several American ships were sunk with significant casualties. The memory of the Matanzas War (1905–06) — "Spain again" in the popular press — activated deep anger among a public that still had living veterans of that conflict. Congress declared war on Spain in early 1934 over sustained JCA opposition. The declaration was understood to mean full Allied entry.

Phase Two — American Entry and the Defense of Chosŏn (1934–1936)

President Leon Palmer, who took office in January 1935, addressed Congress in spring 1935 with the argument for entering the Pacific theatre. His case was strategic and forward-looking — China controlling Korea and Pacific trade routes represented a direct threat to American commercial and security interests. He invoked no ideological framing and no historical precedent. The congressional declaration passed by a narrower margin than the Spanish declaration. The JCA's argument that America had been "dragged into Asia" became a standard talking point for years afterward.

The Pacific war's darkest moment came almost immediately. Chinese forces drove the Allied defensive line back to a narrow perimeter around Busan by late 1935. The Defense of Busan through the winter of 1935–36 became the defining image of the war's most desperate period. The Allied counteroffensive began in spring 1936 — two grinding years of pushing Chinese forces northward — and the front stabilised near the original Chosŏn border by 1938.

In Europe, the American war effort focused primarily on the Spanish theatre and the Atlantic, while British and UER forces bore the brunt of the Prussian front.

Phase Three — Turning the Tide (1937–1938)

By late 1937, the military situation had shifted decisively in the Allies' favour in Europe. Mass protests swept Prussian cities — Berlin, Hamburg, and others. The Nationalist parliamentary coalition collapsed. The Prussian king abdicated. The German Republic was proclaimed in early 1938 by a coalition of democratic parties united by opposition to the Nationalist war policy. The new government opened back-channel armistice communications almost immediately.

Austria-Hungary negotiated a separate armistice. Spain negotiated its armistice in mid-1938 — on punishing terms, losing its remaining colonial pretensions and paying reparations to the families of American sailors killed. The formal European armistice came in autumn 1938. President Palmer announced it in a radio address that became one of the most-listened broadcasts in the medium's history.

In the Mediterranean, the Corsican Bourbon Government had fled to Algiers before the armistice. The reigning pretender formally renounced all claims to metropolitan France; his heir became King of a newly recognised Kingdom of Algeria — a French settler state in all but name. The UER was recognised as the government of metropolitan France, extinguishing the Bourbon dynastic claim as a condition of the settlement.

Phase Four — The Geneva Conference and Treaty (1938–1939)

The Geneva Conference opened in late 1938 and ran into 1939. President Palmer attended personally despite JCA constitutional objections. The key disputes and their resolutions:

German reparations — Substantial but calibrated to be payable. Palmer's argument was purely practical: a failed German state on the UER's western border created a security vacuum that benefited nobody. Hard sell to the UER and Britain, who had bled heavily.

UER territorial questions — Formerly German territories were returned to the UER, with regional autonomy provisions that addressed population concerns imperfectly.

Ottoman and Suez — The Suez Canal operating agreement was renegotiated, removing Prussian influence and replacing it with joint Ottoman-Allied oversight including Britain and the North American Union.

German Republic status — Observer status in the new Global Peace Council, with a path to full membership contingent on democratic consolidation and reparations compliance. The UER was deeply uncomfortable with this arrangement; Palmer held firm.

Kingdom of Algeria — Recognised as an independent sovereign state. The Bourbon dynastic claim to France formally extinguished.

The Treaty of Geneva was signed in spring 1939. Palmer signed for the United States in a ceremony broadcast across the North American continent.

Phase Five — Asian Endgame (1939–1940)

The Asian theatre proved harder to resolve. Ceasefire negotiations began in mid-1940, brokered through Russian intermediaries — Russia had remained a genuine neutral throughout the conflict and was trusted by both sides. Shooting stopped in late 1940. The settlement terms were still being negotiated when Palmer left office; President Henry Freeman inherited the unresolved diplomatic process.

The Asian settlement was painful for the Allies:

Chosŏn was restored as a formally independent state, no longer a British client. Britain accepted — its imperial resources were too depleted to insist on restoring the prewar relationship.

British treaty ports (Hong Kong, Shanghai, and others) were not returned to Britain. China kept them. This was the price of a ceasefire without total war — genuinely painful, and a source of ongoing controversy.

Japan had exhausted itself defending its territory but remained sovereign and undefeated. The inconclusiveness of the Asian theatre fed postwar anxiety about China directly.

The JCA's argument that "Palmer and Freeman lost Asia" became a standard talking point throughout the Nolan Administration (1947–1953) and Priest Administration (1953–1959).

Domestic Effects on the United States

The Global War transformed American society in ways that outlasted the conflict itself. Mass mobilisation mixed citizens across class, race, and region at unprecedented scale, changing the social texture of the country. Women entered the workforce in numbers and roles never seen before, accelerating shifts in labour and social norms that had been building for decades. The labour movement emerged institutionally stronger — union no-strike pledges during the war were exchanged for guaranteed collective bargaining rights. The United States Railway Commission was vindicated completely and permanently — its essential role in wartime logistics settled the question of its institutional value for all time.

The seeds of the postwar gay rights movement were planted in the military experience; mass mobilisation brought together people across all civilian boundaries, planting ideas that would not surface publicly for years but would define the social politics of the 1950s and beyond. SPA-adjacent administrators were heavily represented in the wartime federal bureaucracy — the JCA filed this away and used it as ammunition in the postwar backlash.

Postwar Framework

The Global Peace Council

The Global Peace Council (GPC) was established at Geneva as the permanent successor to the International Peace Council system. Headquartered in Geneva, it maintained a permanent secretariat and held regular sessions. Its founding Executive Board comprised ten members: the United States, Britain, the UER, Russia, China, Canada, Italy, Japan, the Ottoman Empire, and Mexico. China's inclusion generated genuine discomfort among the Allied powers, but exclusion would have rendered the GPC irrelevant in Asia. The German Republic joined as an observer with a path to full membership; the Kingdom of Algeria, the Austria-Hungary successor states, and Spain joined as regular members.

The Fourth North American Conference

The Fourth North American Conference, held in San Francisco in 1940, produced two landmark agreements: the Pan-American Passport — a common passport control zone covering the USA, Canada, Texas, California, and Mexico, allowing free movement of citizens across member borders — and the formal confirmation of Hawaii as a full North American member, preserving the Hawaiian monarchy and sovereignty while securing the economic and security integration the war had demonstrated was necessary.

Legacy

The Global War is regarded as the central event of the twentieth century — the conflict that determined the shape of the postwar world, institutionalised the North American alliance system, and produced the Global Peace Council as the framework for international order. Its domestic legacy was equally profound: the social changes it accelerated took decades to fully manifest, but the postwar gay rights movement, the permanent vindication of organised labour, and the transformation of women's roles in public life all trace their roots to the war years.

The war also defined the fault lines of postwar American politics. The JCA's opposition to American entry, its argument that the administration "lost Asia," and its deep suspicion of the wartime expansion of federal authority set the agenda for the Nolan Administration (1947–1953) and Priest Administration (1953–1959) — the hard-right postwar backlash that would itself produce the reaction that brought Georgina Lincoln to the presidency in 1959.
