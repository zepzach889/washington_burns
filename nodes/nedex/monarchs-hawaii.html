Monarchs of Hawaiʻi
Kamehameha Dynasty · From unification (c. 1795) to the present · Reference list with biographical notes

The following is a list of the sovereigns of the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi from its unification under Kamehameha I to the present day. All monarchs have been members of the Kamehameha Dynasty. The regnal name Kamehameha was used by the first seven sovereigns; Queen Kaʻahulani, the eighth sovereign and the first queen regnant, chose a distinct Hawaiian name upon her accession in 1929, establishing a new naming convention for the dynasty. The monarchs following Kaʻahulani have continued that convention. ✦ denotes a monarch who died in office.

Biographical Notes

Kamehameha I (r. 1795–1819)

Kamehameha I (born c. 1758 — died May 8, 1819) unified the Hawaiian Islands through a combination of military conquest and diplomacy, completing the consolidation of the archipelago under a single sovereign by 1810. He established the institutional foundations of the Hawaiian monarchy, managing early Western contact with deliberate care and selectively adopting Western military technology while maintaining Hawaiian political authority. He died at his home in Kailua-Kona in 1819.

Kamehameha II (r. 1819–1824)

Kamehameha II (Liholiho; born 1797 — died July 14, 1824) ascended at the death of his father and almost immediately oversaw the abolition of the kapu system — the traditional religious and social code governing Hawaiian life — an act of dramatic internal transformation undertaken without outside compulsion. American Protestant missionaries arrived in 1820 during his reign. He died in London at the age of 27, alongside his consort Kamāmalu, of illness contracted during a diplomatic visit to the court of George IV.

Kamehameha III (r. 1825–1854)

Kamehameha III (Kauikeaouli; born March 17, 1814 — died December 15, 1854) reigned for nearly thirty years, the longest reign in the dynasty's history to that point. He promulgated the kingdom's first formal constitution in 1840, establishing a legislature and codifying the rights of Hawaiian subjects. The Great Māhele land reform of 1848 restructured land tenure across the islands. His reign saw the establishment of Hawaiʻi's characteristic triangular pattern of foreign commercial relationships — American, Californian, and British interests present in roughly equal measure, none dominant enough to dictate political terms.

Kamehameha IV (r. 1855–1863)

Kamehameha IV (Alexander Liholiho; born February 9, 1834 — died November 30, 1863) presided over a period of visible commercial growth as trans-Pacific traffic expanded and Honolulu's role as a transit port deepened. He cultivated British relationships as a diplomatic counterweight and founded the Queen's Medical Center alongside his consort Queen Emma in response to the continuing decline of the native Hawaiian population. He died at the age of 29.

Kamehameha V (r. 1863–1882)

Kamehameha V (Lot Kapuāiwa; born December 11, 1830 — died August 14, 1882) was the most consequential monarch since the founder. Strong-willed and conservative in temperament, he promulgated a revised constitution in 1868 establishing the bicameral legislature that endures to the present — a settlement that extended formal representation to the kingdom's foreign landowning community through a property-qualified upper house while preserving Hawaiian political authority. His reign coincided with the completion of the North American transcontinental rail networks in the 1870s and early 1880s, transforming Hawaiʻi's strategic and commercial position as three continental economies simultaneously sought a trans-Pacific waypoint. He died at 51 having secured both the political settlement and the dynastic succession that his predecessors had left uncertain.

Kamehameha VI (r. 1882–1900)

Kamehameha VI (born June 3, 1856 — died February 21, 1900) reigned for eighteen years, overseeing Honolulu's transformation into a major Pacific city. He invested substantially in the port infrastructure — dry docks, bonded warehouses, banking institutions, and a reliable commercial legal framework — that made the kingdom indispensable to trans-Pacific trade. The Japanese trading community in Honolulu grew substantially during his reign, and he cultivated those relationships with personal as well as commercial interest. He died at the age of 43.

Kamehameha VII (r. 1900–1929)

Kamehameha VII (born March 7, 1872 — died September 18, 1929) reigned for nearly thirty years, the longest reign in the dynasty's history. His reign opened with the Japanese republican revolution of 1904, which he navigated pragmatically — officially recognizing the new republic while offering dignified hospitality to displaced aristocratic and military families who sought refuge in Honolulu. He expanded the kingdom's formal diplomatic presence through the International Peace Council framework from 1907 onward. The defining domestic act of his reign was the constitutional succession amendment of 1925, which established primogeniture regardless of sex, designating his eldest daughter as heir to the throne over the objections of conservative members of the upper house. He died at the age of 57.

Queen Kaʻahulani (r. 1929–1983)

Queen Kaʻahulani (born September 12, 1905 — died 1983) ascended to the throne at the age of 24 following the death of her father Kamehameha VII. She was the first woman to reign in the kingdom's history and the first sovereign to take a regnal name outside the Kamehameha numeral sequence — a deliberate gesture understood as an assertion of Hawaiian identity and dynastic confidence. Her early reign was defined by the global crisis of the Global War, during which she opened Hawaiian ports to Allied forces in response to Chinese expansionism in the Pacific. The postwar integration of Hawaiʻi into the North American Union as a full member — with the monarchy and sovereignty preserved — was the defining constitutional achievement of her reign. She reigned for 54 years in total, dying in 1983 at the age of 77.
