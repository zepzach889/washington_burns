The Argentine Republic (Spanish: República Argentina), commonly known as Argentina, is a federal republic occupying the southern portion of South America, bordered by the Empire of Brazil to the northeast, Uruguay to the east, and Bolivia and Paraguay to the north, with the Andes forming its western border with Chile. Its capital and largest city is Buenos Aires.

Argentina is one of the largest and most naturally wealthy nations in the Western Hemisphere, its vast Pampas grasslands among the most productive agricultural territories on earth. Its path to national unity was long and turbulent, marked by decades of conflict between centralizing and federalist political factions, and its emergence as a consolidated republic in the 1860s was followed almost immediately by a costly diplomatic and military reverse that shaped its relationship with its neighbors for generations. Argentina maintained neutrality during the Global War (1932–1939) alongside most of Latin America, and is a member of the Global Peace Council.

Independence and the Age of Caudillos

Argentina declared independence from Spain in 1816, but the decades that followed were among the most turbulent in the hemisphere. The fundamental division between Unitarians — centred on Buenos Aires and favouring a strong central government — and Federalists — rooted in the interior provinces and defending regional autonomy — produced recurring civil conflict that prevented stable national governance for nearly half a century.

The most powerful figure of this era was Juan Manuel de Rosas, the Federalist strongman who dominated Buenos Aires and much of Argentina from 1829 until his defeat and exile in 1852. Rosas governed through a combination of patronage and state violence, suppressing Unitarian opposition while projecting Argentine power into the Río de la Plata basin through intervention in Uruguayan affairs and confrontation with Brazil. His defeat at the Battle of Caseros in 1852 — brought about by a coalition of Brazilian forces, Uruguayan Colorados, and dissident Argentine Federalists under General Justo José de Urquiza — ended his regime and opened the way for a new constitutional order.

National Consolidation

The constitution drafted in 1853 established the framework of a federal republic, but Buenos Aires refused to accept its terms and seceded from the Argentine Confederation for six years. The eventual reconciliation culminated in the Battle of Pavón in 1861, after which General Bartolomé Mitre emerged as the dominant figure of Argentine politics. Mitre was elected the first president of a genuinely unified Argentina in 1862, finally bringing Buenos Aires and the interior provinces under a single constitutional government after five decades of fragmentation.

Mitre's presidency was devoted to building the institutions that a modern state required — a national judiciary, a unified currency, telegraph and railroad infrastructure, and a public education system. The task was enormous and the opposition persistent, with Federalist sentiment in the interior provinces never fully reconciled to Buenos Aires's dominance.

The War of the Río de la Plata and its Aftermath

The great crisis of Argentina's early national period came almost immediately after unification, when the longstanding instability of Uruguay drew Argentina into direct confrontation with the Empire of Brazil. The Uruguayan civil war between the Colorado and Blanco factions had long served as a proxy battleground for Argentine and Brazilian interests, with Argentina broadly sympathetic to the Blancos and Brazil backing the Colorados. As Brazilian intervention intensified in the early 1860s, Mitre's government found itself committed to a contest it was poorly positioned to win.

Argentina's national institutions were barely three years old when the conflict escalated. The country's military was undertrained and underequipped relative to its opponent. Internal opposition — Federalist provinces sceptical of a Buenos Aires-led war, the continued semi-autonomous authority of Urquiza in Entre Ríos and Corrientes — complicated mobilization. Paraguay's decision to align diplomatically with Brazil, trading logistical cooperation for sovereignty guarantees, further tipped the regional balance against Buenos Aires.

The outcome was a diplomatic and military reverse that left Uruguay in the Brazilian orbit and Argentina's regional ambitions checked. The defeat severely damaged Mitre's domestic standing and reinvigorated Federalist opposition in the interior. Recovery came gradually. The natural wealth of the Pampas, the expansion of the railroad network, and the great wave of European immigration that transformed Argentina's population and economy through the 1870s and 1880s eventually reasserted themselves. By the final decade of the nineteenth century Argentina had stabilised, its institutions consolidated, its agricultural exports fuelling rapid economic growth, and its population expanding at a pace that made it one of the fastest-growing economies in the hemisphere.

The rivalry with Brazil persisted as a defining feature of Argentine foreign policy — a cold competition for hemispheric influence that shaped Buenos Aires's approach to every regional question. Argentina never fully accepted the outcome of the Río de la Plata contest, and the memory of that reverse informed its consistent effort to balance Brazilian power through cultivating relationships with other South American states and maintaining a military establishment capable of deterring further Brazilian encroachment.

The Global War and Latin American Neutrality

When the Global War broke out in 1932 — with Prussia invading formerly German territories of the Union of European Republics in Europe and China attacking Chosŏn and the British treaty ports in Asia — Argentina, like most of Latin America, remained neutral. The conflict's principal theaters were distant, the immediate strategic stakes for South American nations were limited, and the memory of the economic disruptions produced by the Great European War (1899–1904) gave Argentine governments ample reason to stay out.

Argentine neutrality was genuine rather than merely formal. The country maintained commercial relationships with both sides of the conflict, as economic necessity and geographic distance from the fighting made strict adherence to any belligerent's trade preferences impractical. The war's end — with the European settlement in the Treaty of Geneva (1939) and the Asian ceasefire and settlement in 1940–1941 — left Argentina's institutions and economy intact, and the country was well-positioned to participate in the postwar international order from a position of relative strength.

The Postwar Period

Argentina participated in the founding of the Global Peace Council as a regular member, joining the international framework that replaced the prewar International Peace Council system. The GPC's founding structure reflected the wartime alliance that had defeated Prussia and contained China; Argentina, as a neutral, occupied a position in the new order that was formally equal but practically peripheral to the inner circle of the Executive Board's founding ten members.

The postwar decades continued to be defined by the fundamental tension in Argentine foreign policy between hemispheric ambition and the persistent reality of Brazilian regional dominance. Argentina's engagement with the GPC framework was active but characteristically independent — Buenos Aires had little interest in anchoring itself to any great power bloc, preferring the leverage that genuine non-alignment within the GPC system could provide.

Government and Politics

Argentina operates as a federal presidential republic under the constitution of 1853, as amended. The President serves as both head of state and head of government for a fixed term, governing alongside a bicameral National Congress comprising a Senate and a Chamber of Deputies. Provincial governments retain significant autonomy — a constitutional guarantee that reflects the Federalist tradition and that no Buenos Aires-centred government has been able to fully override.

The principal political division of the late nineteenth century ran between the liberal modernising tendency associated with the Generation of 1880 — committed to European immigration, export-led agricultural growth, and secular public education — and the persistent Federalist and conservative currents of the interior. By the early twentieth century, the Radical Civic Union had emerged as a significant opposition movement challenging the oligarchic character of the Generation of 1880's political dominance. The subsequent development of Argentine political life — through the upheavals of the early twentieth century, the experience of neutrality in the Global War, and the postwar decades — reflects the ongoing contest between these traditions and the new political forces generated by Argentina's rapid economic and social transformation.
