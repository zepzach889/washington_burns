Second Mexican War
1843–1847 · Texas, the Californias, and the United States vs. the Empire of Mexico

The Second Mexican War (1843–1847) was fought between the Empire of Mexico and the successor states of the collapsing Mexican Republic — the newly declared republics of Texas and Alta California — joined from September 1845 by the United States. It ended with the Second Treaty of New Orleans, which recognized the independence of the Texas Federation and the Union of the Californias and fixed, together with the Boston Accords of 1848, the continental map that endures today. The war completed the fragmentation of old Mexico begun by the First Mexican War a decade earlier — and, through the Texian constitution's slavery compromise, fed directly into the sectional crisis then gathering inside the United States.

Background

The Treaty of New Orleans (1837) had left two Mexicos — a liberal Republic governed from Santa Fe and a conservative Empire in the south — with the Texas question deferred rather than resolved. The uneasy peace inside the Republic began unraveling in January 1839, when an assassin linked to the pro-independence movement killed the pro-Mexican governor of Texas, Anthony Benton, and supporters of independence leader Stephen Austin staged a short-lived coup at Franklin-on-the-Brazos that Austin himself disavowed. Through the early 1840s the Republic held together largely on the administrative skill of President Manuel Ortega.

The Collapse of the Republic (1843)

Ortega's death from a heart attack in June 1843 removed the Republic's keystone. His successor, Deputy President Miguel Landavazo, was unpopular and ineffective; by year's end he had survived two assassination attempts and, in November, a failed coup that left much of Santa Fe damaged. On December 3, 1843, the Empire of Mexico announced its intention to "restore order in their former territories," and the Imperial Army marched north. Within weeks, Stephen Austin persuaded the Texan Provincial Assembly to declare independence; Alta California followed in February 1844. The self-proclaimed Texan Free State immediately appealed to the United States for aid.

Independence and American Neutrality (1844–1845)

President Victor Yates, privately sympathetic to the Texan cause, found his hands tied: less than a decade after the first war in Mexico, there was no congressional appetite for another. He sent armaments to the Texan forces but could not obtain a declaration of war, and the question dominated the election of 1844, which Vice President Wilbur Haines won while carefully refusing to commit to intervention. Meanwhile the Imperial Army retook Coahuila, Nuevo León, and Tamaulipas, consolidated coastal Sonora, and raided the Californian coast — though the Californians struck back with an invasion of Baja California, and the Imperial push into Chihuahua was stopped.

The Bostonian and American Entry

On August 13, 1845, the Mexican Navy sank the American merchant ship S.S. Bostonian as she attempted to enter the Texan port of Galvez Town (today Galvezport). The political winds shifted overnight everywhere outside staunchly Federalist New England, and on September 2 President Haines asked Congress to declare war on the Empire — which it did, over a handful of nays from Massachusetts and New Hampshire. The Navy cleared the Texan coast and raided Veracruz in November; and on December 11, 1845, Texan soldiers occupied Santa Fe, where Landavazo had maintained the pretense of republican authority to the last — the formal end of the Mexican Republic.

The Campaigns of 1846

In January 1846, before American troops could arrive in force, the Imperial Army crossed the Rio Grande toward San Antonio, aiming for the Texan capital at Franklin-on-the-Brazos. The Texan Army slowed the advance until several hundred American soldiers — the van of thousands marching west from the railheads in Arkansas and Louisiana — joined the Battle of San Antonio (February 3–5, 1846); after three days the Imperials retreated, and were pushed back to the Rio Grande within a month. When Imperial forces crossed the Colorado River into Baja California and threatened to cut off the Californian army, the allies answered indirectly: a march toward Mexico City and the bombardment of the Gulf ports. The Battle of Monterrey (October 3–6, 1846), the largest of the war, was a costly allied victory that exhausted the momentum for a deeper drive south — but with Veracruz destroyed as a functioning port, the Empire pulled men and materiel out of California, and the Californians drove the Imperials back across the Colorado into Sonora.

The End of the War (1847)

An Imperial attempt to retake Monterrey in March 1847 was repelled, and the allied armies pushed toward Ciudad Victoria. In April, a cadre of Imperial military officers forced Emperor Raul to accept a ceasefire, which the United States, Texas, and California readily joined. Peace talks convened — once again — at New Orleans, and on May 10, 1847, the Second Treaty of New Orleans formally ended the war, recognizing the independence of the Texas Federation and the Union of the Californias. The United States pledged to respect the sovereignty of both new republics and to make no attempt at annexation.

Aftermath

Texas emerged controlling the former provinces of Texas and Nuevo Mexico, keeping its capital at Franklin-on-the-Brazos — renamed Texopolis in 1848 — and adopting an American-style constitution with a National Assembly and elected president. The Californias established a unicameral Parliament electing a Premier. The Texian settlement carried a poison pill: the populous eastern state of Austin refused abolition, the constitution left slavery to the individual states, and the Austinian delegation's 1848 runaway-slave law bound the new federation into the sectional politics tearing at its northern neighbor. In the Empire, the same officers who had forced the ceasefire compelled Emperor Raul to accept a liberal constitution in 1848, blaming the throne for the war. And with the fighting done, the diplomats finished the map: the Boston Accords of May 1848 settled the remaining boundary disputes — above all the Oregon Country — completing the borders of North America still in use today.
