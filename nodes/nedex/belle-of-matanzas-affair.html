Belle of Matanzas Affair
August 23, 1902 · Atlantic Ocean off South Carolina

The Belle of Matanzas affair was the interception on August 23, 1902 of the Cuban steamer SS Belle of Matanzas by the USS Philadelphia off the coast of South Carolina. The vessel was found to be carrying rifles, ammunition, and explosives consigned to anti-government radicals in the Coastal South.

It furnished the first documentary proof that the American exile communities at Matanzas Bay were arming insurrectionists inside the United States, and it opened the four-year deterioration in relations with the Kingdom of Spain that ended in the Matanzas War.

Background

That the Matanzas settlements maintained contact with separatist elements in the Coastal South had been known to the federal government for a generation and had never been treated as urgent. The correspondence intercepted over the years was voluble and largely empty, the product of ageing men in Cuba writing to disaffected men in Virginia about a restoration neither had the means to attempt. Departmental assessments through the 1890s described the traffic as essentially harmless, and a Navy that had fought no war in forty years had little appetite for patrolling against it.

Reports in the summer of 1902 of a vessel loading under unusual arrangements at Matanzas were treated seriously largely because a junior officer at the Charleston station declined to file them as routine.

The interception

The Philadelphia took the Belle eleven miles off the South Carolina coast on the morning of August 23. There was no resistance; the master surrendered his papers on demand, apparently in the belief that the manifest would survive inspection. Beneath a declared cargo of sugar machinery the boarding party found some four hundred rifles of Spanish manufacture, ammunition in quantity, and a consignment of commercial explosives.

The papers

The evidentiary value of the seizure lay in the ship's documents rather than her hold. The papers named consignees — men in three states, several of them of some local standing — and established that payment had originated with the common funds of The Assembly, the governing body of the Matanzas Bay Compact. What the federal government had understood as sympathy between two communities of like mind was revealed as a financed operation directed from Cuban soil.

The Belle was taken into Charleston and condemned in admiralty. Her crew, Spanish subjects, were detained for four months and released following protests from Madrid, a concession the administration made without enthusiasm. Prosecutions of the American consignees followed through 1903 and produced convictions in two states.

The Fleak administration's response

President Alexander Fleak declined to act against Spain. His reasoning, set out to the cabinet in September, was that the Great European War then raging made the moment the worst conceivable for a confrontation in the Caribbean, and that the United States had no interest in becoming a second front to a conflict it had successfully avoided. He confined the American response to a formal demand that Spain permit federal investigators access to the Matanzas settlements. It was refused, and refused again in every subsequent year — a persistence that puzzled the State Department at the time and is now generally attributed less to Spanish pride than to what an inspection would have found on the enclave's estates.

The judgement was defensible and it was unpopular. Fleak was denounced across the Coastal South, where the conspiracy had been aimed, and in the Northern press, which had less patience for Spanish sovereignty than the State Department did. He held to it until his stroke in January of 1903, and his successor Peter Landon held to it after him.

Escalation

The affair established a pattern that repeated for two years with rising cost. The San Angelo fired on a boarding party from the USS Manhattan in November 1903 and killed Ensign Thomas M. Keeler. The schooner Spirit of 1861 was beached near Key Largo with a hold of Spanish rifles and correspondence addressed to extremists in three states. The patrol boat USS Harrington was lost with all hands off Perdido Key in October 1904, her wreck bearing signs of attack and recovered a week before the presidential election.

Legacy

The affair is conventionally dated as the beginning of the Matanzas crisis, and the handling of it as the defining question of the election of 1904, which Cornelius Roosevelt II won on an explicit undertaking to bring the Matanzas Bay clique to heel.

Its longer significance lies in what the ship's papers proved: that the exile enclave had ceased to be a community of embittered emigrants and become an organisation making war on the United States by proxy, and that four decades of federal indifference had been a policy rather than an oversight.
