Provinces of Canada

Administrative divisions of the Dominion of Canada

The Dominion of Canada consists of fifteen provinces, one territory, and one federal district. Ten provinces joined at Confederation in 1865; the remaining five were added over subsequent decades as population grew and territorial administration required reorganization.

Federal District

The District of Manitoulin is a federal capital district, established in the mid-1880s on Manitoulin Island in Lake Huron. It is the site of Aurora, the capital city of Canada. The District is separate from all provinces, modelled on the American example of the District of Washington. It contains the Wikwemikong Unceded Indian Reserve, whose people did not sign the treaty under which the island was acquired by the federal government — a situation that has been a persistent civil rights issue throughout the twentieth century.

Founding Provinces (1865)

The following ten provinces joined Confederation at its establishment:

ProvinceCapitalNotes
QuebecQuebec CityFormerly Lower Canada. French-Catholic majority. One of the two founding cultural pillars of the Dominion.
OntarioTorontoFormerly Upper Canada (core). English-Protestant majority. Served as the de facto seat of federal government until Aurora became operational in the early 1900s.
MichiganDetroitFormerly the Michigan district of Upper Canada, acquired by Britain following the War of 1812. The most economically dynamic province of the early Dominion, with a character shaped by its proximity to the United States.
TecumsehTecumsehFormerly the Wisconsin district of Upper Canada. Named for the Shawnee leader whose alliance with Britain was central to the War of 1812. Centered on the city of Tecumseh on the Chicago River — a major border city opposite the American city of Calumet — and Fond du Lac to the north.
Nova ScotiaHalifaxOne of the original British North American colonies. Maritime province with strong seafaring and fishing traditions.
New BrunswickFrederictonOne of the original British North American colonies. Home to a significant Acadian (French-Catholic) minority population, making it a persistently bilingual province.
Prince Edward IslandCharlottetownSmall island province in the Gulf of St. Lawrence.
NewfoundlandSt. John'sIsland province and adjacent Labrador coast. Historically oriented toward the Atlantic fishery.
ColumbiaPugetsburghPacific coast province, extending from the Columbia River northward. Established as a colony in 1848 following the settlement of the Pacific Northwest boundary. Pugetsburgh is the largest city on Canada's Pacific coast.
Vancouver IslandCharlotteIsland province off the Pacific coast. Established as a separate colony from Columbia in 1848.

Later Provinces

The following provinces were added after Confederation:

ProvinceCapitalAdmittedNotes
AssiniboiaRed RiverLate 1880sCreated following negotiations with the Métis community of the Red River valley, whose land rights were threatened by incoming prairie settlers. Small in area but significant in symbolic importance. French-Catholic, Michif-speaking character. The only province outside Quebec where French is the primary language of a majority of the population. Its boundaries incorporate portions of territory that extend south of the original border into what would in other circumstances form part of the northern United States.
SaskatchewanReginac. 1890sPrairie province carved from the Interior Territory as agricultural settlement grew. Named for the Saskatchewan River, from the Cree kisiskāciwani-sīpiy, meaning "swift-flowing river."
AthabascaEdmontonc. 1900sWestern prairie province, east of the Rocky Mountains. Named for the Athabasca River and Lake. Shares a border with Columbia Province along the Rocky Mountain divide.
AlexandriaTBDc. 1900sCanadian Shield and boreal forest zone, covering the territory north of the Great Lakes between Ontario and the prairie provinces. Named in honour of Prince Alexander of the Netherlands (1818–1881), husband of Queen Charlotte I and father of the royal line. Sparsely populated until the development of mining and forestry industries in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
AlaskaJuneauc. 1898–1902Purchased from the Russian Empire in 1878 and administered as Alaska Territory, incorporating the Yukon and the northern interior of the Pacific Northwest. A major gold rush in the late 1890s rapidly expanded the population and created pressure for provincial status, which was granted around the turn of the century.

Territory

TerritoryNotes
Northern TerritoryCovers the vast Arctic and sub-Arctic interior of the continent. Sparsely populated throughout the territory's history; has not achieved province status.

Notes on Geography

The border between Canada and the United States does not follow the 49th parallel precisely. It follows watersheds and natural features, meaning that portions of the northern prairie region lie within Canada that would in other configurations form part of the United States, particularly in the areas corresponding to portions of the northern Great Plains. Small slivers of territory above the 49th parallel in the west are within the United States.

The Great Lakes are almost entirely within Canadian territory — only Lakes Ontario and Erie share shorelines with the United States. Columbia Province extends south to the Columbia River, incorporating territory on the Pacific coast that includes Pugetsburgh. Alaska Province includes the Yukon region and the northern interior of the Pacific Northwest in addition to the Alaska peninsula itself.
