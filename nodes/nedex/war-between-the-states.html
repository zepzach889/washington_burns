War Between the States
Civil conflict that reshaped the United States, 1857–1860

The War Between the States (1857–1860) was a civil conflict that divided the United States along the fault lines of slavery, constitutional legitimacy, and regional identity. It began in the spring of 1857 as a confrontation between the federal government of President James Hawthorne and the seceding states of New England, and expanded over three and a half years into a conflict involving four distinct armed factions, a slave uprising of historic proportions, British military intervention, and the near-total collapse of the constitutional order. It ended on October 17, 1860, when Hawthorne surrendered in Atlanta and resigned the presidency.

The war is the defining event of 19th-century American history. Its consequences — the abolition of slavery, the Constitutional Convention of 1861, the Constitution of 1861, and the transformation of the republic's political and social landscape — shaped every subsequent era. It is remembered not as a war of North against South in the simple geographic sense, but as a war of freedom against slavery, of constitutional legitimacy against executive tyranny, fought by an unlikely alliance of New England abolitionists, Western free-state advocates, former slaves, Native nations, and eventually the British Empire.

Background

The road to war

The immediate causes of the war lay in the escalating crisis of the Lee Administration (1851–1856). The Runaway Slave Act, passed in 1850, had produced years of defiance from Northern states — particularly Massachusetts — and a string of legal and political confrontations. When President Robert E. Lee was assassinated in January 1856, his vice president, James Hawthorne of Georgia, assumed the office and immediately hardened the government's stance. The Anti-Radical Act, passed in March of that year, allowed the arrest of anyone deemed to be fomenting radicalism — a law used almost immediately against abolitionist editors and politicians in New England.

Hawthorne won his own full term in November 1856 despite losing the combined popular vote to his opponents — a result that reflected the structural inequalities of the electoral system, with the South's restricted franchise inflating its electoral weight relative to its actual population. New York alone cast over 600,000 votes and Ohio nearly 400,000, while Virginia and Tennessee cast only around 130,000 each and South Carolina still selected its electors through the state legislature without a popular vote at all. When Hawthorne reshuffled his cabinet with loyalists and showed no sign of moderation, Federalists and Northern Democrats in New England prepared for confrontation.

The Boston Crisis (January–May 1857)

The crisis broke on January 12, 1857, when Lionel Anderson, the Federalist vice-presidential candidate, gave a fiery speech in Boston calling for Hawthorne's resignation and a constitutional convention to fix the nation's broken electoral system. Two days later, Anderson was arrested by federal marshals under the Anti-Radical Act and charged with treason. On January 15, an angry mob stormed the federal courthouse in Boston, freed Anderson, and looted the building. Massachusetts Governor Upton Ellsworth declared martial law in Boston on January 16 — not to suppress the mob, but to protect Anderson and seize federal property in the state.

President Hawthorne ordered Ellsworth to stand down and hand over Anderson on January 17. Ellsworth publicly refused on January 19, calling for New England leaders to convene in Boston on February 23. Connecticut and Rhode Island announced they were standing with Massachusetts. A standoff at the New York-Connecticut border on February 4 — where the Connecticut state militia refused to allow federal troops through — ended with the federal commander backing down; an enraged Hawthorne had him sacked and began planning the invasion of Boston with his Secretary of War, Oscar Pence.

On February 23, Ellsworth convened a conference in Boston attended by delegates from all of New England as well as New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, and even Ohio. After more than a week of often angry debate — with talk of a "second revolution" and some delegates openly floating secession — the conference produced the Boston Ultimatum: Hawthorne and his Vice President must resign, handing the presidency to Speaker of the House Hugo Brandt; the Anti-Radical Act and Runaway Slave Act must be repealed immediately; and a national conference must be convened to reform a presidential election system that had produced a president without a popular mandate. If these demands were not met within one month, the participating states would reconsider their relationship with the Union.

The Boston Ultimatum was delivered to Hawthorne on March 9. He crumpled the telegram and returned to planning the invasion. His only formal response was to order the congressional delegations of Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Rhode Island arrested on March 10 — an action Speaker Brandt protested but was powerless to stop. On March 17, nineteen warships and several troop ships sailed for Boston with orders to blockade the harbor and land troops to arrest the governor and senior legislators. On March 19, as the fleet entered the harbor, Governor Ellsworth — then overseeing the evacuation of the state government to Springfield — ordered the commander of Fort Warren to fire a warning shot across the bow of the lead ship. The flotilla's captains mistook the shot for an attack and opened fire on the fort, then turned their guns on the city itself. Federal troops landed and took tentative control of Boston amid panic and flight.

The shelling of Boston shocked the nation. New Hampshire, Maine, and Vermont called up their militias in solidarity, and a second New England convention was set for April 1 in Montpelier, Vermont. Hawthorne ordered the arrest of the remaining New England congressional delegations, drawing condemnation not only from Brandt but from representatives of Pennsylvania, Ohio, and Indiana. Indiana Senator Abraham Lincoln — later identified by genealogists as the great-great-grandfather of future president Georgina Lincoln — rose on the Senate floor to denounce the president's actions as tyranny.

At the Montpelier Conference, delegations from Ohio and Pennsylvania walked out rather than support secession, but the remaining New England delegates voted to affirm the intent to secede on April 1, and on April 9 a committee led by Massachusetts delegate Peter Hendricks presented the Declaration of Liberty, framing the break as the start of a "Second Revolution" aimed at renewing rather than destroying the Union. Massachusetts formally voted to secede on April 15. The following day, Massachusetts militia — reinforced by Connecticut and Rhode Island units — moved to drive federal forces out of Boston entirely; by April 18 the Union troops had retreated to their ships, still holding a naval blockade but no longer controlling the city itself, an important early morale victory for the New England cause. By the end of April, all six New England states had voted to secede, forming the Confederation of American States.

On May 1, 1857, the Montpelier Conference declared Ulysses Portman Acting President of the Confederation. That same day, the "Rump Congress" in Franklin — minus the New England delegations — declared the seceding states in open rebellion and authorized a call-up of 200,000 men, over the objections of many Northern Democrats who abstained or voted no despite fear of reprisal. The United States was at war with itself.

The war

Phase One — The Eastern theater and the Goal Decree (1857)

New York State, caught between the Union government and the Confederal cause, fractured: the state legislature voted narrowly to remain in the Union on May 4, but New York City and Long Island voted to join the Confederation, producing two rival governments for the state. On July 4, an unknown shooter fired on Hawthorne's carriage in Franklin, gravely wounding First Lady Christina Hawthorne, who died days later. Hawthorne responded by suspending habeas corpus, and Democratic support for the war effort began to collapse.

Painting depicting the bombardment of New York City, September 1857.

The first major battle of the war came September 13–18, 1857, when General Jason Gains led the Union First Army in an assault on Manhattan Island against the "Confederal State of Lower New York." A massive bombardment on the night of the 13th preceded a landing at dawn on the 14th; badly outnumbered Confederal forces put up a brave defense, but Manhattan fell by sunset on the 15th, with Gains' troops sweeping through Brooklyn and the surrounding settlements over the following three days. The scale of violence against an American city shocked the nation — Gains earned the lasting nickname "Butcher of New York" in the Democratic and Federalist press — and public opinion turned sharply against the war. Ohio announced it would send no more men, and anti-war protests broke out in Philadelphia, Columbus, Indianapolis, and elsewhere across the Midwest.

A New Jersey secession attempt in mid-October was crushed when Gains occupied Trenton and declared martial law on October 19, placing the state under military rule for the war's duration — a distraction that bought the Confederation valuable time to regroup. In November, roughly half the ships in the Union's blockading fleet mutinied and declared for the Confederation in what became known as the "Mutiny Fleet," ending the first Boston blockade after a brief naval skirmish.

At the end of November 1857, the Confederal Congress held its famous "Goals Debate" over the ultimate purpose of the rebellion — permanent independence, or a renewal of the United States as a whole. The latter view prevailed, formalized on November 20 as the Goal Decree: the Confederation's stated aim was to end slavery nationwide and secure constitutional reforms preventing a recurrence of the Hawthorne crisis, not permanent secession — but with an expiration date. If Hawthorne's government had not been overthrown within five years, the Confederation would pursue permanent independence instead.

Phase Two — Organization (1858)

Through 1858, both the Confederation and a new third faction organized for the fight ahead. The Confederation adopted a Westminster-style constitution, with a popularly elected President — Upton Ellsworth — serving mostly as a figurehead alongside a unicameral Congress that elected Ulysses Portman as Consul, the office wielding most practical executive power. The Confederal Army, under General Harrison Bradshaw of Massachusetts, numbered only about 70,000 volunteers, and Portman and Ellsworth quietly secured British assurances of continued trade and arms sales.

Meanwhile, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Indiana, Illinois, Iowa, and several other Midwestern states, refusing to send further men to Hawthorne's war, assembled in Philadelphia as the Free State Congress and by May 1858 had reconstituted the United States government under Acting President Hugo Brandt, declaring Hawthorne's government illegitimate. Where the Confederation had revolutionary clarity and New England's industrial base, and Hawthorne's Union retained the standing army and the South, Brandt's Free States commanded the loyalty of the nation's most populous and productive states — a third, decisive faction in the war.

Phase Three — Franklin, Kentucky, and the eastern counterstroke (1859)

General Alexander Bloom of Indiana took command of the Free Army of the West in December 1858 and by March 1859 was ready to move on Franklin. The assault began March 11 against the newly built Fort Lee near Reading, on the Ohio-District of Washington boundary. After two days of fighting the Union Army retreated into Franklin proper, and by March 14 had fallen back to the south bank of the Ohio River, surrendering the "legislative bank" of the capital — including Congress Hall — while the Union's Second Army held Washington House and the "executive bank." With the bridges cut, Bloom sent a large force secretly east to cross the river and strike the Union's southern position on March 22; after a day of fighting the Union retreated entirely from the capital toward Frankfort, Kentucky. Acting President Brandt, cautious about overextension, kept the Free State capital in Philadelphia for the time being rather than relocating immediately to the fallen city.

Bloom pressed the advantage southward. The Battle of Frankfort began May 30 and lasted nearly three days before Union forces broke and retreated — most toward Lexington, a smaller contingent toward Louisville. When news of the Free State victory reached Lexington, the town rose up in support, and its militia harried the retreating Union column alongside Free State soldiers; nearly 50,000 Union troops surrendered outside Lexington on June 7. Free State forces spent the rest of June securing eastern Kentucky before turning on Louisville, which fell after a three-day bombardment beginning June 30, surrendering July 2 — effectively liberating the entire state.

In the east, the Union struck back. The First Army marched up the Hudson beginning April 3 and was bombarding Free State positions south of Albany by April 15; a diversionary thrust toward Hartford, Connecticut was checked by Confederal forces at the Battle of New Haven, but this succeeded in preventing the Confederation from reinforcing Albany in time, and the city fell to the Union on April 24. Saratoga Springs followed within two weeks. By mid-May 1859, the Free States and the Confederation were cut off from one another, forced to rely on British-controlled Canada to communicate — a propaganda triumph for Hawthorne that overshadowed news of Franklin's fall for months, thanks to tightening press censorship in Union territory. In July, Gains attempted to press the advantage further, driving toward the British Canadian frontier; the effort was repulsed at the First Battle of Albany on July 17 by New York militia and Confederal Army Group Alpha, forcing his retreat back down the Hudson.

The Union scored one further success that spring: the Second Battle of Boston Harbor on May 22 was a decisive Union naval victory, sinking a third of the Confederal Navy and scattering the rest to Maine and Lower Canada. The blockade of Boston Harbor was restored — a triumph Hawthorne did not yet realize would prove his undoing, as it set the stage for the confrontation with Britain that followed.

Phase Four — British entry, the slave uprising, and endgame (1859–1860)

With Boston blockaded again, British merchants pressed to keep trading with the Confederation, and by late August 1859 pressure from Parliament and the Palace pushed Prime Minister Longfellow to authorize the Royal Navy to protect British shipping around Boston. The confrontation came in September: the Royal Navy engaged the Union blockading squadron directly, sinking eight Union warships and effectively lifting the blockade by force. Hawthorne, reportedly stunned that Britain would intervene militarily, issued an ultimatum on September 20 demanding Britain pay reparations for the destroyed ships, surrender the responsible flotilla commander, and cease all trade with the rebel territories — or face war. Britain refused by the end of October. On November 2, Hawthorne asked Congress for a declaration of war against Britain, which passed unanimously, adding a fourth belligerent to the conflict. Britain's response, reaching Atlanta on December 12, was to formally recognize the Confederation's independence and Brandt's Free State government as the legitimate United States — news that led Hawthorne to write in his journal that "the Union I was elected to lead is truly broken... the South must remain free."

The final blow arrived from an unexpected direction. On January 19, 1860, a slave named Joshua Black, a senior house attendant on a plantation near Aberdeen, Mississippi, launched an uprising, supplied with arms by the Native Nations as part of a broader effort to harry the Union on the Free States' behalf. Black's movement spread rapidly; within weeks hundreds of slaves had joined him, and Mississippi's governor was pleading with Atlanta for troops. Hawthorne, with his Second and Third Armies already struggling against the Free Army of the West's advance through Tennessee, made the fateful decision to pull troops from the First Army in New York to fight the uprising instead — fatally weakening the eastern front. On May 1, 1860, at his headquarters in Columbus, Mississippi, Black declared the Republic of New Africa, drawing both fleeing slaves and free Black volunteers from the North to its administration.

At the end of May, Black's deputy Ben Cain arrived in Franklin to negotiate with the Free States: the Republic of New Africa would fold back into the United States and back the Free State cause fully, but only if Brandt's government unequivocally committed to full Negro equality — otherwise Black would fight on for full independence. Despite considerable resistance within Brandt's own coalition, two weeks of intensive negotiation with congressional leadership produced the Negro Emancipation Act: it abolished slavery nationwide, compensated slaveholders in loyal territory while freeing — without compensation — all enslaved people in Hawthorne-controlled territory, and granted full citizenship to the formerly enslaved. The Act passed Congress on June 30 and was signed in a special ceremony on July 4. Within a week, Black announced the Republic of New Africa's loyalty to the Free State cause, though a minority faction that had hoped for permanent independence broke away to form scattered guerrilla bands that harassed both sides for the remainder of the war.

The summer and fall of 1860 saw the Southern Union collapse. Britain and the Confederation launched "Operation Atlantis," a joint invasion staged from New York Harbor; landings south of Savannah, Georgia on July 7 took the city within a week, and Confederal and British forces pushed toward Atlanta via Augusta (captured August 19) and Covington (September 26). The Free Army of the West crossed into Alabama, taking Huntsville on June 10, and won one of the war's bloodiest engagements at Birmingham on August 1; Union commander General Matthews was killed in an ambush three days later. Virginia voted to join the Free States on August 18 after securing compensation guarantees for slaveholders; Maryland followed August 24, Delaware September 1. By October 1, Confederal and British forces held the southern and eastern approaches to Atlanta while Free State forces closed from the north and west. Joshua Black's forces took New Orleans after four days of fighting; Native units from Kanasaw occupied Prosperity, the capital of Jefferson, on October 12. On October 14, a slave assassinated Vice President Josiah Blankenship.

On October 16, Hawthorne's remaining cabinet and army commanders confronted him and demanded surrender. He agreed after hours of argument. On October 17, 1860, negotiators carried a ceasefire request to the encircling Allied armies; the guns fell silent at 3:00 p.m. The following day, combined columns of Free State, Confederal, and British troops marched unopposed into Atlanta. Generals Harrison Bradshaw and Alexander Bloom met Hawthorne at his residence, where he signed an unconditional surrender to "the Allied Forces of the Free State Alliance, the Confederation of American States, and the British Empire," followed immediately by his resignation as president — the only president in American history to surrender to enemy forces. His remaining cabinet resigned alongside him. He departed into exile abroad, where he remained for the rest of his life.

Aftermath

The Interregnum (1860–1863)

Over 500,000 soldiers and tens of thousands of civilians had died. Georgia, Tennessee, Kentucky, Mississippi, Louisiana, Alabama, and New York lay scarred by the fighting, with Manhattan — fought over three separate times — reduced to a hollowed ruin and its population collapsing from nearly half a million before the war to an estimated 75,000 by 1863. Fighting did not fully end with the surrender: local militia bands and renegade Union units continued sporadic resistance, and violent unrest persisted in Republic of New Africa territory. A week after the surrender, Acting President Brandt and Confederal Consul Portman met in Philadelphia for the Brandt-Portman Accords, establishing four key agreements: joint Free State-Confederal military occupation of the southern states under a Joint Military Command; a roadmap toward reunification; provisions for handling the Republic of New Africa's status; and a commitment to convene a constitutional convention. The Confederation would dissolve once a new constitution was ratified — a process that dragged on until November 1861, when Rhode Island, the last holdout, moved to dissolve the Confederation and rejoin the Union, ending four years of the Confederal blue-and-white-striped flag flying over Montpelier.

Emancipation was enforced unevenly and often violently. Through the winter of 1860 and spring of 1861, Joint Military Command detachments visited plantations across Georgia, Florida, Arkansas, Jefferson, and the Carolinas to formally free the enslaved population still in bondage; celebrations were common, but so were violent confrontations with plantation owners who refused to comply. Tens of thousands of freedmen migrated toward former Republic of New Africa territory in Louisiana and Mississippi, while many white Southerners fled west to Jefferson, the Upper Louisiana Territory, or the still-slaveholding Texas Federation — and some smuggled enslaved people and wealth to Cuba or Brazil. The resulting collapse of plantation agriculture produced famine conditions across parts of the Deep South, met only partially by Northern relief efforts and "grain trains" from Ohio and Pennsylvania. New York's destruction as the nation's financial hub shifted the center of American capitalism to Philadelphia's Walnut Street for years to come.

The Constitutional Convention of 1861

Two developments nearly derailed the path to a constitutional convention before it began. First, the Native Nations District — organized as the Territory of Gigadohi — and the Kanasaw Territory successfully petitioned in January 1861 for representation, winning Brandt's support for a rule granting all federal territories three delegates each. Second, on January 27, Commander Black announced that the Republic of New Africa would dissolve and reconstitute the state governments of Louisiana, Mississippi, and Alabama, sending Black delegates to the convention — a move that stunned even Brandt, who had assumed white control of Southern state governments would continue by default. Black's state conventions admitted whites who swore an oath rejecting slavery, and with the Carolinas independently adopting new constitutions abolishing slavery, the Free State government found little principled ground to exclude the delegations. Alabama proved the exception: the state remained split between a former-slave-led government in occupied Birmingham and a pre-war government exiled to Mobile, and the resulting stalemate meant no Alabamian delegates ultimately participated.

The Constitutional Convention convened 484 delegates from twenty-five states and four territories, opening at Independence Hall in Philadelphia on March 4, 1861, with most working sessions held at the Merchant's Exchange Building. Notable delegates included Pennsylvania Senator Thaddeus Stevens, Massachusetts Lieutenant Governor Charles Sumner, New York's former Free State Governor William H. Seward, Indiana Senator Abraham Lincoln (who presided over the convention), Kentucky's pre-war governor John J. Crittenden, Kentucky Representative Cassius Clay, North Carolina Senator William Graham, South Carolina's pre-war governor James Petigru, former Oregon territorial governor George Abernethy, Mississippi's acting governor Joshua Black, Louisiana's acting lieutenant governor Ben Cain, and Gigadohi Deputy Governor Degataga Watie. Of the 484 delegates, twenty were Black — including seven of Louisiana's eleven and nine of Mississippi's thirteen — and five were Native American.

The resulting Constitution of 1861 abolished slavery outright in Article I, established the Secretary General system linking the executive and House leadership, retained the single six-year presidential term, introduced congressional term limits, and clarified the presidential line of succession. The document was adopted on May 15, 1861, by a vote of 468 to 16, and ratified by the required nineteen states by September. Hugo Brandt became the first president to serve under it.

Legacy

The War Between the States is the central event of American 19th-century history. Its immediate legacy was the abolition of slavery and the transformation of the constitutional order. Its longer legacy was the normalization of Black political participation — with the Freedmen States of Louisiana and Mississippi sending Black representatives to Congress from the first post-war elections — and the eventual path that led to Peter Landon's presidency in 1903 and Henry Freeman's election in 1940.

The war also produced its darker legacies. The destruction of New York City — fought over three times and reduced to ruins — took decades to reverse. The exile community of former slaveholders who settled in Matanzas Bay, Cuba and formed the Matanzas Compact became the seed of the crisis that produced the Caribbean War (1905–06). And the social wounds of the conflict — the resentments of the Coastal South, the unresolved questions of racial justice in practice — did not simply vanish with the constitution's ratification.

Abraham Lincoln's opening address to the Constitutional Convention is among the most quoted speeches in American history. Hugo Brandt's wartime leadership is commemorated in the Brandt Memorial in Franklin.
