Empire of Mexico
Imperio de México · Constitutional monarchy · Founded 1835
Contents

  2. Government
  3. Geography
  4. Foreign Relations

The Empire of Mexico (Imperio de México) is a constitutional monarchy occupying the southern portion of the North American continent. Bordered to the north by the Texas Federation, the Union of the Californias, and the United States, it is the largest nation by land area in the North American Conference framework. Its capital and largest city is Mexico City.

The Empire was founded in 1835 following a conservative counter-coup against the liberal Mexican Republic, with General Raul Guerra proclaimed Emperor Raul I. After decades of tension between the imperial throne and the elected Cortes, the Empire fought a devastating civil war from 1913 to 1921, from which Empress Gabriella emerged victorious. A new constitution adopted in 1922 established genuine parliamentary democracy, universal suffrage, and a formal separation of church and state. Mexico joined the North American Conference in 1925 and has been a member of the Global Peace Council since its founding in 1940.

History

Mexico's imperial era began with the counter-coup of 1835, in which conservative military officers overthrew the liberal republican government and proclaimed General Raul Guerra as Emperor Raul I. A constitution adopted in 1848 following the Second Mexican War established a unicameral Cortes but retained sweeping powers for the throne. The following decades were marked by persistent conflict between the crown and the legislature, with reformist and conservative factions competing for control of the government.

The reign of Emperor Jorge I (1897–1913) represented a decisive shift toward constitutional governance. Jorge I expanded the franchise, enacted early labor protections and church-state separation measures, and secured the passage of universal male suffrage shortly before his unexpected death in 1913. His eldest child, Empress Gabriella, succeeded him at the age of 26.

Within months of her accession, Gabriella's younger brother declared himself Emperor Raul IV, plunging the country into eight years of civil war. Gabriella's forces, backed by the military leadership her father had quietly cultivated, prevailed. The pretender died of battlefield wounds in 1920 and his cause collapsed the following year. The Constitution of 1922 formally established parliamentary supremacy, separated church from state, and extended the vote to women — making Mexico one of the first nations in the world to adopt universal suffrage.

Mexico provided economic support and logistical access to the Allied powers during the Global War (1932–1940) without committing military forces directly. The postwar period has brought deepening integration with Mexico's North American neighbours, though elements of the conservative establishment remain wary of American influence.

Government

The Empire of Mexico is governed as a constitutional monarchy. The Empress serves as head of state, a role that has become increasingly ceremonial over the course of the twentieth century. Executive authority rests with the Prime Minister, who leads the government and commands a majority in the Cortes. The Cortes may be dissolved to trigger new elections; a mandatory election must be held at least every five years regardless.

The principal parties are the reformist Partido Liberal Mexicano (PLM), the moderate conservative Partido Nacional Conservador (PNC), and the labor-aligned Partido Laborista (PL), which has served as a parliamentary kingmaker since the 1930s. The hardline Partido Imperial, dominant through much of the nineteenth century, was effectively destroyed by its association with the Raulist cause in the civil war.

Geography

The Empire occupies all of the territory of the former Mexican Republic south of the Rio Grande, with the exception of Baja California, which was ceded to the Union of the Californias following the Second Mexican War. Its northern frontier includes portions of the Sonoran Desert and the territories that in earlier centuries were contested with the United States and Texas.

Foreign Relations

Mexico is a founding member of the North American Conference and a participant in the Pan-American Passport zone, though it negotiated more opt-outs and transition provisions than any other member state. Its conservative establishment has historically viewed close economic integration with the United States with suspicion, a sentiment rooted in the wars of the 1840s and Mexico's long experience of defending its sovereignty against more powerful neighbours. The postwar period has nonetheless seen meaningful deepening of North American economic ties.
