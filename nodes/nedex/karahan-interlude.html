Karahan Interlude
Autumn 1915 – Spring 1917 · Suspension of the Ottoman Constitution of 1883

The Karahan Interlude (Ottoman Turkish: كرهان فاصله, Karahan Fasıla; Turkish: Karahan Arası) refers to the period from the autumn of 1915 through the spring of 1917 during which the Ottoman Constitution of 1883 was suspended and the Ottoman Empire was governed by Grand Vizier Sheikh Hafız Ibrahim Karahan under a framework that subordinated constitutional authority to Islamic governance principles. The interlude began with Karahan's appointment by Sultan Mehmed V in the aftermath of the Macedonian Crisis (1913–1915) and ended with the restorative coup of spring 1917 led by Kemal Şahin, which resulted in Karahan's arrest, Mehmed V's abdication, and the accession of Mahmud III.

The Karahan Interlude is the most significant interruption of constitutional governance in the empire's modern history and is regarded by historians as the crisis that clarified the New Ottoman Era's political program more decisively than any other event. Its failure — administrative, economic, diplomatic, and ultimately military — demonstrated both the practical impossibility of governing a modern empire through religious principles alone and the depth of the constitutional order's institutional roots after three decades of operation.

Origins

The conditions that made the Karahan Interlude possible were created by the Macedonian Crisis (1913–1915). The negotiated settlement that recognised Macedonian independence in mid-1915 destroyed the parliamentary coalition that had governed the empire throughout the constitutional era, leaving no faction with the arithmetic to form a stable successor government. The lower chamber of the Imperial Parliament debated inconclusively through the summer and autumn of 1915, generating recrimination without resolution.

Sultan Mehmed V's response to this paralysis reflected both his temperamental limitations and the genuine desperation of a man watching the political system he had presided over collapse in real time. He was not, by most accounts, a convinced adherent of Karahan's theological and political program. He was a man looking for someone to blame and someone to act — and Karahan, with his decades of public scholarship, his genuine popular following in Anatolian religious circles, and his ready explanation for the empire's failures, offered both.

The appointment was also, in part, a punishment directed at the modernising faction that the Sultan held responsible for Macedonia. Karahan's appointment was Mehmed V's most consequential exercise of the residual appointment powers the 1883 constitution had left him — and the exercise that would prompt those powers' curtailment in the 1917 amendments.

The Suspension of Parliament

Parliament was suspended within weeks of Karahan's appointment, announced through an imperial decree framed as a temporary emergency measure necessitated by the national crisis. Karahan's constitutional justification — such as it was — drew on the argument that the 1883 constitutional framework was a human instrument subordinate to divine law, and that in a moment of existential emergency the empire's Islamic governance foundations took precedence over its constitutional superstructure. He maintained publicly that constitutional governance would be restored once the empire's spiritual foundations had been re-established on firmer ground.

The suspension was received differently across the empire's diverse constituencies. In parts of Anatolia and the Hejaz, where Karahan's scholarly reputation was strongest and where the constitutional era's secularising drift had been most resented, the announcement was met with genuine if cautious approval. In Istanbul's commercial districts, in the Levantine port cities, and throughout the Arab provinces' educated classes, it was received with alarm. Among the officer corps — men whose professional formation was entirely a product of the Prussian-advised constitutional era — it was received with hostility that went beyond political disagreement into institutional rejection.

Egypt's response was the most consequential of the Arab provinces'. The Khedivate of Egypt formally declined to recognise the authority of a government that had suspended the constitution, issuing a public declaration that stopped deliberately short of independence but withdrew Egyptian recognition from the Karahan government entirely. The declaration signalled to the wider Arab world that the empire's most autonomous province did not regard the interlude as legitimate Ottoman governance.

Governance

Religious Courts and Legal Reform

The most immediately visible change of the Karahan government's early months was the elevation of religious courts in their jurisdiction and authority. Karahan's government issued decrees expanding the scope of Islamic law in areas that the constitutional era's secular legal codes had occupied — commercial disputes, certain categories of criminal matter, and questions of public conduct that religious authorities had long argued fell within their proper domain.

The practical implementation of these decrees was inconsistent. The empire's legal infrastructure was staffed by secular-trained jurists whose professional formation made them poorly suited to administer an expanded religious jurisdiction, and the religious courts themselves lacked the administrative capacity to absorb the caseload the new decrees implied. The result was a period of legal confusion in which neither the suspended secular framework nor the elevated religious one functioned coherently.

Relations with Prussia

Karahan's relationship with the Prussian partnership was the most diplomatically consequential dimension of his government's foreign policy. He maintained the relationship — recognising that an abrupt severance of the canal partnership and the military advisory mission would produce immediate and severe consequences for the empire's financial and military stability — but treated Prussian representatives with a cold formality that communicated his government's ambivalence about its continuation.

Karahan's longer-term vision for the Prussian relationship was never fully articulated, partly because his government did not survive long enough to implement it and partly because he himself had not resolved the tension between his theological objections to the partnership's implicit subordination of Ottoman to Prussian institutional models and his pragmatic recognition of the relationship's material indispensability. His private correspondence from the period suggests a man who intended to renegotiate the partnership's terms substantially — reducing Prussian operational involvement in canal administration, scaling back the military advisory mission, and developing relationships with Muslim-majority states as an alternative diplomatic framework — but who recognised that the sequencing of such changes required a stability his government was not achieving.

The Prussian government communicated its alarm through economic channels within months of the interlude's beginning. Trade volumes through the Suez Canal declined modestly as European commercial houses expressed uncertainty about the reliability of Ottoman administration. Prussian diplomatic representatives in Istanbul sent assessments to Berlin describing a government unlikely to survive in its current form — assessments that, when they eventually became known to Ottoman historians, were regarded as accurate if diplomatically understated.

The Arab Provinces

Karahan's government's relationship with the Arab provinces was characterised by the same ambivalence that marked his broader political thought. His rhetoric of Islamic solidarity resonated with some religious figures in Damascus and the Hejaz, who had grown uncomfortable with the constitutional era's secularising tendencies and saw in Karahan's program a restoration of the empire's Islamic character. His government made gestures toward these constituencies — elevating religious authorities in provincial administration, framing its communications with Arab populations in the language of shared Islamic civilisation rather than Ottoman administrative hierarchy.

But the commercial elites of Beirut and the educated classes of Damascus and Baghdad were alarmed rather than reassured. They had prospered under the constitutional era's relatively predictable administrative and legal framework, and the suspension of that framework introduced uncertainties that affected commercial planning and investment. Egypt's formal non-recognition of the Karahan government — communicated publicly and unmistakably — gave these populations a signal that the empire's most autonomous province had concluded the interlude was not a legitimate Ottoman government, a conclusion many of them were already reaching independently.

The Counter-Movement

The organised opposition to the Karahan government developed through 1916 along several distinct but eventually convergent tracks.

Within the suspended parliament's former membership, informal meetings began within months of the suspension. Former deputies from across the constitutional era's factional spectrum — men who had disagreed about nearly everything during their parliamentary careers — found common ground in their rejection of a government that had torn up the institutional framework within which their disagreements had taken place. These meetings were initially cautious and exploratory; they became progressively more coordinated as it became clear that the Karahan government was failing on its own terms and that the Sultan was unlikely to reverse course voluntarily.

Within the officer corps, opposition organised around Kemal Şahin, a colonel in the general staff whose analysis of the constitutional era's failures had led him not to sympathy with Karahan's program but to a distinctive alternative vision — an Ottoman nationalism that drew on the empire's own traditions rather than either European institutional models or Islamic governance frameworks. Şahin's coalition within the military was built carefully through 1916, drawing on the deep institutional hostility of the officer corps to a government that had sidelined everything the military's professional culture valued.

The Prussian partners' quiet economic pressure provided the coalition with a practical argument that supplemented its constitutional and ideological ones: the Karahan government was not merely wrong in principle, it was visibly failing in practice, and its failure was measurable in the declining revenues of the canal and the cooling of the Prussian relationship on which the empire's material position depended.

Şahin's most consequential decision in building the coalition was his approach to Şehzade Mehmed Ziyaeddin, eldest son of Sultan Mehmed V. A purely military coup risked the appearance of illegitimacy — a naked power grab with no authority beyond the force it commanded. Ziyaeddin provided dynastic legitimacy that transformed the action's political character. He had already attempted, through private argument with his father, to persuade Mehmed V to reverse course. Having failed, he was ready to consider alternatives.

The Coup and Its Aftermath

The restorative coup was executed in the early hours of a spring morning in 1917. Military units loyal to Şahin's coalition secured government buildings in Istanbul. Karahan was arrested at his residence without significant resistance — he received the officers who came for him with the composure of a man who had known for weeks that the end was approaching and had made his peace with it. Former parliamentary members were summoned to emergency session and formally reconvened the suspended lower chamber. Sultan Mehmed V, confronted by his son and the officers of the coalition, chose to abdicate rather than be deposed.

Ziyaeddin ascended the throne as Mahmud III, taking a regnal name that deliberately invoked the great reforming Sultan of the previous century. The 1883 constitution was reaffirmed and amended — most significantly by curtailing the Sultan's power to appoint a Grand Vizier without parliamentary confidence, closing the mechanism Mehmed V had used to install Karahan.

Karahan was tried by military tribunal on charges related to the unconstitutional suspension of parliament and the misuse of his appointment authority. He was convicted and sentenced to internal exile in a provincial Anatolian town, where he spent the remainder of his life under house arrest. He was not executed — a deliberate decision by Şahin's government that reflected both a genuine reluctance to make him a martyr and a recognition that his scholarly following in Anatolian religious circles made his death more politically dangerous than his exile.

Historical Assessment

The Karahan Interlude occupies a distinctive place in Ottoman historiography. Its brevity — less than eighteen months — and its unambiguous failure have made it easy to dismiss as an aberration, a moment of crisis-induced irrationality that the empire's constitutional institutions were ultimately strong enough to reverse. More careful assessments have noted that it was not aberrational at all: it was the product of real failures in the constitutional era's modernisation project, real resentments in the religious establishment and the Anatolian provincial population, and real weaknesses in the constitutional order's institutional design that allowed a Sultan to circumvent parliamentary confidence through his appointment powers.

Karahan himself, in the writings of his exile, developed an account of the interlude's failure that was more self-aware than his critics generally acknowledged. He did not recant his core convictions — he continued to believe that the constitutional era's project had been spiritually empty and that the empire needed governance rooted in Islamic tradition rather than European institutional models. But he acknowledged, with increasing explicitness in his later writings, that his government had not found the administrative form that such governance would require, and that the gap between the vision and the practical reality of governing a diverse, commercially integrated empire of the Ottoman Empire's scale had been wider than he had understood when he accepted the appointment.

This acknowledgment did not rehabilitate him in the eyes of those who regarded the interlude as a catastrophe. It did make him a more interesting and more honest figure than the simple villain of the restorative coup's founding narrative.
