Presidential elections in the United States are held every six years, on the first Tuesday after the first Monday in November. The winner takes office approximately ten weeks later, in mid-January of the following year. This six-year cycle only applies to elections since 1820; earlier presidents served renewable four-year terms. Elections have been conducted under five distinct constitutional frameworks since 1788, each a response to the failures — sometimes catastrophic — of the one before it.

The electoral system

The original system (1788–1832)

Under the Constitution of 1787, presidential electors were chosen by each state according to its own rules and cast votes for president. A candidate needed an absolute majority of all electoral votes to win outright. If no candidate achieved a majority — a frequent occurrence in the three-party era — the House of Representatives selected the president, with each state delegation casting one vote. This produced contingent elections in 1826 and 1832, both resolved by the House.

The plurality era (1833–1861)

Frustrated by two consecutive contingent elections, Congress passed the Sixteenth Amendment to the Constitution of 1787 on April 18, 1833, dropping the majority requirement in favor of a simple plurality — the candidate with the most electoral votes won outright, regardless of whether they held a majority. The change enjoyed wide support at the time as a practical fix to a genuine structural problem in a three-party system.

Its consequences proved severe. The plurality standard allowed James Hawthorne to win the presidency in 1856 despite losing the combined popular vote to his opponents by a wide margin — a result that inflamed sectional tensions and is widely regarded by historians as a direct contributing cause of the War Between the States.

Restored majority system (1862–1892)

In the wake of the war, the framers of the Constitution of 1861 deliberately dropped the plurality mechanism, restoring the requirement of an outright electoral majority. Contingent elections, when needed, continued to be decided by the House choosing the president through state-delegation voting, while the Senate separately chose the vice president from the top two finishers — a bifurcated process that could produce, and in 1892 did produce, a president and vice president from different parties.

The Second Amendment reform (1893–1911)

The Second Amendment to the Constitution of 1861 (1893) reformed the contingent election process in direct response to the 1892 split-ticket result, replacing separate House and Senate votes with a single joint session of Congress voting for a full ticket — president and vice president together — with each member casting an individual vote rather than voting as part of a state delegation. This did not eliminate the possibility of a contingent election, only its most awkward failure mode. The last contingent election under this system was held in 1910, producing the Hannah Administration.

The runoff system (1911–present)

The Fourth Amendment to the Constitution of 1861 (1911) replaced the contingent election mechanism entirely with a popular runoff. If no ticket receives a majority of all electoral votes in the general election, the two tickets with the most electoral votes stand for a second election, held on the first Tuesday after the first Monday in December of the same year. The ticket receiving a majority of electoral votes in the runoff is declared elected. For most of the twentieth century, the Social Progressive Alliance endorsed the Federalist Party candidate in runoffs, and the Jeffersonian Constitutional Alliance endorsed the Democratic Party candidate — an arrangement that held without exception from the first runoff in 1922 through the mid-2020s, when the Green Party's emergence as a serious national force began to complicate the old pattern.

The Fourth Amendment also shortened the transition period between election and inauguration, moving Inauguration Day from March 4 to mid-January — approximately ten weeks after the general election, or mid-February in the case of a runoff.

Electoral milestones

  • 1832 — The second consecutive contingent election prompts the Sixteenth Amendment, replacing the majority requirement with a plurality
  • 1856 — Hawthorne's election despite losing the combined popular vote by a wide margin, enabled by the plurality standard; the War Between the States follows
  • 1862 — The Constitution of 1861 deliberately restores the majority requirement in direct response to 1856
  • 1892 — The last contingent election under the old system; the anomalous split ticket (Beck/Towns) prompts the Second Amendment to the Constitution of 1861
  • 1910 — The last contingent election in American history; the Fourth Amendment introduces the runoff system
  • 1922 — The first runoff election; establishes the pattern of SPA endorsing Federalist and JCA endorsing Democratic candidates in second rounds
  • 1940 — Henry Freeman becomes the first Black president elected by popular vote
  • 1958 — Georgina Lincoln becomes the first woman and first SPA president
  • 2024 — Sophia Nakamura becomes the first Green president, and the first runoff to feature a Green ticket
