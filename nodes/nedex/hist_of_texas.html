History of the Texas Federation
Chronicle of development from 1836 to the present era

The history of the Texas Federation spans from its founding declaration of independence on January 3rd, 1844, through its emergence as a mature federal republic and member of the North American Conference by the mid-twentieth century.

Founding and the Second Mexican War, 1844-1847

The Texas Federation was born out of necessity. When the Mexican Empire declared its intention to "restore order" in the territories of the collapsing Mexican Republic in December 1843, the leaders of the Texas Free State and the Province of Texas faced a stark choice. On January 3rd, 1844, Chief Executive Stephen Austin of the Texas Free State and Provincial Governor Esteban Herrera jointly declared the independence of the Texas Federation. The two territories would govern their respective regions separately for the duration of the war, with a unified constitutional convention to follow.

The war against the Mexican Empire lasted three years. Texian forces held the Rio Grande line through the spring of 1844 before American entry into the conflict following the sinking of the S.S. Bostonian in August 1845 shifted the balance decisively. The Second Treaty of New Orleans, signed May 10th, 1847, formally recognized Texian independence and assigned the former province of Nuevo Mexico to the Federation. The Texian constitutional convention convened that summer, adopting a federal constitution on September 8th, 1847. Elections were held in March 1848, and Esteban Herrera was elected the Federation's first president.

The Herrera and Austin Years, 1848-1866

The Federation's first two presidents set the terms of Texian political life for generations. Esteban Herrera, leader of the Reform Party, governed cautiously, prioritizing institutional stability and national unity over bold reform. His reluctant signing of the Runaway Slave Act of 1848 — which required Texian authorities to assist in the return of escaped slaves from Austin state — was the defining controversy of his single term.

Stephen Austin, who succeeded Herrera in 1854 as leader of the Democratic-Nationalist Party, governed with considerably more ambition across two full terms. His 1863 compromise abolishing slavery through compensated manumission resolved the Federation's deepest constitutional tension, if imperfectly. He secured French investment for the transcontinental railroad, conducted the first state visit by a Texian head of government to Franklin, District of Washington, and maintained strict neutrality during the American War Between the States despite considerable pressure. Austin left office in 1866 and died that September at the age of seventy-three.

The Railroad Era, 1866-1890

The decades following Austin's presidency were defined by the construction and completion of the transcontinental railroad linking Galvezport to San Francisco. Presidents Edward Marsh and James Whitmore presided over this era, with Whitmore driving the ceremonial golden spike at El Paso in 1881 — a moment widely regarded as marking Texas's arrival as a mature economic actor within the North American order. The railroad transformed the Federation's western states, opened new markets, and accelerated immigration from the United States and Europe, particularly from the German communities of Brazos state.

The benefits of this growth were unevenly distributed, however, and Reform candidate Rodrigo Vásquez won the presidency in 1884 on a platform of labor rights and economic fairness. His single term delivered meaningful if incomplete reforms before the DNP reasserted itself in 1890 under Samuel Reeves.

The Reeves Era and the Voss Affair, 1890-1908

Samuel Reeves presided over two prosperous terms as the most business-friendly president since Austin, deepening Texian commercial ties with the United States and the Caribbean. His legacy was permanently damaged by the Voss Affair. When the Caribbean War of 1905–1906 exposed the Matanzas Clique, investigations revealed that Reeves's Commerce Secretary, Harlan Voss, had maintained deep financial entanglements with the Clique's network of contracts and bribes stretching back to at least 1893. The scandal consumed the presidency of Reeves's successor, Thomas Aldrich, who had no personal connection to the affair but could not escape its shadow. Reform candidate Ernesto Salinas swept the 1908 election on a clean government platform.

The Progressive Era, 1908-1920

Ernesto Salinas is widely regarded as the most consequential Texian president of the twentieth century. His two terms expanded workers' rights, established a federal public health initiative, invested seriously in public education in the western states, and delivered the first meaningful federal protections for the formerly enslaved population of Austin state. His foreign policy gave Texas a distinct voice within the North American Conference framework and established the first coherent Texian policy toward the Mexican Empire, whose chronic instability had long been a source of border anxiety.

The Interwar Period, 1920-1932

The DNP returned to power in 1920 under Arthur Donnelly, a moderate who had publicly supported elements of Salinas's clean government reforms. His presidency ended abruptly when he died of a heart attack on March 12th, 1923, at the age of sixty-one. His Vice President, George Farwell of Colorado state, assumed the presidency and won election in his own right in 1926. Farwell's nearly decade-long tenure was defined by steady economic growth, expanding North American integration, and a significant expansion of the Texian military as global instability mounted.

The Global War, 1932-1940

Marcos Delgado, a Nuevo Mexico senator and the dominant Reform figure of his generation, won the 1932 election as the first signs of the Global War became visible. His two terms were defined entirely by that conflict — deepening the Federation's commitment to the Allied framework, managing the border with an increasingly unstable Mexican Empire, and mobilizing the Texian economy and military for war. He was reelected in 1938 and governed through the Treaty of Geneva in 1939 and the Asian ceasefire in 1940, leaving office as the president who had brought Texas through the defining crisis of its first century.
