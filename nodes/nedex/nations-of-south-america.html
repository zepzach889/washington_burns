South America is home to three major powers — the Empire of Brazil, the Argentine Republic, and the Republic of Colombia — whose histories are treated in separate articles. The remaining nations of the continent are surveyed here. All maintained neutrality during the Global War (1932–1939) alongside most of Latin America, and all are members of the Global Peace Council as regular members.

Uruguay

The Oriental Republic of Uruguay (República Oriental del Uruguay) is a small republic on the eastern bank of the Río de la Plata, bordered by the Empire of Brazil to the north and the Argentine Republic to the west. Its capital is Montevideo.

Uruguay was created in 1828 as an independent buffer state following a war between Brazil and Argentina over the territory known as the Banda Oriental, with British diplomatic mediation producing a negotiated settlement. Neither Brazil nor Argentina was entirely satisfied with the arrangement, and Uruguay's position as a contested buffer state defined its subsequent political history.

The chronic civil conflict between the Colorado and Blanco political factions — representing, broadly, the urban liberal constituencies of Montevideo and the conservative rural interior respectively — made Uruguay a persistent flashpoint for regional instability through the mid-nineteenth century. Brazilian intervention on behalf of the Colorados during the crisis of the 1860s proved decisive; the resolution left Uruguay broadly aligned with Brazilian interests. The Colorado Party has governed continuously since that settlement, maintaining a stable if not entirely autonomous republic under Brazilian patronage. Uruguay retains genuine formal sovereignty, its internal governance its own, its foreign policy within the gravitational field of its larger neighbor.

Paraguay

The Republic of Paraguay (República del Paraguay) is a landlocked republic in the heart of South America, bordered by the Empire of Brazil to the east and northeast, the Argentine Republic to the south and southwest, and Bolivia to the northwest. Its capital is Asunción.

Paraguay's political development was shaped by a succession of strong executives who maintained the country's independence through diplomatic caution, economic protectionism, and military preparedness. Under Francisco Solano López, who assumed the presidency in 1862, Paraguay made the strategically astute decision to align diplomatically with Brazil during the War of the Río de la Plata — providing logistical cooperation in exchange for Brazilian guarantees of Paraguayan sovereignty and resolution of longstanding border disputes. Paraguay emerged from the regional crisis of the 1860s with its independence confirmed and its international standing modestly enhanced.

As his health declined in the late 1870s, López broke with dynastic tradition by appointing General Bernardino Caballero as his successor rather than a family member. Caballero's decade in power introduced legislative elections, professionalized the civil service, and founded the Partido Colorado as the organized expression of the López legacy. The first genuinely contested presidential election, which Caballero won and then honored by not seeking re-election, established the norm of electoral succession — an achievement without precedent in Paraguayan history. By the turn of the twentieth century Paraguay had achieved a competitive if imperfect republic and participated in the International Peace Council of 1907 as a recognized sovereign state.

Venezuela

The Republic of Venezuela (República de Venezuela) is a republic on the northern coast of South America, bordered by Colombia to the west, the Empire of Brazil to the south, and British Guiana to the east, with a long Caribbean coastline to the north. Its capital is Caracas.

Venezuela was the first successor state to emerge from the dissolution of Gran Colombia in 1830, its separation driven by the regionalist sentiment of its landowning elite and the political ambitions of General José Antonio Páez, who became the new republic's first president. The early decades were marked by caudillo politics, recurring civil conflict, and the dominance of regional strongmen. By the latter half of the nineteenth century Venezuela had developed a recognizable if imperfect republican framework, its politics dominated by competing Liberal and Conservative factions. Venezuela's considerable natural wealth — cattle, coffee, and cacao exports — supported a modest prosperity unevenly distributed and frequently disrupted by political instability.

Ecuador

The Republic of Ecuador (República del Ecuador) is a republic on the northwestern coast of South America, bordered by Colombia to the north and Peru to the east and south, with a Pacific coastline to the west. Its capital is Quito.

Ecuador separated from Gran Colombia in 1830, its independence reflecting the geographic and cultural distinctiveness of the Quito region. The new republic inherited a society marked by sharp inequalities between the Spanish-descended elite, a large indigenous population concentrated in the Andean highlands, and a smaller Afro-Ecuadorian population on the coastal lowlands. Ecuador's political history followed the familiar Andean pattern of Liberal/Conservative oscillation, recurring conflict over the role of the Church, and the persistent difficulty of governing a geographically fragmented country. The coastal lowlands, oriented toward Pacific trade and export agriculture, developed a more commercially dynamic and Liberal political culture than the Conservative highland interior — a division producing persistent internal tension. By the early twentieth century Ecuador had achieved a degree of political stability under Liberal governments.

Peru

The Republic of Peru (República del Perú) is a republic on the western coast of South America, bordered by Ecuador and Colombia to the north, the Empire of Brazil and Bolivia to the east, and Chile to the south, with a long Pacific coastline to the west. Its capital is Lima.

Peru was among the last South American territories to achieve independence from Spain, its liberation completed in 1824 through the campaigns of Simón Bolívar and Antonio José de Sucre. The colonial legacy was particularly deep: Peru had been the seat of the most important Spanish viceroyalty in South America, and the transition to republican governance was correspondingly difficult. A large indigenous population largely excluded from political life coexisted uneasily with a coastal creole elite whose republican institutions rested on narrow social foundations.

The defining catastrophe of Peru's nineteenth century was its defeat in the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), in which Chile seized Peru's nitrate-rich province of Tarapacá and occupied Lima for more than two years. The war left Peru economically devastated and politically traumatised, its treasury stripped, its best warships lost, and its national confidence shattered. Recovery was slow, the country burdened by debt and consumed by political struggles between factions that blamed each other for the defeat. By the early twentieth century Peru had achieved greater stability, its coastal economy gradually recovering, and participated in the International Peace Council of 1907.

Bolivia

The Republic of Bolivia (República de Bolivia) is a landlocked republic in the heart of South America, bordered by Peru and Chile to the west, the Empire of Brazil to the north and east, and the Argentine Republic and Paraguay to the south. Its administrative capital is La Paz; its constitutional capital is Sucre. Bolivia is named in honor of Simón Bolívar, who oversaw its independence in 1825.

Bolivia's geography — high Andean plateau, tropical lowlands, and until the late nineteenth century a narrow Pacific coastal strip — shaped its political and economic development in fundamental ways. The country's considerable mineral wealth, particularly silver and later tin, has been the foundation of its export economy and the source of recurring political conflict.

The catastrophic event of Bolivia's nineteenth century was its defeat in the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), in which Chile seized Bolivia's entire Pacific coastal territory — the nitrate-rich Atacama province — leaving Bolivia permanently landlocked. The loss was devastating not only economically, stripping Bolivia of its principal port and its share of the nitrate wealth, but psychologically, generating a national grievance that has defined Bolivian foreign policy ever since. Recovering meaningful sovereign access to the sea became the organizing obsession of Bolivian statecraft in the decades following the war — a goal that brought Bolivia into tension with Paraguay over the disputed Chaco region as an alternative route to Atlantic commerce.

Chile

The Republic of Chile (República de Chile) is a republic occupying a long, narrow strip of territory along the western coast of South America, bordered by Peru to the north, Bolivia to the northeast, and the Argentine Republic to the east, with the Pacific Ocean to the west. Its capital is Santiago.

Chile has been among the most politically stable republics in South America, its constitutional order established relatively early and maintained with greater consistency than most of its neighbours. The country's geographic isolation — bounded by the Atacama Desert to the north, the Andes to the east, the Pacific to the west, and Patagonia to the south — gave it a degree of insulation from the regional conflicts that destabilised its neighbours, and its relatively homogeneous creole society produced a political culture more inclined toward constitutional procedure than caudillo politics.

The defining achievement of Chile's nineteenth century was its decisive victory in the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), in which Chile defeated the combined forces of Bolivia and Peru, seizing Bolivia's entire Pacific coastal territory and Peru's nitrate-rich province of Tarapacá. The war transformed Chile's economic position almost overnight, the nitrate revenues funding a generation of infrastructure investment, military modernisation, and institutional development that consolidated Chile's position as the dominant power on South America's Pacific coast. The victory was not without complications: Chilean occupation of Peruvian territory for more than two years generated lasting bitterness in Lima, and Bolivia's permanent landlocked condition created an irredentist grievance shaping regional diplomacy for generations. Chile attended the International Peace Council of 1907 as one of South America's most significant states.
