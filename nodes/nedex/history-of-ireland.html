History of Ireland
From the Act of Union (1800) to the Global War (1934–1940)

The history of the Republic of Ireland spans from the incorporation of Ireland into the United Kingdom by the Act of Union of 1800 through the nineteenth century struggle for independence and the establishment of the republic in 1904, to the republic's entry into the Global War alongside the United States in 1934.

Ireland Under the Union

The Act of Union of 1800 incorporated Ireland into the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. Irish members of parliament sat at Westminster throughout the nineteenth century, and a tradition of constitutional nationalist politics — working within the British parliamentary system to achieve greater Irish autonomy — developed alongside a more radical republican underground.

The Irish Famine of the late 1840s, caused by the arrival of the potato blight in Ireland in 1847–48, proved a defining catastrophe for the island. Approximately half a million people died of starvation and disease, with similar or greater numbers emigrating in the years that followed. Though the British government's response was meaningfully better than it might otherwise have been — the Poor Relief Reform Act of 1834 had already established a national relief infrastructure — the response fell well short of what the crisis demanded. Anti-Irish cultural contempt, administrative indifference, and the distraction of the Russo-Ottoman War (1849–1851) all contributed to a relief effort that the Irish knew could have done more. Continued emigration in the decades after the Famine left Ireland with a population of approximately five and a half to six million by the end of the nineteenth century. The Famine permanently marked Irish collective memory and gave the nationalist movement a lasting and pointed grievance: not that Britain lacked the tools to help, but that it chose not to use them fully.

The Constitutional Nationalist Era (1850s–1897)

In the decades following the Famine, constitutional nationalism — the pursuit of Irish autonomy through parliamentary means — remained the dominant strand of Irish political life. Irish MPs at Westminster organised into a disciplined bloc, extracting concessions on land reform and tenant rights from successive British governments. The movement reached its peak under Cathal O'Brien, a barrister-politician who built the cause of Home Rule into a serious national and international question through the 1880s and early 1890s.

Simultaneously, the Gaelic Republican Brotherhood (GRB) operated as a clandestine republican organisation, publishing nationalist literature, maintaining networks among the Irish diaspora abroad, and waiting for an opportunity that constitutional politics seemed unlikely to deliver.

That opportunity came in 1897, when a Home Rule bill secured passage through the House of Commons only to be decisively rejected by the House of Lords. The defeat was fatal to constitutional nationalism's credibility. O'Brien, broken by the reversal, effectively retired from public life. Moderate nationalists who had spent careers working within the British system concluded that the system would never deliver meaningful change. The centre of gravity in Irish political life shifted decisively toward the GRB and the republican movement.

The Uprising (1900–1904)

The political climate in London made the coming confrontation more likely rather than less. In 1899, the British Tory leader Jonathan Oswald — who had previously served as Prime Minister from 1884 to 1896 — returned to 10 Downing Street, replacing the defeated Liberal government. Oswald was vehemently opposed to any devolution of power in Ireland, a stance that left the question of Home Rule with no remaining avenue for peaceful resolution.

The Irish Home Rule Uprising began in 1900, when the GRB — now drawing on a broad coalition of radicalised former moderates — coordinated rebel actions against Oswald's government. The city of Galway fell under sustained rebel control, even as the eastern part of the island initially remained in British hands; the scale and coordination of the rebellion demonstrated that the independence movement had genuine popular support beyond a hardened republican fringe.

Women for the Republic, organised and led by Brigid Halloran, played a critical role throughout the uprising — providing organisational infrastructure, intelligence networks, and sustained public agitation that kept political pressure on the British government even as military operations continued.

Oswald was succeeded as Prime Minister by fellow Tory Peter Hampton in 1901, but the change in leadership did not initially alter British policy. By 1902, Queen Charlotte I had died and been succeeded by Charlotte II, who was publicly sympathetic to Irish self-determination and applied sustained pressure on Hampton's government to seek a settlement. The Hampton government introduced a revised Home Rule bill in 1904 offering significant autonomy within the British imperial framework. Irish leaders rejected the offer outright and reaffirmed the republic. Hampton's government collapsed on a vote of no confidence, and the subsequent general election swept the British People's Party into power for the first time under Jason McBride.

McBride moved quickly to recognise Irish independence. Negotiations in 1903–1904, led on the Irish side by Thomas Healy, produced a settlement that recognised the Republic of Ireland over the whole island. At British insistence, the settlement included strong constitutional protections for Ireland's Protestant minority community, primarily concentrated in Ulster — provisions the Irish republican leadership accepted in exchange for swift and unambiguous recognition. McBride formally recognised the Republic of Ireland in late 1904.

The Early Republic (1904–1919)

The first years of the republic were dominated by the work of consolidation — building state institutions, establishing the civil service and national army, implementing land reform, and settling the provincial assembly framework established by the constitution. Éamon Brennan, the military architect of the uprising, became the republic's first Taoiseach, leading the dominant Republican Party through three successive parliamentary victories (1904, 1909, 1914). Thomas Healy, the constitutional architect of independence, served as the republic's first President, bringing both charisma and international credibility to the new state's early years.

Ireland made its international presence felt quickly. It sent delegates to the First International Peace Council in London in 1907 and participated in the first World Olympic Games in Athens in 1910.

Healy was succeeded in the presidency by Diarmait Ó'Sullivan, a Cork-born protégé who maintained Republican dominance through the consolidation phase. Brennan continued as Taoiseach, winning a third parliamentary term in 1914, though the Republican Party's grip on Irish politics was beginning to loosen. The Irish Progressive Party, drawing support from the urban professional class and the modernising wing of Irish society, built steadily through the 1910s.

The Progressive Era (1919–1933)

In 1918, the IPP's Desmond Fitzgerald won the presidency — the first non-Republican to hold the office — signalling a broader political shift. Brennan stepped down as Taoiseach following the 1919 parliamentary elections, in which the IPP emerged as the largest party for the first time. Liam Bourke became Taoiseach, beginning more than a decade of IPP-led government.

Bourke's government pursued a modernising agenda — normalising relations with Britain, expanding Ireland's international trade relationships, and gradually extending the social welfare provisions of the state. In 1921, Bourke called an early parliamentary election to strengthen his mandate, winning a larger majority. The IPP held the presidency again in 1925, with Ciarán Molloy winning a close election against a resurgent Republican Party.

The late 1920s brought increasing political difficulty. The 1926 parliamentary elections saw the IPP slip, forcing Bourke to bring the Irish Workers' Party into government as a junior coalition partner. The arrangement proved uncomfortable, and the coalition's parliamentary strength continued to erode through the 1931 elections. In 1932, with the Global War beginning in Europe, Irish voters returned the presidency to the Republican Party, electing Seamus Cahill — an intellectual who had made a philosophical case for Irish sovereignty and neutrality — on a platform of keeping Ireland out of the conflict. In 1933, a vote of no confidence brought down Bourke's coalition, and Declan Forde formed a Republican–Provincial Union government.

The Global War (1934–1940)

Forde's government initially maintained Irish neutrality as the Global War expanded. In late 1933 and into 1934, the Prussian Alliance made a secret diplomatic approach to Dublin, offering guaranteed neutrality in exchange for Irish ports being denied to Allied shipping. The approach was not immediately rejected — a decision that would prove politically catastrophic when the contact leaked publicly.

Shortly after, a Prussian submarine attacked an Irish merchant vessel, killing Irish civilians. The combination of the secret Prussian contact and the killing of Irish sailors produced a political crisis that Forde's minority government could not survive. The opposition parties — the IPP, IWP, and Provincial Union — delivered an ultimatum: form a genuine four-party national unity government, or face an immediate vote of no confidence.

Ireland entered the Global War on the Allied side within weeks of the United States joining the conflict in 1934, framing its entry explicitly as solidarity with a coalition of democracies rather than a return to Britain's orbit. A national unity government was formed, with all four parties represented as equally as possible. Forde remained Taoiseach. Former Taoiseach Bourke returned to cabinet in a senior IPP role.
