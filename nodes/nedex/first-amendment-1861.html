First Amendment to the Constitution of 1861
Federal Districts Amendment · Ratified 1892
Contents
  1. Background
  2. Provisions
  3. Ratification
  4. Legacy
  5. See also

The First Amendment to the Constitution of 1861, ratified in 1892, defined the status of the federal districts of the United States and granted their citizens congressional representation, full constitutional rights, and the vote for president. Known as the Federal Districts Amendment, it was the first revision made to the Constitution of 1861 and the constitutional foundation for the modern three-district system.

Background

The establishment of the District of Manhattan in 1880 had created a constitutional anomaly: residents of the new district — like those of the District of Washington around Franklin — enjoyed no representation in Congress and no vote for president. With national attention drawn to New York Harbor by the impending completion of the Freedom Triumphant statue, the Society for Manhattan Voters launched a major protest and lobbying campaign in 1889 demanding that Congress and the President "do something." President Leeland Rowling, a constitutional scholar by training, concluded that only an amendment could fix the oversight, and drafted one in collaboration with Secretary General Thorne, Speaker Caldecott, and the congressional leadership. The proposal was released to the public in January 1890.

Provisions

The amendment formally defined what a "federal district" was; granted each district one senator and at least one representative, apportioned by population on the same basis as the states; guaranteed district citizens the full rights and protections enjoyed by the citizens of the several states, including the right to vote for president; and created a formalized process for establishing new districts — with the safeguard that no district could be carved from a state's territory without the consent of that state's legislature.

Ratification

The proposal was controversial: critics warned of cities effectively "seceding" from their states and of the revenue such separations might cost state governments, the consent clause notwithstanding. After months of debate, the amendment passed both houses of Congress on July 1, 1890 — allowing President Rowling to announce the achievement at the dedication of the Freedom Triumphant statue three days later. Ratification by the required 30 of 39 states took two more years, and by the election of 1892 the citizens of both Franklin and New York City voted for president and sent representatives to Congress for the first time.

Legacy

The amendment's machinery was used within four years of its ratification: the District of Dearborn, uniting the twin cities of Calumet, was established through its formal process in 1896 under President Colin Beck. Its representation provisions gave the districts genuine political weight — a senator from the District of Washington, James Fraser, would figure prominently in the constitutional debates of the following year — and its guarantees remain the basis of federal district citizenship today.
