Fourteenth Amendment
Constitution of the United States (1787) · Ratified December 20, 1817
Contents

The Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States was ratified on December 20, 1817, as part of the Grand Compromise of 1817. It limited the presidency to a single six-year term and prohibited any person from serving as president if the immediately preceding president had been from the same state. The amendment was one of three constitutional changes negotiated during the Crawford Conference of 1817 and represented a significant concession by the Democratic-Republican majority to Federalist demands for executive reform.

Background

The Fourteenth Amendment emerged from the broader political negotiations that followed the War of 1812. With the federal capital in ruins, the economy weakened, and regional tensions running high, President William Crawford convened congressional leaders in Philadelphia in the spring of 1817 to broker a series of compromises that would stabilise the government and address longstanding grievances across party lines.

Federalist members of Congress, particularly those from New England, had campaigned on elements of the Hartford Resolution, which called for — among other reforms — a limitation on the length of the presidential term. The existing system, which permitted a president to serve unlimited four-year terms, had drawn criticism as potentially enabling entrenched executive power. Federalists argued that a single, longer term would insulate the presidency from the pressures of perpetual campaigning while also ensuring regular turnover in the office.

Crawford agreed to support the single-term limitation as part of the broader compromise package, securing in return Federalist acquiescence on the capital relocation and the Wisconsin Treaty. His agreement to pledge personally that he would not seek reelection in 1820 was understood, at the time, as a condition of the amendment's passage through Congress — a pledge whose precise legal force would itself become a matter of significant controversy within just a few years. See The 1820 Controversy, below.

Provisions

The amendment contained two operative clauses. The first established that no person elected to the presidency could serve more than one term, with that term set at six years in duration — an increase from the existing four-year term. The second clause provided that no person could be elected president from the same state as the person who immediately preceded them in that office.

Ratification

The amendment passed both houses of Congress on September 22, 1817, and was transmitted to the states for ratification. Pennsylvania became the fourteenth state to approve the amendment on November 19, reaching the constitutional threshold required for the related Thirteenth Amendment (vice presidential succession) but leaving the Fourteenth still short. The Fourteenth Amendment itself received the required approval of three-fourths of the states on December 20, 1817, becoming part of the Constitution. The related Fifteenth Amendment, governing declarations of war and the admission of new states, would not achieve ratification until September 12, 1818.

The 1820 Controversy

The amendment's language produced an unexpected and immediate constitutional crisis at the very first election it governed. When the election of 1820 arrived, President Crawford shocked the nation by announcing that he would not be bound by his earlier pledge after all — not because he had reconsidered his decision not to seek reelection, but because he had concluded, on the advice of legal counsel, that the amendment's language only applied to the president elected in 1820 and afterward. Legal experts of the day broadly agreed with this reading: Crawford, having taken office in 1817 under the prior constitutional framework, was arguably free to seek a second term in 1820 had he chosen to, regardless of his earlier promise.

Crawford ultimately did not run again — but his public assertion that he could have, framed as a matter of legal right rather than personal restraint, was read by contemporaries as a calculated repudiation of the spirit of the bargain that had secured the amendment's passage. The announcement formalised the divide already building within the Democratic-Republican Party, with the party's Western faction organising as the new Democratic Party and the Southern faction organising as the Republican Party in time for the 1820 election — the schism that produced the three-party system of the following decades.

Legacy

The Fourteenth Amendment reshaped the rhythm of American political life during the remaining decades of the original Constitution. The six-year single term became a defining feature of the presidency through the era of the War Between the States, and was carried forward in modified form into the Constitution of 1861, which retained the single six-year term while superseding the same-state restriction and other provisions of the earlier document. Henry Clay, elected in 1820 as the first nominee of the newly formed Democratic Party, became the first president to serve a full term under the amendment's six-year framework.
