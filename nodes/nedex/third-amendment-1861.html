Third Amendment to the Constitution of 1861
The Sanders Amendment · Women's suffrage · Ratified 1900
Contents
  1. Background
  2. Provisions
  3. Ratification
  4. Legacy
  5. See also

The Third Amendment to the Constitution of 1861, ratified on July 1, 1900, and known as the Sanders Amendment, extended the right to vote to all American women. Its operative text declared that the right of citizens to vote "shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex," with Congress empowered to enforce the article by legislation.

Background

Women's suffrage had advanced state by state since the 1870s: by the late 1880s seven states — Washingtonia, Clark, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, New Hampshire, and Vermont — had given women the vote, and the 1886 Federalist convention had endorsed "suffrage by state." President Rowling, wary after the exhausting fight over the First Amendment, declined the federal route; it fell to his successor. Colin Beck pledged in his 1893 inaugural address that women would have the vote by the time he left office, and with the Moore Proposal ratified, he threw the administration behind a measure introduced by Senator Elias Sanders of Rhode Island, a Federalist of measured but progressive instincts.

Provisions

The Sanders Amendment prohibited the United States and every state from denying or abridging the vote on account of sex, and granted Congress the power to enforce the prohibition through appropriate legislation — a deliberately simple text that left no state-level carve-outs.

Ratification

The measure's path was long and contentious. Congressional debate began in earnest in 1897, propelled by rallies and petitions from the strengthening national suffrage movement; opposition came primarily from the Republican Party, joined by conservatives in both the Democratic and Federalist ranks. Congress approved the amendment in the summer of 1898 and sent it to the states. It was still six states short when Alexander Fleak — who pledged his support in his inaugural address — succeeded Beck in March 1899, and on July 1, 1900, the state of Jefferson became the 30th to ratify, completing adoption. Celebrations swept the country, folding into that year's Fourth of July festivities, and candidates in the autumn midterms scrambled to court the new electorate — all but the Republicans, most of whom still refused.

Legacy

Within a generation, women in professional and political life were unremarkable facts of American public affairs. The amendment's arc of firsts ran from Lynne Powell, the first female vice president (1923), through Patricia Perry, the first female Secretary General (1935), to Georgina Lincoln, the first female president (1959) — under whose administration the Civil Equality Amendment completed constitutionally what the Sanders Amendment had begun.
