The Union of European Republics (UER), known in French as the Union des Républiques Européennes and in German as the Union der Europäischen Republiken, is a communalist federal republic in western and central Europe, formed in 1904 from the simultaneous revolutionary collapse of France and the South German Confederation at the close of the Great European War. It is the successor state to both nations, though it explicitly rejected the national categories of its predecessors in favour of a post-national ideology of class solidarity and revolutionary internationalism.

The UER's history falls into four broad periods: the founding era under Jean Marchand (1904–1914), in which the new state struggled to consolidate itself against internal opposition and international isolation; the radical Holtz era (1916–late 1920s), in which Mathias Holtz imposed a sweeping ideological program that brought the UER to the edge of internal collapse; the post-Holtz constitutional settlement (late 1920s–1932), in which the state reformed itself into a more stable if still ideologically distinctive republic; and the Global War (1932–1940), in which the UER emerged as a major Allied power and achieved international recognition as the legitimate government of metropolitan France.

The UER has been a source of sustained ideological anxiety for conservative political movements in the United States and elsewhere since its founding, and its existence as a functioning communalist state has shaped international diplomacy throughout the twentieth century.

History

Foundation and the Marchand Era (1904–1914)

The Union of European Republics was born not from a planned revolution but from the simultaneous exhaustion of two warring societies. By 1904, five years of the Great European War had hollowed out the armies and governments of both France and the South German states. Communalist organisations, long suppressed but never destroyed, had built networks in the industrial cities through years of underground organising. When the French and Bavarian governments finally lost the capacity to hold the line at home, the revolutions came quickly and in parallel.

In the autumn of 1904, revolutionary delegates from France and the South German states convened and established the Union of European Republics, declaring it the successor government of both nations. The driving intellectual force behind the new state was Jean Marchand, a French theorist and organiser whose writings had shaped the communalist movement for two decades. Marchand became the UER's first First Delegate — the title the new state gave to its chief executive, a deliberate rejection of the monarchical and presidential titles of the old order.

Jean Marchand governed as a pragmatist by the standards of the communalist movement. He was ideologically committed but not reckless, and recognised that the UER's survival in its early years depended on establishing basic administrative competence rather than pursuing ideological purity. His most consequential early decision was the 1906 repudiation of all debts held by the pre-revolutionary French and South German governments — a move that sent shockwaves through European financial markets and caused a cascading crash of institutions in Great Britain and Prussia. The UER was not in a position to be compelled to honour those debts by force, and Marchand calculated correctly that the financial pain inflicted on rival powers was worth the diplomatic isolation it produced.

Jean Marchand died in 1914. He is remembered within the UER as its founding father and ideological touchstone — a figure whose moderation and pragmatism in the founding years gave the state the stability it needed to survive. Outside the UER, he is more ambivalently regarded: the architect of a state that alarmed conservative governments across the world, but also a figure who kept the revolution from consuming itself in its first decade.

The Juarez Interlude (1914–1916)

Marchand's chosen successor was François Juarez, a French-born organiser who had been among the UER's founding delegates and had served in senior administrative roles throughout the Marchand years. Juarez assumed the position of First Delegate following Marchand's death and moved to consolidate his position within the Congress of Delegates.

This section will be expanded to cover the Juarez period in more detail — his policy priorities, his relationship with the various factions within the Congress of Delegates, and the circumstances under which Mathias Holtz was able to displace him. The UER's participation in the Fifth International Peace Council in Rome in 1915, its first re-engagement with international diplomacy, belongs here.

Juarez was ousted in 1916, displaced by Mathias Holtz, whose faction had built sufficient support within the Congress of Delegates to force a change of leadership. The transition marked a sharp break in the UER's political direction.

The Holtz Era (1916–late 1920s)

Mathias Holtz came to power in 1916 with a radical program that went far beyond anything Jean Marchand had envisioned. Where Marchand had been a pragmatist, Holtz was an ideological absolutist, convinced that the UER's survival required the total dissolution of the national and cultural categories it had inherited from its predecessor states.

Holtz's program had four main pillars. First, he dissolved the internal administrative boundaries that had followed the old national and regional divisions, replacing them with new districts drawn without regard for language or cultural tradition. Second, he instituted a policy of religious suppression, targeting the Catholic Church in particular as an institutional rival to the state's authority. Third, he commissioned the creation of a constructed language — Europan — intended to replace French and German as the medium of official life, on the theory that a shared language would dissolve the national identities that he regarded as the UER's greatest internal threat. Fourth, he moved the capital from Paris to Strasbourg, a city on the Franco-German border that he regarded as symbolically neutral between the UER's two founding peoples.

The Holtz era also saw the UER withdraw from the International Peace Council framework and decline participation in the World Olympic Games, on the grounds that both institutions reflected the national-competitive order the UER had rejected.

Holtz's downfall came when he overreached. In the late 1920s, convinced that elements of the military leadership were plotting against him, he moved to purge the senior officer corps. The officers acted first. A military coup removed Holtz from power before the purge could be carried out. The precise circumstances of his removal — and his subsequent fate — remain a subject of historical dispute.

The Constitutional Crisis and Reform Settlement (late 1920s–1932)

The removal of Holtz left the UER in a profound constitutional crisis. Three factions contested the future of the state. The Restorationists argued for a fundamental revision of the UER's structure, including the restoration of some form of national autonomy for the French and German-speaking regions and a rollback of the most radical Holtzian policies. The Reformed Communalists sought to preserve the UER's communalist ideology and administrative structure while restoring the civil liberties and religious freedoms that Holtz had suppressed. The Holtzian Loyalists maintained that Holtz's program had been correct and that the coup represented a counter-revolutionary betrayal.

The settlement that emerged represented a victory for the Reformed Communalists, with significant concessions to the Restorationists. The UER retained its communalist ideology and the Holtzian administrative districts, but restored genuine elections, civil liberties, and religious freedom. French and German were reinstated as co-official languages alongside Europan, which was retained for official and ceremonial use but no longer imposed as the sole medium of public life. The capital was moved back to Paris.

The reformed UER re-engaged with international diplomacy in the early 1930s and rejoined the Peace Council framework. Internally, it remained politically divided — the three factions of the constitutional crisis had not dissolved, merely reached an accommodation — but it was stable enough to face the storm that was coming.

The Global War (1932–1940)

By 1932, the reformed UER faced an existential threat from Prussia, whose Nationalist Party government had built a program of revanchism around the claim that the German-speaking populations of the UER's South German territories were being held in a French-dominated state against their will. When Prussia formally demanded referenda in those territories in 1931 and the UER refused, the diplomatic situation deteriorated rapidly. In 1932, Prussian forces invaded the formerly German UER territories. The UER declared war.

This section will be expanded to cover the UER's military situation in the early years of the Global War, the internal political dynamics during wartime, and the UER's relationship with its British and eventually American allies.

The war lasted eight years. Despite its internal divisions, the UER proved a more resilient belligerent than Prussia had anticipated — the shared threat of Prussian invasion temporarily unified the Reformed Communalist and Restorationist factions, while even many Holtzian Loyalists concluded that Prussian victory would mean the UER's dissolution. By 1937, the military situation had shifted decisively in the Allies' favour. The Prussian democratic revolution of early 1938 brought the war in Europe to a close, and the formal European armistice was signed in the autumn of that year.

At the Treaty of Geneva (1939), the UER achieved its most important diplomatic victory since the founding: formal international recognition as the legitimate government of metropolitan France. The Corsican Bourbon claim to France was extinguished as a condition of the Algerian settlement. The UER also secured the return of formerly German territories that Prussia had occupied during the war, though the regional autonomy provisions negotiated at Geneva left those territories' populations imperfectly satisfied.

The UER emerged from the Global War more legitimate and more confident than it had entered it. Its survival against a major military power had demonstrated that the communalist experiment was not merely a revolutionary moment but a durable state. This only deepened the ideological anxiety it generated among conservative movements in the United States and elsewhere.

Government

The UER is governed under a communalist federal constitution adopted at its founding and substantially revised following the post-Holtz settlement of the late 1920s. The chief executive is the First Delegate, elected by the Congress of Delegates — the UER's unicameral legislature — for a fixed term. The Congress of Delegates is itself elected by the UER's adult population under a system of proportional representation by administrative district.

The UER's administrative districts were drawn under Mathias Holtz without regard for the old national or regional boundaries of France and the South German states, and were retained by the post-Holtz settlement as a matter of practical stability. This has remained a source of tension, particularly in regions with strong cultural or linguistic identities that cut across district lines.

Language

The UER officially recognises three languages. French and German are co-official languages of government, law, and public life. Europan, the constructed language commissioned under Mathias Holtz, is retained as an official language for ceremonial and certain administrative purposes and is taught in UER schools, but is not imposed as a medium of daily life. In practice, French predominates in the western districts and German in the eastern districts, with Europan occupying a symbolic rather than functional role in most contexts.

Demonym

In keeping with the UER's post-national ideology, no national demonym in the traditional sense has been officially adopted. The most common term in use is Union Citizen — rendered as citoyen de l'Union in French, Unionsbürger in German, and civitano de la Uniono in Europan. The term is used in official contexts and on travel documents, though in informal speech residents of the UER's French-speaking districts often simply say "French" and those in the German-speaking districts say "German" — a persistent reminder of the national identities the UER's founders sought to transcend.
