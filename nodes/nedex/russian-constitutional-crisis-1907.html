Russian Constitutional Crisis of 1907

Political upheaval and constitutional reform in the Empire of Russia

The Russian Constitutional Crisis of 1907 was a period of acute political instability in the Empire of Russia, precipitated by the Tsar's dissolution of the Imperial Duma in response to escalating industrial unrest and resolved through a negotiated constitutional settlement that significantly restructured the balance of power between the throne and the legislature. The crisis is generally regarded as the most dangerous moment in the history of the Russian constitutional order and the event most directly responsible for the reform settlement of 1908, which transformed Russia from a constitutional monarchy in which the throne retained decisive authority into one in which the legislature held meaningful and enforceable power.

Background

The Constitution of 1854, promulgated by Tsar Michael II, had established the Imperial Duma as Russia's legislature while preserving substantial monarchical authority. The tsar retained an absolute veto over all legislation, the Prime Minister was required to be drawn from the hereditary nobility of the Chamber of Peers, and the tsar retained meaningful discretion over the Prime Minister's appointment and dismissal. For the first several decades of the constitutional era, this arrangement functioned adequately, partly because the tsar and his ministers actively worked to make it do so, and partly because the Chamber of Deputies lacked the organizational coherence to press its claims forcefully.

By the early twentieth century, however, several forces had combined to put the existing arrangement under severe strain. Rapid industrialization had produced a large and growing urban working class concentrated in Saint Petersburg, Ivanovo, the Donbas mining region, and other industrial centers. Wages in many industries had failed to keep pace with the rising cost of living. Workers had no legal right to organize or strike. At the same time, the Labor and Socialist Party, led by Grigori Antonov, had spent years building organizational networks among industrial workers, and the Party of the Russian People had become an increasingly assertive force in the Chamber of Deputies, pushing for a broader franchise and reduced monarchical authority. Periodic smaller strikes and labor actions had occurred through the early 1900s without producing a decisive confrontation, but the underlying pressures continued to accumulate.

The Strikes of 1906

In 1906, strikes broke out roughly simultaneously among factory workers in Saint Petersburg and textile workers in Ivanovo. The actions were not initially centrally coordinated, arising from organic grievances over falling real wages, dangerous working conditions, and the absence of any legal mechanism for collective bargaining. The Labor and Socialist Party moved quickly to provide organizational structure and articulate demands that combined economic grievances — recognition of the right to organize, improved safety standards, wage increases — with explicit political demands, including the expansion of the franchise and reform of the tsar's veto powers.

The Party of the Russian People recognized in the strikes both an opportunity and a danger. Party leaders, including Alexei Lvov, concluded that the movement's energy had to be channeled into the constitutional arena or risk producing an outcome that the constitutional order could not absorb. The People's Party began working to bring the strikes' political demands into the Duma's reform agenda, positioning itself as the responsible interlocutor between the workers' movement and the government.

Through the autumn and winter of 1906, the strikes spread to the Donbas mining region and, critically, to railroad workers across multiple cities. The entry of railroad workers into the movement threatened the empire's transportation network and elevated the crisis from a significant labor dispute to a matter of national economic stability.

The Dissolution and Its Aftermath

Tsar Alexander III and his Prime Minister, Count Pyotr Valuev-Stroganov, concluded that the Duma's increasingly vocal support for the strikers' political demands represented an overreach that required a firm response. In early 1907, Alexander III dissolved the Duma by imperial decree, a power he retained under the 1854 constitution. The dissolution was intended as a demonstration of monarchical authority that would disperse the political momentum behind the reform movement and return the situation to manageable levels.

The effect was precisely the opposite. The dissolution was widely perceived as confirmation of the strikers' argument that the existing constitutional order was inadequate to protect ordinary Russians' interests. Protests spread rapidly beyond the industrial working class to broader urban populations in Saint Petersburg, Moscow, and other cities. Two assassination attempts were made against senior government officials in the weeks following the dissolution; neither succeeded, but the attempts intensified the atmosphere of crisis and drew extensive coverage in the international press.

Within months it was apparent that the dissolution had been a catastrophic miscalculation. Valuev-Stroganov, whose advice had been decisive in the decision, submitted his resignation at Alexander III's request in mid-1907. The tsar opened negotiations almost immediately thereafter, with Alexei Lvov of the Party of the Russian People emerging as the principal interlocutor on the reform side. Lvov's standing with both the reform movement and the tsar — he was a nobleman by birth who had spent his career in the Chamber of Deputies championing reform from within the system — made him an acceptable negotiating partner to all parties.

The Settlement

Negotiations between the tsar's representatives and the reform leadership produced an agreement finalized in late 1907 and implemented in early 1908. The settlement included the following provisions:

  • The tsar's absolute veto over Duma legislation was replaced with a suspensive veto, which the Chamber of Deputies could override by a specified supermajority.
  • The franchise was extended to universal male suffrage, significantly expanding the electorate and in particular bringing large numbers of urban working-class voters into the political system for the first time.
  • The requirement that the Prime Minister be drawn from the Chamber of Peers was abolished, opening the office to any citizen regardless of hereditary status.
  • The tsar's power to freely appoint and dismiss the Prime Minister was curtailed, making the Prime Minister more directly accountable to the Duma majority.

The tsar's dissolution power was not entirely abolished — Alexander III retained significant authority — but the balance of the constitutional order shifted meaningfully toward the legislature. Lvov became Prime Minister upon the settlement's conclusion and spent the following eight years normalizing the new arrangements.

Legacy

The crisis and its resolution are widely regarded as the pivotal moment in Russian constitutional history. The settlement of 1908 transformed the practical relationship between the throne and the legislature without eliminating the monarchy or producing the kind of revolutionary rupture that characterized political upheavals elsewhere in Europe during this period. Grigori Antonov and the Labor and Socialist Party claimed partial credit for the outcome, arguing correctly that it was the workers' movement that had made the settlement necessary; critics within the LSP argued, also correctly, that the People's Party had captured the movement's energy for its own ends and delivered a settlement that fell well short of structural transformation. The debate between these positions shaped left-wing Russian politics for a generation.

Tsar Alexander III's role in the crisis is debated among historians. His decision to dissolve the Duma is universally regarded as a serious error; his subsequent willingness to negotiate rather than escalate, and to accept a settlement that genuinely reduced his own authority, is generally credited as the decision that saved the constitutional monarchy.
