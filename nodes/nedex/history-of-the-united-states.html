This article covers the political and national history of the United States from the colonial era to the present day, organised around the major periods of American political development. For individual presidencies, see Presidents of the United States.

Colonial Era and Independence (to 1787)

The thirteen British colonies of the Atlantic seaboard declared independence on July 4, 1776, and secured it through the Revolutionary War. After a brief and unsatisfactory period of loose confederation, the Constitution of 1787 established a federal republic with an elected president, a bicameral Congress, and a national judiciary. George Washington, commander of the Continental Army, was elected the first president, and his decision to relinquish power voluntarily set the young republic's most enduring precedent.

The Early Republic (1787–1817)

The first decades under the new constitution saw the emergence of organised political parties and the steady westward growth of the republic. Mounting friction with Britain over maritime rights drew the country into the War of 1812, the conflict that gave the era its defining catastrophe: in 1814, British forces captured and burned the unfinished capital of Washington City, a national humiliation compounded by an unfavourable peace. The war's aftermath discredited the existing political order and produced a constitutional crisis over the future of the presidency, the capital, and the Union itself.

The Grand Compromise Era (1817–1857)

President William Crawford's Grand Compromise of 1817 resolved the postwar crisis with a sweeping constitutional settlement: three amendments — most consequentially the single six-year presidential term — and the relocation of the national capital to a new federal district on the Ohio River. The government took up residence in Franklin in 1827. The following decades brought explosive westward expansion, the plurality-election controversies of the 16th Amendment period, and the loss of the far west, as Texas and California established themselves as independent republics in the 1840s and Mexico fought the Second Mexican War to a continental settlement. Above all, the era was defined by the deepening sectional conflict over slavery, which successive compromises managed but never resolved.

The War Between the States and Refounding (1857–1862)

The crisis of 1857 shattered the Union. The War Between the States (1857–1860) saw the republic fracture into three rival governments, and widened further in 1859 when President Hawthorne's administration declared war on Britain after the sinking of the S.S. Bombay, bringing the Royal Navy into the conflict on the side of the Free States. The victory of the Free State cause under Acting President Hugo Brandt destroyed the slave power, and the Negro Emancipation Act of 1860 ended slavery on American soil. The Constitution of 1861 refounded the republic — creating the office of Secretary General, abolishing the electoral college, and cementing the six-year presidency — while the Yates Treaty of the same year laid the foundation of the modern Anglo-American relationship. In 1862, Kanasaw and Gigadohi entered the Union as the first Native-majority states.

Reconstruction and the Continental Era (1862–1899)

Reconstruction of the defeated South proceeded alongside the physical binding of the continent: the transcontinental railroad, mass settlement of the interior, and a steady procession of new states that brought the Union to its present 39 states with the admission of Clark in July 1882. The federal districts of Manhattan (1880) and Dearborn (1896) joined the District of Washington, and the First Amendment of 1892 secured their representation in Congress. Reconciliation with Britain matured into genuine friendship, marked by the dedication of the Freedom Triumphant statue in 1890 and President Rowling's celebrated state visit to London in 1891 — the first by a sitting president.

The Reform Era (1899–1932)

The turn of the century opened a generation of democratic reform. The Third Amendment extended the vote to all American women in 1900; three years later, Vice President Peter Landon succeeded to the presidency as the first Black man to hold the office. The Matanzas War (1905–1906) under Cornelius Roosevelt II ended Spanish rule in the Caribbean and established the independent republics of Cuba and Puerto Rico, while the Fourth Amendment of 1911 replaced the old plurality mechanics with the modern runoff system, entrenching multi-party democracy. The United States remained aloof from the Great European War and prospered through the 1920s, but the deteriorating international order of the early 1930s pressed ever harder against American neutrality.

The Global War (1932–1940)

When the Global War erupted in 1932, the United States again declared neutrality — until Spain declared the Mediterranean a closed sea in 1933 and began attacking Allied-bound shipping, sinking several American vessels with significant loss of life. Congress declared war in early 1934, and President Leon Palmer, inaugurated the following year, led the Allied coalition through the war's decisive campaigns. The European armistice of autumn 1938 was formalised by the Treaty of Geneva (1939), which established the Global Peace Council with the United States as a founding Executive Board member; fighting in Asia ceased in late 1940 under a far murkier settlement whose consequences shadowed American politics for a generation.

The Postwar Era (1940–1958)

The 1940 election of Henry Freeman — the wartime Secretary General and the first Black president chosen by the American electorate — capped eighty years of constitutional promise. His term finalised the Asian settlement, established the Continental Defense Command (1942), and transformed wartime cooperation with America's neighbours into the North American Union in 1945; it was also consumed by an exhausting European peacekeeping commitment that opened the door to twelve years of Jeffersonian Constitutionalist government under James Nolan and Timothy Priest. Beneath the era's surface conservatism, its defining social fault line was emerging: the murder of the Episcopal priest who had sheltered the outed war veteran Jesse Pike ignited protest marches that founded the American gay rights movement.

The Second Reform Era (1959–1989)

The 1958 election of Georgina Lincoln — the first female president, heading the first all-female ticket in American history — opened three decades of transformation. Lincoln signed the Freedom to Love Act, announced the space program that became the continental Hermes Program, and governed through a durable SPA–Federalist coalition. Under Julius Langston, the NAU Assembly was established in 1973, and the Hermes lunar landing in Mare Imbrium arrived alongside the national bicentennial in 1976 — a joint North American triumph. The era culminated in the presidency of Jesse Pike himself, elected in 1982 as the movement symbol turned senior legislator, whose Civil Marriage Recognition Act of 1985 completed the arc that had begun with a murder four decades earlier.

The Multilateral Era (1990–2012)

The quarter-century that followed was defined by America's management of the international order it had built. Regina Holman, a scholar of multilateral institutions, deepened NAU and GPC integration; Victor Liu committed American forces to the GPC ceasefire-monitoring operation in Mesopotamia in 1996, an intervention whose moral wounds — above all the 1998 atrocity inside a GPC-protected zone — and economic strains dominated the politics of the turn of the century. Stephen Mercer, elected outright in 2000 as a referendum on both governing parties, brought the Mesopotamia commitment to a workable resolution and initiated the robotic Mars probe program; Eleanor Sinclair's steady institutionalism closed the era. Its unfinished business — above all climate policy, where carbon pricing repeatedly failed — was left to the generation that followed.

The Contemporary Era (2013–Present)

Xavier Hudson's presidency delivered a generational modernisation of the national rail network and completed the reform of the Global Peace Council. His successor, Naomi Clearwater, faced the defining foreign crisis of the era when the Chinese Spring of 2019 toppled the Zhongxing dynasty's old order; her restraint through that week, and her groundwork toward a formal NAU constitution, defined the period. Climate urgency and generational change then produced the political earthquake of 2024: the election of Sophia Nakamura, the first Green Party president, whose Climate Infrastructure Package became the party's first major legislative achievement. In 2026 the United States celebrates its semiquincentennial — 250 years of independence — as delegates prepare to draft the NAU constitution and a joint continental crew readies the first human voyage to Mars.
