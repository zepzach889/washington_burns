India is a major independent nation in South Asia, comprising the former territories of British India and the successor to the Dominion of India established in 1906. The Dominion — the culmination of more than half a century of Indian political organisation, reform agitation, and constitutional negotiation with London — was governed as a federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy under the British Crown from 1 January 1906 until full independence was achieved in 1963, when Britain honoured the wartime commitments it had made to Prime Minister Indrakant Vishwanath Sapru during the Global War.

Independent India immediately established a doctrine of non-alignment — refusing to anchor itself to either the North American Union, with whose stated values it had considerable sympathy, or the Union of European Republics, with whose social ideals there was ideological overlap. This genuinely independent posture gave India a distinctive role in the postwar international order, most visibly when New Delhi hosted the settlement negotiations of the Chosŏn War in 1967–1968, producing the Treaty of Delhi — India's proof of concept for what non-alignment meant in practice. India is a member of the Global Peace Council and holds a seat on its Executive Board.

Background: Company Rule and the Reform Era (1814–1880)

In the early decades of the nineteenth century, the East India Company consolidated its political and economic authority across the Indian subcontinent, establishing its presidency towns of Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras as the administrative and commercial hubs of British India. Early reformist governors oversaw a gradual expansion of English-medium education and Western-style legal codes, laying the foundation for a class of anglicised Indian elites who would, in time, press for political change. Urban Advisory Councils, composed of nominated Indian merchants, landowners, and professionals, were established in the major presidency towns as limited channels for Indian consultation on municipal affairs.

In 1848, discontent among sepoy troops in the Bengal Presidency — compounded by anxiety over land annexation policies, religious insensitivity, and the perceived indifference of Company administration to Indian interests — erupted into open revolt. Centred on Awadh, where Nawab Nasir ud-Din Ali Khan led a coalition of sepoy commanders and civilian resisters in sustained armed resistance, the uprising spread across the Bengal Presidency before being suppressed by British forces over the following eighteen months. Known to British authorities as the Bengal Mutiny and to Indian nationalist tradition as the Bengal Rising, the conflict was more geographically contained than it might otherwise have been: the Advisory Council system and early civil service access had given the anglicised Indian administrative class sufficient institutional investment to remain largely loyal. Nawab Nasir ud-Din Ali Khan was defeated in the field and subsequently exiled, but his active resistance gave Indian nationalist historiography a founding martyr of uncommon stature.

The shock of the Bengal Rising accelerated changes already under discussion in London. In 1855, Parliament formally dissolved the East India Company and transferred authority over India to the Crown, inaugurating the era of Crown Rule. The new administration pursued a more explicitly collaborative approach to governance: Provincial Councils were established in Bengal, Bombay, and Madras; the Indian Civil Service opened competitive examinations in Calcutta as well as London; and an Indian Officer Training Corps was formally established within the army. Over the following decades, these measures produced the educated Indian professional class — barristers, civil servants, physicians, journalists — that would form the political core of the Indian nationalist movement.

The Nationalist Movement (1880–1904)

By the early 1880s, the reform generation had organised itself into formal political associations. The Indian Reform League, founded in 1882, drew its membership primarily from the anglicised professional elite who believed that the path to Indian dignity ran through demonstrating competence within British institutions. The League's politics were those of loyalist gradualism — more Indian participation within the Empire, without directly challenging British sovereignty. Three years later, in 1885, the Indian National Congress was founded on a broader and more explicitly ambitious platform. Where the Reform League was the insider game — petitions, civil service participation, measured negotiation — the Congress was prepared for public agitation, mass mobilization, and the deliberate embarrassment of the British government when necessary.

The two organisations were often in tension but recognised their mutual dependence. When the British government of Prime Minister Jonathan Oswald resisted Home Rule agitation after 1899, the Reform League and the INC presented a unified front. On 10 June 1900, at Amritsar, their joint leadership issued the Amritsar Declaration of Rights, demanding an elected Indian legislature, control over internal affairs, and full Dominion status under the British Crown. The Declaration became the foundational document of the Indian Home Rule movement.

The Conservative government of Oswald's successor, Peter Hampton, initially resisted these demands. But the broader crisis of the British Empire in the early 1900s — Ireland in revolt, Japan in civil war, China under strain — had stretched London's capacity to maintain the status quo. Queen Charlotte II, who had succeeded her mother in 1902 and was instinctively more sympathetic to Indian aspirations than the Conservative government she had inherited, applied increasing pressure toward accommodation. When the Irish Home Rule Bill of 1904 was rejected by the Irish who proclaimed their republic outright, Hampton resigned. New elections swept the British People's Party to power under Prime Minister Jason McBride, who had campaigned explicitly on Indian Dominion status as part of a broader reformist imperial programme. Negotiations moved quickly. The Indian Dominion Act was passed by Parliament in 1905 and came into effect on 1 January 1906.

Dominion Status and the First Government (1906–1912)

The Indian Dominion Act established a bicameral Parliament — the elected Sabha as the lower house, the Parishad as an upper chamber with representation drawn from the provinces — and granted the Dominion full control over its domestic affairs. Foreign policy and defence remained formally with London, though an advisory relationship was enshrined in the Act. The princely states, whose rulers had treaty relationships with the Crown rather than with British India proper, were not incorporated into the Dominion's constitutional framework — a structural fault line that would define Indian parliamentary politics for decades.

The first government was formed by the Indian Reform League, whose long years of insider negotiation had made them the natural partners of McBride's BPP administration. The first Prime Minister of the Dominion of India was Sir Debendranath Chaudhuri (1906–1912), a Bengali Hindu barrister from Calcutta who had been among the principal architects of the Amritsar Declaration and whose London connections had proved indispensable in the final negotiations. Chaudhuri governed with careful institutionalism, treating the Dominion's early years as a demonstration that Indian self-governance was viable and responsible. His government expanded civil service participation, began construction of a national public school system, and established a National University at Delhi. He was criticised by the INC for excessive deference to London on the princely states question, but his defenders argued that consolidating the Dominion's institutions was the necessary first task.

The Later Reform League Era (1912–1922)

Chaudhuri was succeeded in 1912 by Sir Jehangir Manekji Wadia (1912–1918), a Parsi barrister from Bombay whose family had been among the city's most prominent commercial and civic dynasties for generations. Wadia's appointment signalled the Reform League's national breadth — not merely a Calcutta establishment party, but one that could draw on the Bombay Presidency's distinct legal and commercial tradition. His government oversaw the consolidation of the Dominion's administrative machinery and navigated the increasingly turbulent international situation as the European powers moved toward the conflict that would become the Global War. The death of Charlotte II in 1918 and the accession of Charlotte III — less instinctively sympathetic to Indian aspirations than her mother — subtly shifted the atmosphere of British-Indian relations.

Wadia was succeeded by Sir Jagannath Prasad Mathur (1918–1922), a Kayastha lawyer from the Mathura region of the United Provinces — the first Prime Minister drawn from the Hindi heartland. Mathur was a capable administrator who found himself governing as the structural foundations of Reform League dominance eroded beneath him. The All-India Muslim League, formally organised in this period, began pressing for guaranteed constitutional representation for India's Muslim population. The People's Party of India, representing industrial workers and the rural poor, agitated loudly in Bengal and Bombay. The INC, reorganising and broadening its coalition, pressed relentlessly on the princely states question. Mathur held the majority together through sheer administrative competence, but the 1922 elections ended Reform League majority governance permanently.

The Coalition Era (1922–1929)

The 1922 elections produced the result that INC strategists had long anticipated and Reform League veterans had long feared: the INC emerged as the largest single party in the Sabha for the first time, without an outright majority. After lengthy negotiations, Keshavrao Vithal Patil (1922–1924) was sworn in as the first INC Prime Minister — a non-Brahmin Marathi lawyer from Bombay whose rise through the Congress's mass mobilization machinery made him a pointed contrast to every Prime Minister who had preceded him. His minority government, sustained by the People's Party of India on confidence and supply, pushed further on princely states integration than any Reform League government had dared. It fell in 1924, brought down when the Muslim League withdrew its confidence over an integration measure the League regarded as insufficiently protective of Muslim-majority princely populations.

The Reform League regrouped. In coalition with the Muslim League, Sir Subramania Krishnaswami Iyer (1924–1929) — a Tamil Brahmin from Madras, the first southern Indian to serve as Prime Minister — formed a government that both parties understood was a rearguard action rather than a genuine revival. Iyer governed with personal distinction and cross-party respect, but the coalition's energy went primarily into managing its internal tensions rather than advancing a positive agenda. The coalition's most significant constitutional achievement was the Reserved Seats Settlement of 1926, which guaranteed Muslim League representation in the Sabha through a system of reserved constituencies — a landmark moment in Indian constitutional history that resolved the League's most pressing immediate demand. By 1929, with the INC having spent the coalition years broadening its base and deepening its organisational presence in the Hindi heartland, the Reform League's hold on government was exhausted. The Reform League has not governed alone since.

The INC Government and the Approach of War (1929–1932)

The 1929 elections returned the INC to government with a stronger mandate than in 1922. Indrakant Vishwanath Sapru (1929–), a Kashmiri Pandit lawyer from the United Provinces, became Prime Minister — the first whose political career had been built entirely within the INC's organisational structure rather than in the civil service or colonial legal establishment. Sapru's government set about the unfinished business of the Patil years: a renewed push on princely states integration, expansion of the public education system, and the construction of a more assertive Indian voice in the advisory relationship with London on foreign affairs.

The Global War (1932–1939) and Wartime Commitments

The Global War, which broke out in 1932 with Prussia's invasion of UER territories and China's simultaneous attack on Chosŏn and the British treaty ports, drew India into the conflict as a component of the British war effort. Indian forces served in both the European and Pacific theaters, their contribution substantial and their sacrifices significant. Prime Minister Sapru, understanding that wartime service created unprecedented leverage over London, used India's indispensable military role to extract explicit commitments from the British government regarding India's postwar constitutional position — commitments that went beyond anything the Dominion Act of 1906 had contemplated, and that pointed unambiguously toward full independence.

The Treaty of Geneva, signed in spring 1939, ended the European theater of the war. The Asian ceasefire followed in late 1940, with the formal Pacific settlement concluded in 1941. India's wartime grand coalition government — the broadest parliamentary alliance in the Dominion's short history — navigated these years with the specific objective of holding London to Sapru's wartime commitments when peace came.

Independence (1963)

The process of honoring those wartime commitments was neither swift nor smooth. Through the 1940s and 1950s, American diplomatic pressure — applied quietly but consistently by successive NAU administrations — complemented Indian political pressure in keeping London to its obligations. South Africa's evolution toward Commonwealth membership, and the broader transformation of British imperial relationships in the postwar decades, accelerated the timeline further.

Formal independence negotiations were concluded in 1961–1962, with the treaty ratified and India's full independence taking effect in 1963. Britain honoured the wartime commitments. India entered the Global Peace Council framework as a fully independent state — and immediately established its founding foreign policy doctrine: non-alignment. India would not be NAU-aligned, despite genuine American sympathy and material support during the independence process. It would not be UER-aligned, despite ideological overlap between the Indian socialist tradition and European communalism. It would be genuinely its own thing — a great power charting an independent course in a world still defined by the rivalries of the postwar settlement.

The Postwar Period and Non-Alignment

India's non-aligned doctrine proved its worth most dramatically in 1967–1968, during the settlement of the Chosŏn War. The war — triggered by a Chinese-backed coup against Chosŏn's constitutional government in 1966 — had been resolved militarily by the autumn of 1967, but required a settlement venue that all parties could accept. India was the only plausible neutral host: it had no military stake in the outcome, no alliance obligations to either the NAU or China, and a reputation for principled independence that no party to the conflict could credibly dispute.

The Treaty of Delhi, signed in February or March 1968, secured Chosŏn's independence under GPC-mandated monitoring and explicit security guarantees. India's role as broker established non-alignment not merely as a philosophical posture but as a genuine diplomatic capability — proof of concept for everything the doctrine claimed to be. India arrived at the Treaty of Delhi as an independent great power. It left as something more: a recognized mediating force in the international order, with the institutional credibility that only successful mediation can confer.

India holds a seat on the GPC Executive Board as part of a broader expansion of that body, joining Australia, Brazil, and South Africa as new members added to the original founding ten.

Government and Politics

During the Dominion period (1906–1963), India was governed as a federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy, with the British monarch as ceremonial head of state and the elected Sabha as the primary seat of democratic authority. Since independence in 1963, India has governed itself as a federal parliamentary republic, the institutional architecture of the Dominion period largely retained and adapted. The traditions of the Indian Reform League, the Indian National Congress, the All-India Muslim League, and the People's Party of India — the four political formations that shaped the Dominion's parliamentary life — continue to define the currents of independent Indian politics, though the alignments and coalitions have evolved considerably since 1906.
