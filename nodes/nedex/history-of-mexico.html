History of the Empire of Mexico
From the civil war of 1835 to the present

The history of the Empire of Mexico spans from its founding in 1835 through the present day. It encompasses the establishment of the imperial dynasty under Raul I in the crucible of civil war, decades of constitutional struggle between the throne and the Cortes, the civil war of 1913–1921, and the emergence of a functioning parliamentary democracy under Empress Gabriella.

Founding and the First Constitution (1835–1857)

The Empire of Mexico was forged in the Mexican Civil War of 1835, fought between the Constitutionalist government of President Manuel Ortega — which had relocated to Santa Fe under pressure — and the Imperialist faction led by General Raul Guerra. The Constitutionalists won an early victory at the Battle of Chihuahua on May 11, 1835, a moment that briefly raised hopes the Ortega government might prevail or at least secure a stable breakaway republic in the north.

The United States intervened on the Constitutionalist side, declaring war on the "Imperialist Rebels" on June 21, 1835, and ultimately drafting more than 100,000 men into the conflict across two mobilisation orders. The war proved divisive at home — New England saw repeated draft riots, with many Federalists viewing the intervention as a war of territorial expansion despite American assurances against annexing Texas. President Calhoun, an outspoken supporter of the war, was assassinated by an anti-war protester while delivering a pro-war address in Philadelphia on May 1, 1836, dying eight days later; Vice President Brandon succeeded him.

Despite sustained American backing, Ortega's Constitutionalist government was ultimately unable to prevail. General Guerra's Imperialist forces secured victory, and Guerra was proclaimed Emperor Raul I, establishing the House of Guerra as Mexico's imperial dynasty — a monarchy founded, unusually, in direct military victory over a government the United States had armed and fought alongside. The peace settlement that followed, concluded at New Orleans, left the Empire in control of central and southern Mexico while the northern territories — devastated by years of fighting — entered a period of contested and uncertain governance that would not be fully resolved for another decade.

A second, larger conflict erupted in the mid-1840s. Imperial Mexico moved to reassert control over its former northern territories beginning in late 1843, prompting Texas to declare independence under the organisation of Stephen Austin, with Alta California following in February 1844. President Yates initially lacked the congressional support to intervene directly, but the political calculus shifted dramatically when the Mexican Navy sank the American merchant vessel S.S. Bostonian off Galvez Town on August 13, 1845. Within weeks, President Wilbur Haines secured a formal declaration of war against the Empire of Mexico from Congress on September 2, 1845.

The war that followed — sometimes called the Second Mexican War — saw fighting across Texas, California, and northern Mexico through 1846 and into early 1847, including major engagements at San Antonio and Monterrey. A cadre of Imperial military officers, blaming Emperor Raul I for the war's costs and outcome, forced him to agree to a ceasefire in April 1847. The Second Treaty of New Orleans, signed on May 10, 1847, formally ended the conflict and recognised the independence of the Texas Federation and the Union of the Californias, with the United States pledging to respect both republics' sovereignty and forswearing annexation.

In the war's aftermath, the same military council that had forced the ceasefire compelled Raul I to adopt a constitution, holding the throne responsible for the catastrophe and arguing that the monarchy alone could not be trusted with safeguarding imperial interests. The Constitution of 1848 established a unicameral Cortes with property qualifications for voting. While the Cortes could initiate legislation, the Emperor retained an absolute veto, approval authority over prime ministers, and the power to dissolve the legislature. The pro-Army faction dominated the early Cortes, and the Emperor's preferred candidate, General Ramón Alvarado, was confirmed as the first Prime Minister.

The Reactionary Era and Constitutional Crisis (1857–1897)

Raul II, who succeeded his father in 1857, pursued an aggressive policy of reasserting imperial authority against the growing reform movement in the Cortes. His reign reached its most dramatic moment in the constitutional crisis of 1868–1869, when the Cortes nominated a prime ministerial candidate Raul II refused to confirm. The resulting deadlock left Mexico without a functioning government for several months before the Cortes relented and accepted the Emperor's counter-nominee, Marcos Vega. The episode became a landmark grievance for the reform movement even as it demonstrated the throne's formal supremacy.

Raul III, who came to the throne in 1879, proved a weaker figure than his predecessor. Without surviving direct heirs and facing an increasingly organised reform bloc in the Cortes, he made a series of grudging concessions over the course of his reign. His appointment of Diego Herrera as Prime Minister in 1882 — motivated largely by a personal dispute with the Partido Imperial leadership rather than any reformist conviction — proved inadvertently consequential. Herrera allowed modest reductions in property qualifications to pass without serious resistance, setting a precedent that made future moderate appointments harder to reverse.

The final years of Raul III's reign were shadowed by the succession crisis. He died without a direct heir in 1897, having been forced to pass over his designated successor following a serious personal scandal. The throne passed to his nephew, Jorge I, the younger son of Raul III's brother Juan.

The Reform Reign of Jorge I (1897–1913)

Jorge I came to the throne as an unexpected heir with no traditional grooming for power, and his reign was defined by a sustained and deliberate program of constitutional reform. His first act was the dismissal of the sitting Prime Minister, General Victoriano Cazares, who had anticipated managing the young emperor as a pliant figurehead. The swift and pointed nature of the dismissal signalled clearly the character of the reign to come.

Over the following years, Jorge I oversaw the expansion of the franchise, the enactment of early labour protections, the passage of legislation establishing a formal separation of church and state, and legal reforms expanding the rights of women. His appointment of Federico Reyes as Prime Minister in 1910 — the first member of the reformist Partido Liberal Mexicano to hold the office — represented the culmination of his reform program. Universal male suffrage was enacted in 1912.

Jorge I died unexpectedly in 1913 after a short illness, at approximately 48 years of age. He did not live to see the full consequences of the political transformation he had set in motion.

The Civil War (1913–1921)

Empress Gabriella succeeded her father at the age of 26. Within months, her younger brother declared himself Emperor Raul IV, claiming that a woman could not legitimately hold the throne and positioning himself as the standard-bearer of the conservative and clerical factions that had lost ground steadily under Jorge I. The country fractured along lines that had been building for decades.

The military, whose senior leadership Jorge I had quietly reshaped over the course of his reign, largely backed Gabriella. The Partido Imperial and elements of the church hierarchy aligned with Raul IV. The Texas Federation and the Union of the Californias declined to recognise Raul IV's claim and extended informal financial support to Gabriella's government.

Eight years of grinding civil war followed. Prime Minister Federico Reyes died of illness in 1917 or 1918, a severe blow to the stability of Gabriella's government, and was succeeded by Alejandro Vega, a younger PLM figure who managed both the final years of the war and the subsequent reconstruction. Raul IV died of wounds sustained on the battlefield in 1920. A loyalist faction briefly attempted to continue the cause in his name before collapsing in 1921.

The Constitution of 1922 and Democratic Consolidation

The new constitution adopted in 1922 fundamentally transformed the character of the Mexican state. Parliamentary supremacy was formally established, stripping the throne of meaningful veto and appointment powers. The separation of church and state was codified and strengthened. Universal suffrage — extended to women as well as men — was enshrined in law, making Mexico one of the earliest nations to adopt universal suffrage without restriction. Church leaders who had actively supported the Raulist cause faced legal consequences.

Mexico's post-civil war reconstruction was difficult and uneven. The country entered the North American Conference in 1925 from a position of economic weakness, negotiating more opt-outs and special transition provisions than any other member. The conservative establishment, embodied in the Partido Nacional Conservador, remained deeply suspicious of American intentions even as the economic benefits of North American integration became increasingly tangible.

The Global War Era (1932–1940)

Mexico did not commit military forces during the Global War but provided significant economic support and logistical access to the Allied powers throughout the conflict. The wartime government was led by Arturo Delgado of the PLM, who served two consecutive terms from 1932 to 1942 with Partido Laborista coalition support. Mexico's entry into the Pan-American Passport zone and its founding membership in the Global Peace Council in 1940 marked a new stage in its integration with the postwar international order.
