Fifteenth Amendment
Constitution of the United States (1787) · Ratified September 12, 1818

The Fifteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States was ratified on September 12, 1818, as part of the Grand Compromise of 1817. It required a two-thirds majority vote of both houses of Congress to declare war, and applied the same threshold to the admission of new states to the Union. It was the last of the three amendments produced by the Grand Compromise to be ratified, and the most contentious.

Background

The amendment represented the second of two constitutional priorities secured by Federalist members of Congress during the Crawford Conference negotiations of 1817. Federalists — particularly those from New England — had long sought higher thresholds for declarations of war, a demand rooted in deep opposition to the War of 1812, which they regarded as an unnecessary and disastrous conflict entered into without adequate deliberation. The Hartford Resolution, which had defined Federalist constitutional goals in the years following the war, had called for exactly such a reform.

The inclusion of a matching threshold for the admission of new states was a related concern, reflecting Federalist anxieties about the pace of westward expansion and the admission of new slave-holding states that could further dilute New England's influence in Congress.

Provisions

The amendment stipulated that a declaration of war required the affirmative vote of two-thirds of both the House of Representatives and the Senate. The same two-thirds majority was required for the admission of any new state to the Union.

Ratification

The amendment passed Congress by a narrow margin on October 1, 1817 — the last of the three Grand Compromise amendments to clear both chambers — and was transmitted to the states for ratification. Unlike the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments, which ratified quickly in the autumn of 1817, the Fifteenth faced a slower and more contentious path through the states, with its war powers and statehood provisions drawing opposition from Western and Southern legislators who viewed the supermajority requirement as an unreasonable check on national expansion. It was not ratified until September 12, 1818 — nearly a full year after the Fourteenth Amendment had cleared the same threshold.

Legacy

The Fifteenth Amendment remained in force for the remainder of the life of the original Constitution, shaping congressional debates over territorial expansion and military action through the middle decades of the nineteenth century. Its supermajority requirement for admitting new states meant that the steady admission of new Western and Southern states throughout the antebellum period required broader cross-sectional political coalitions than a simple majority threshold would have demanded — a structural feature that shaped the pace and sequencing of statehood debates for over forty years. Its provisions were superseded upon the adoption of the Constitution of 1861.
