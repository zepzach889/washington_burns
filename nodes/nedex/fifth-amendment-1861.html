Civil Equality Amendment
Fifth Amendment to the Constitution of 1861 · Introduced 1959 · Ratified 1964
Contents

  2. Provisions
  3. Ratification
  4. Legacy
  5. See also

The Fifth Amendment to the Constitution of 1861, universally known as the Civil Equality Amendment (CEA), guarantees the equal standing of all citizens under the law regardless of sex, race, national origin, or religion. Introduced in 1959 under President Georgina Lincoln and ratified during her term, it is the constitutional capstone of the Second Reform Era and the most recent amendment to the American constitution.

Background

Proposals for a general equality amendment were nearly as old as the century — measures floated in the Beck, Roosevelt, and Hannah eras had all stalled in Congress — and the postwar decades made the gap between the Constitution's promises and its text increasingly conspicuous. The Sanders Amendment had secured the vote; Article I's equal protection clause had felled the Harmony Laws; but no provision spoke in general terms. President Lincoln introduced the CEA in 1959 with the full backing of Washington House — and, crucially, with the Federalist Party as public co-owner of the measure, framed deliberately as an achievement of the governing bloc rather than of any single party.

Provisions

The amendment's language is universalist by design: it guarantees every citizen equal standing under the law on account of sex, race, national origin, and religion, in a single provision rather than a category-by-category enumeration — a drafting choice intended to place the principle itself, not any particular application, beyond ordinary politics.

Ratification

The CEA passed Congress in 1961. Ratification followed the era's political geography: SPA- and Federalist-controlled state legislatures moved quickly, while a bloc of Jeffersonian-led holdout states dug in, stretching the process across the expected two to three years. The amendment was fully ratified before the end of President Lincoln's term, completing the signature constitutional achievement of her presidency.

Legacy

The Civil Equality Amendment supplied the constitutional foundation for the civil-rights jurisprudence and legislation of the following decades, and its universalist framing proved farsighted: later courts applied its guarantee to circumstances its drafters never debated. Politically, its bloc-achievement framing became a model — invoked in the marriage-equality campaigns of the 1980s and cited whenever American reformers argue that constitutional change belongs to coalitions, not parties. More than sixty years on, it remains the last word added to the Constitution of 1861.
