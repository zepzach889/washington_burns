Abraham Lincoln
1809 – c. 1880s · Senator · Constitutional Convention President · Chief Justice of the United States

Abraham Lincoln (1809–c. 1880s) was an Indiana senator, jurist, and statesman best known as the president of the Constitutional Convention of 1861 — the gathering that produced the Constitution of 1861 and established the framework under which the United States has operated ever since. He subsequently served as an Associate Justice and later Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States. He is one of the most revered figures in American civic memory, and his opening address to the Constitutional Convention is among the most quoted speeches in the republic's history.

Lincoln occupies a dramatically different place in this timeline than in the history of other worlds. He never served as President of the United States — the sequence of events that in other histories produced his presidency never occurred here. Instead, he served as a senator, a wartime supporter of the Free State cause, and ultimately as the presiding officer of the convention that remade the republic after the War Between the States.

Early Life and Career

Abraham Lincoln was born in Kentucky in 1809 and grew up in modest frontier circumstances. Self-educated in the law, he was admitted to the bar and built a career as a lawyer before entering politics. He eventually settled in Indiana, where he became associated with the Democrat Party and rose to the Senate. Throughout his political career he was a consistent and vocal abolitionist — a position that put him at odds with much of his party's Southern wing but earned him deep respect among the Free State movement.

During the years leading up to the War Between the States, Lincoln was one of the most prominent voices in Congress calling for abolition and challenging the constitutional legitimacy of the Runaway Slave Act and the measures taken by the Hawthorne Administration. When the Hawthorne government began arresting congressional delegations who opposed it, Lincoln rose in condemnation on the Senate floor: "President Hawthorne, or should I say His Majesty King James the First, is not content to hold in bondage the black race which the South relies on for labor. He has now set his sights on the North, and would take away our precious First Amendment right to free speech." When the Free State Congress reconstituted itself in Philadelphia in 1858, Lincoln was among its members and a supporter of Hugo Brandt's acting government throughout the conflict.

The Constitutional Convention of 1861

When the Constitutional Convention of 1861 opened at Independence Hall in Philadelphia on March 4, 1861, Acting President Hugo Brandt and Confederal Consul Ulysses Portman presided over the election of the convention president. The leading candidates were Lincoln, the radical Pennsylvania senator Thaddeus Stevens, and former Oregon territorial governor George Abernethy. Lincoln was chosen — not because he was the most radical voice in the room, but because he was trusted by all factions. He had been a lifelong abolitionist, which satisfied the Confederal delegates and the freedmen delegations; but he was also seen as a moderate compared to Stevens, which made him acceptable to the more conservative Free State and Southern delegates.

As convention president, Lincoln's role was primarily procedural — managing debate, maintaining order, and brokering compromise when the process threatened to break down. He performed that role with considerable skill through two and a half months of contentious negotiation. His most consequential intervention came when the Confederal and radical delegates walked out after the counter-proposal to the Executive Compromise was presented, breaking the convention's quorum. Lincoln spent several days in private negotiations before the Executive Compromise was presented on April 26 — the arrangement that produced the Secretary General system and the modern structure of the executive branch. He made clear in those negotiations that he agreed with the Confederal position that more substantive change was needed, which helped bring the radical delegates back to the table.

His opening address to the convention on March 5, 1861, remains one of the most celebrated speeches in American history:

Four score and five years ago, our forebears brought forth on this continent a new nation, conceived in liberty and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal. That promise, though noble, has been marred by strife and imperfection, its realization delayed by the weight of division and the flaws of our human nature. Today, we assemble not merely to mend a fractured union, but to remake it, to set its foundation anew upon firmer ground… We must answer three great questions. First, how do we ensure that freedom, once proclaimed, is preserved for all? Second, how do we structure a government that is strong enough to protect our liberties, yet restrained enough to avoid oppressing them? And third, how do we bind together the scars of war into a Union that is not merely whole, but just and enduring?

On May 15, 1861, Lincoln called the final vote on the completed document. It passed 468 to 16. With that vote, the Constitutional Convention of 1861 dissolved, and Lincoln's most consequential public service — as presiding officer — was complete.

Later Career

Lincoln was considered as a running mate for Hugo Brandt in the 1862 presidential election but withdrew his name after two rounds of balloting, writing to a friend that the party ticket needed a Northerner and a Southerner, and Clay — the planter-turned-abolitionist — would be a powerful symbol for the country should he win. Cassius Clay of Kentucky was ultimately chosen instead.

Lincoln retired from Congress after the 1862 election. In 1863, President Brandt nominated him as one of two new Associate Justices created by the Judicial Reform Act of 1863, which expanded the Supreme Court from seven to nine members. Lincoln's appointment met almost no resistance; the Senate confirmed him unanimously on May 1, 1863.

Lincoln served as Associate Justice through the critical early years of Reconstruction, helping to establish a consistent jurisprudence around the new Article I citizenship and equal protection provisions. He is credited with anchoring the court's interpretation of the Constitution of 1861 during its most formative period — what historians would come to call the Lincoln Court era, a designation that applied first to the court on which he sat as an associate member.

In 1869, Chief Justice Samuel Nelson stepped down from the court due to poor health. President Quincy nominated Associate Justice Abraham Lincoln to fill his position as Chief Justice of the United States. He served in that capacity through the remainder of his judicial career, presiding over consequential cases including Faulkner v. The United States (1870), in which the court upheld the federal government's authority to dissolve non-compliant states, effectively forcing Georgia's belated ratification of the Constitution of 1861. He also swore in Vice President Bradley Kinder as the 16th President of the United States following President Quincy's departure from office.

Family and Legacy

Lincoln's family became one of the most prominent in Indiana's political life across subsequent generations. His great-grandson, Leonard Lincoln, served as Governor of Indiana. Georgina Lincoln — Abraham's great-great-granddaughter — became the 33rd President of the United States (1959–1965), the first woman and the first Social Progressive Alliance president to hold the office. The Lincoln family's arc from the Constitutional Convention to the first female presidency is frequently cited as one of the more remarkable threads in American political history.

Abraham Lincoln is commemorated throughout the republic. His opening address to the Constitutional Convention is taught in schools, quoted in political speeches, and inscribed on public buildings. The Brandt Memorial in Franklin — dedicated in 1910 — includes Lincoln's address in full on its interior walls. A statue of Lincoln stands in the rotunda of Congress Hall.
