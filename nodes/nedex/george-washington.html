George Washington
1st President of the United States (1789–1797)

George Washington (1732–1799) was the first President of the United States, serving two terms from 1789 to 1797. A Virginia planter and surveyor who rose to prominence as commander of the Continental Army during the American Revolutionary War, he presided over the Constitutional Convention of 1787 before being unanimously chosen as the republic's first chief executive.

Washington's presidency established many of the precedents and norms that governed the office for generations. He navigated the new republic through its first years with a deliberate focus on establishing the dignity and independence of the executive — refusing to be drawn into European conflicts, managing the competing ambitions of his cabinet, and insisting on the legitimacy of federal authority in the face of early challenges such as the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794.

He chose not to seek a third term and delivered his Farewell Address in September 1796, warning against the dangers of faction and foreign entanglement. The address remains one of the most cited documents in American political history. He died at Mount Vernon on December 14, 1799, at the age of 67, and was mourned across the republic as its indispensable founding figure.

Several of Washington's decisions and precedents took on particular significance in the Washington Burns Timeline. His two-term tradition was eventually codified in the Grand Compromise of 1817, which limited the presidency to a single six-year term. Washington House, the official residence of the American presidency, is named in his honor, and his statue stands in the forecourt of the building in President's Square, Franklin, District of Washington.
