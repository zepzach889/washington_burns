History of the Texas Federation
From independence (1844) to the present

The history of the Texas Federation spans from its founding declaration of independence on January 3rd, 1844, through its emergence as a mature federal republic and member of the North American Conference by the mid-twentieth century, and its continued role as an active North American Union member state to the present day.

Founding and the Second Mexican War, 1844–1847

The Texas Federation was born out of necessity. When the Mexican Empire declared its intention to "restore order" in the territories of the collapsing Mexican Republic in December 1843, the leaders of the Texas Free State and the Province of Texas faced a stark choice. On January 3rd, 1844, Chief Executive Stephen Austin of the Texas Free State and Provincial Governor Esteban Herrera jointly declared the independence of the Texas Federation. The two territories would govern their respective regions separately for the duration of the war, with a unified constitutional convention to follow.

The war against the Mexican Empire lasted three years. Texian forces held the Rio Grande line through the spring of 1844 before American entry into the conflict following the sinking of the S.S. Bostonian in August 1845 shifted the balance decisively. The Second Treaty of New Orleans, signed May 10th, 1847, formally recognised Texian independence and assigned the former province of Nuevo Mexico to the Federation. The Texian constitutional convention convened that summer, adopting a federal constitution on September 8th, 1847. Elections were held in March 1848, and Esteban Herrera was elected the Federation's first president.

The Herrera and Austin Years, 1848–1866

The Federation's first two presidents set the terms of Texian political life for generations. Esteban Herrera, leader of the Reform Party, governed cautiously, prioritising institutional stability and national unity over bold reform. His reluctant signing of the Runaway Slave Act of 1848 — which required Texian authorities to assist in the return of escaped slaves from Austin state — was the defining controversy of his single term.

Stephen Austin, who succeeded Herrera in 1854 as leader of the Democratic-Nationalist Party, governed with considerably more ambition across two full terms. His 1863 compromise on compensated manumission — adopted with a seven-year timeframe for the gradual manumission of slaves throughout the Federation — represented the first serious step toward resolving the Federation's deepest constitutional tension, though the institution would not be fully and finally abolished within Texian borders until the early twentieth century, with the state of Austin remaining the last holdout for decades after Austin's presidency ended. He secured French investment for the transcontinental railroad, conducted the first state visit by a Texian head of government to Franklin, District of Washington, and maintained strict neutrality during the American War Between the States (1857–1860) despite considerable pressure from both sides. That neutrality reflected genuine political necessity: slavery remained legal in Austin state even as the rest of the Federation had moved against it, and most of Texas's foreign commerce — by sea and overland alike — remained tied to the trade networks of the southern United States, leaving Texopolis with little appetite for entanglement in a conflict that risked tearing its own union apart along nearly identical lines. Austin left office in 1866 and died that September at the age of seventy-three.

The Railroad Era, 1866–1890

The decades following Austin's presidency were defined by the construction and completion of the transcontinental railroad linking Galvezport to San Francisco. Presidents Edward Marsh and James Whitmore presided over this era, with Whitmore driving the ceremonial golden spike at El Paso in 1881 — a moment widely regarded as marking Texas's arrival as a mature economic actor within the North American order. The railroad transformed the Federation's western states, opened new markets, and accelerated immigration from the United States and Europe, particularly from the German communities of Brazos state.

The benefits of this growth were unevenly distributed, however, and Reform candidate Rodrigo Vásquez won the presidency in 1884 on a platform of labour rights and economic fairness. His single term delivered meaningful if incomplete reforms before the DNP reasserted itself in 1890 under Samuel Reeves.

The Reeves Era and the Voss Affair, 1890–1908

Samuel Reeves presided over two prosperous terms as the most business-friendly president since Austin, deepening Texian commercial ties with the United States and the Caribbean. His legacy was permanently damaged by the Voss Affair. When the Matanzas War of 1905–1906 exposed the Matanzas Clique, investigations revealed that Reeves's Commerce Secretary, Harlan Voss, had maintained deep financial entanglements with the Clique's network of contracts and bribes stretching back to at least 1893. The scandal consumed the presidency of Reeves's successor, Thomas Aldrich, who had no personal connection to the affair but could not escape its shadow. Reform candidate Ernesto Salinas swept the 1908 election on a clean government platform.

The Progressive Era, 1908–1920

Ernesto Salinas is widely regarded as the most consequential Texian president of the twentieth century. His two terms expanded workers' rights, established a federal public health initiative, invested seriously in public education in the western states, and delivered the first meaningful federal protections for the formerly enslaved population of Austin state — protections that finally completed the process of abolition that Stephen Austin's 1863 compromise had only begun decades earlier. His foreign policy gave Texas a distinct voice within the North American Conference framework and established the first coherent Texian policy toward the Mexican Empire, whose chronic instability had long been a source of border anxiety.

The Interwar Period, 1920–1932

The DNP returned to power in 1920 under Arthur Donnelly, a moderate who had publicly supported elements of Salinas's clean government reforms. His presidency ended abruptly when he died of a heart attack on March 12th, 1923, at the age of sixty-one. His Vice President, George Farwell of Colorado state, assumed the presidency and won election in his own right in 1926. Farwell's nearly decade-long tenure was defined by steady economic growth, expanding North American integration, and a significant expansion of the Texian military as global instability mounted.

The Global War, 1932–1940

Marcos Delgado, a Nuevo Mexico senator and the dominant Reform figure of his generation, won the 1932 election as the first signs of the Global War became visible. His two terms were defined entirely by that conflict — deepening the Federation's commitment to the Allied framework, managing the border with an increasingly unstable Mexican Empire, and mobilising the Texian economy and military for war. He was reelected in 1938 and governed through the Treaty of Geneva in 1939 and the Asian ceasefire in 1940, leaving office as the president who had brought Texas through the defining crisis of its first century.

Postwar Era and the North American Union

The decades following Delgado's presidency saw the Texas Federation deepen its integration into the postwar North American framework, evolving alongside the United States, Canada, the Union of the Californias, and the Empire of Mexico as the North American Conference matured into the formal North American Union. Texian governments of both major parties maintained consistent support for the integration project even as domestic debate over its pace and terms continued.

The Federation's most celebrated moment of the postwar era came in 1976, when Texian astronaut Abner Lyons served as a crew member aboard Hermes 8, the joint North American Union mission that achieved humanity's first crewed lunar landing in the Mare Imbrium. Lyons drew the short straw among the four-person crew and remained in lunar orbit while American commander Curtis Waverly, Canadian astronaut Owen Stirling, and Californian astronaut Isabel Carrera — the first woman to walk on the Moon — descended to the surface. Lyons's good-humoured acceptance of his role made him a beloved figure in Texian popular culture, remembered fondly as "the man who went to the moon but didn't quite touch it." The mission, deliberately staffed with representatives of four NAU member states, was framed by American President Langston as a statement of shared continental achievement arriving alongside the American bicentennial — a moment that found even sceptics of deeper NAU integration applauding.

The Texas Federation remains an active and engaged member of the North American Union into the present day, its head of state a regular participant in joint NAU addresses and summits alongside the leaders of the United States, Canada, Mexico, the Union of the Californias, and Hawaiʻi.
