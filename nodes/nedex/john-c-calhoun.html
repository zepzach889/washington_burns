John C. Calhoun
8th President of the United States (1833-1836)

John C. Calhoun (1782–1836) was the eighth President of the United States, serving from March 1833 until his assassination on May 9, 1836. The first Republican to win the presidency since the schism of the Democratic-Republican Party in 1820, Calhoun came to office after twice being denied the presidency through the contingent election process — first in 1826, when the House chose Alexander Hamilton, Jr. despite Calhoun having received the most electoral votes, and then in 1832, when the House again decided the election in his favor after no candidate reached a majority.

Calhoun's three-year presidency was eventful and ultimately tragic. He passed the 16th Amendment to the Constitution of 1787, ending the era of contingent elections. He established the United States Railway Company — the seed of what would become the republic's most beloved institution. He oversaw the first trial of the Preston System of education. And he led the United States into the First Mexican War — a conflict that consumed the final year of his life and which he did not live to see concluded. On May 1, 1836, while giving a pro-war speech in Philadelphia, he was shot by an anti-war protester named Hiram Polk. He died eight days later.

Early life and career

Born in South Carolina in 1782, Calhoun trained as a lawyer and entered Congress as a young man, quickly establishing himself as a forceful voice for Southern interests and a champion of states' rights. Through the 1820s he became the de facto leader of the Republican Party — the Southern-oriented successor to the old Democratic-Republican Party — and its most prominent national figure. His two failed attempts to win the presidency through the contingent election process, in 1826 and 1832, only deepened his conviction that the electoral system was broken and that the republic needed reform.

Presidency

The 16th Amendment

Calhoun's first major act as president was to resolve the electoral crisis that had produced his own troubled path to office. For two consecutive elections, the selection of the chief executive had been sent to the House of Representatives. Members from all three parties agreed the system was not working. The solution they settled on was straightforward: replace the majority requirement with a simple plurality — the candidate with the most electoral votes would win regardless of whether they held a majority.

On April 18, 1833, Congress passed the 16th Amendment to the Constitution of 1787, which did exactly that. The amendment received broad bipartisan support and was ratified by the required number of states by early 1834. It would remain in effect until the Fourth Amendment to the Constitution of 1861 replaced the plurality system with the runoff mechanism in 1911.

Domestic affairs

Calhoun came to office with a promise to renegotiate the Franklin Indian Treaty — the 1830 agreement that had established two protected Native districts in the South. He convened a conference in Franklin in March 1834 between tribal leaders, administration officials including Secretary of State Phillip Colfax and Vice President Giles Brandon, and elected officials from the southern states. Calhoun's goal was to move from two districts to one — or ideally, to relocate all Native peoples east of the Mississippi to the Kanasaw Territory. The tribal delegations balked. The talks broke down quickly and the issue remained unresolved for the rest of his presidency, frustrating Calhoun considerably.

Two more constructive domestic achievements defined his term. In August 1834, Congress approved the charter of the United States Railway Company — initially funded to construct a test line between Boston and Brooklyn. Ground was broken in October 1834 and the first train ran in early 1836, proving the concept and laying the groundwork for the nationwide network that would transform American life in the coming decades. The USRC would go on to become one of the most enduring and beloved institutions in the republic's history.

That same year, Frederick Preston, a professor at the University of the United States, received approval to pilot his proposed three-tiered school system in the District of Washington. The Preston System organized schooling into primary schools for ages 5–12, secondary schools for ages 13–15, and tertiary schools for ages 16–18, feeding into regional colleges and universities. The Franklin pilot was a success, and within half a century the Preston System became the mandated legal framework for education throughout the republic.

The First Mexican War

Foreign affairs consumed the second half of Calhoun's presidency. In January 1835, Mexican conservatives launched a counter-coup against the liberal government in Mexico City and declared the Empire of Mexico. The resulting civil war between the Liberals under President Manuel Ortega and the Imperialists under General Raul Guerra drew in Texas, whose settlers were divided between a pro-independence faction led by Stephen Austin and a constitutionalist faction led by Anthony Benton. Austin and Benton agreed to a truce — the separatists would join forces with the constitutionalists to defeat the Imperialists, with Texas's long-term status to be settled afterward.

On May 11, 1835, a Constitutionalist victory at the Battle of Chihuahua gave Ortega's forces a much-needed morale boost. Calhoun's allies in Congress moved quickly. On May 30, they proposed a declaration of war against the Imperialist faction. Federalists and many Democrats initially resisted — most saw it as a war of expansion despite official assurances that the United States would not attempt to annex Texas. After sustained lobbying, including back-room negotiations on both the Native question and Texas's postwar status, enough votes were secured. On June 21, 1835, the United States declared war on the "Imperialist Rebels" and passed a draft order for 50,000 men.

American forces joined Ortega's faction and pushed southward against the Imperialists, but casualties were higher than expected. A second draft order for an additional 50,000 troops in December 1835 triggered the first significant draft riots in New England, where opposition to the war ran deep. By the spring of 1836, the country was divided — Southerners largely supportive, Northerners largely hostile — and protests were spreading to major cities including Boston, New York, Philadelphia, and even Richmond.

Assassination

It was in this atmosphere that Calhoun traveled to Philadelphia to make the case for the war effort. On May 1, 1836, while delivering a pro-war address, he was shot by Hiram Polk, an anti-war protester. The nation was shocked. Vice President Giles Brandon, who was visiting family in Georgia, was called back to the capital. Eight days later, on May 9, 1836, President Calhoun died of his wounds — the first president of the United States to be assassinated.

Brandon was sworn in as the ninth president. Congress acted swiftly in the aftermath, establishing the Presidential Guard in June 1836 — an elite Army unit dedicated to the protection of the president, their family, and senior members of the executive administration and congressional leadership.

Legacy

Calhoun's legacy is complicated by the brevity of his term and the manner of its ending. His most durable achievements — the 16th Amendment, the founding of the USRC, and the first trial of the Preston System — shaped the republic in ways that long outlasted his presidency. The USRC in particular became so central to American life that it is difficult to imagine the republic without it.

The First Mexican War he initiated was resolved under his successor — the Treaty of New Orleans (1837) established the postwar borders and recognized the Republic of Mexico and Empire of Mexico as separate states, with Texas's status preserved as semi-autonomous within the Republic. Whether Calhoun's decision to enter the war was wise remains debated; what is not debated is that he did not live to see it concluded.

His assassination, and the establishment of the Presidential Guard that followed, marked a turning point in how the republic thought about the safety of its leaders. He was the first — but not the last — president to die at an assassin's hand.
