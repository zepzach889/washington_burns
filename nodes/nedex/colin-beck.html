Colin Beck
20th President of the United States (1893-1899)

Colin Beck (1839–1921) was the twentieth President of the United States, serving a single six-year term from 1893 to 1899. The son of German immigrants who had studied civil engineering at Illinois State University — now Calumet University — Beck came to the presidency from a career in local government and Congress rather than from law or military service, making him one of the most unusually credentialed men ever to occupy Washington House. He was nicknamed the "Engineer President" by the press, and the name stuck: his six years in office were defined by a systematic approach to federal governance that produced the Second Amendment to the Constitution of 1861, a landmark reorganization of the federal cabinet, the legalization of labor unions, the establishment of the District of Dearborn, and — his most enduring achievement — the Third Amendment to the Constitution of 1861, the Sanders Amendment, which granted women the right to vote nationwide. It was ratified in 1900, the year after he left office.

Beck is widely regarded as one of the most consequential reforming presidents in the republic's history — the man who, as the Franklin Observer put it upon his departure from office, "gave form to the modern republic."

Early life and career

Colin Beck was born into a family of German immigrants and grew up in the divided industrial city of Calumet, which straddled the border between Illinois and Indiana. He studied civil engineering at Illinois State University in the 1860s and began work for the Calumet Department of Public Works in 1867. His early career was shaped by the practical challenge of governing a city split between two states — a problem he addressed by championing the Joint Council of Calumet, a cross-state body handling infrastructure, policing, public parks, and shared city services. His success with the Joint Council earned him election to the Calumet Board of Aldermen in 1876.

In 1880, fellow Federalists drafted him to run for Congress from Illinois' 15th Congressional District — the Illinois side of Calumet. He won and served in the House from 1880 to 1893, becoming a consistent champion of national infrastructure, a fierce opponent of Theodore Drake's budget cuts to the FBI and USRC, and a vocal advocate for the Federal Health Bureau in the wake of the Italian Flu epidemic, which had claimed the life of his first wife, Lucille. His 1892 campaign slogan — Vote Beck for Practical Progress: Better Roads, Safer Homes, and Cleaner Water — captured both his governing philosophy and his popular appeal.

The Election of 1892

Beck won the 1892 Presidential Election with 344 electoral votes — the most of any candidate but 22 short of a majority under the existing rules. Democrat Peter Tindle came second with 238 and Republican Brance Haley third with 148. The contingent election in the House selected Beck on the second ballot, with 21 of 41 state delegations. However the Senate — in the second instance of a cross-party result in American history — chose Democratic candidate Richard Towns of Platte as vice president over Federalist Dale Hamlin, 47 votes to 33. Beck took office with a vice president from the opposing party, and the immediate political conversation focused almost entirely on making sure it never happened again.

Presidency

The Second Amendment

Beck and Towns both supported constitutional reform of the electoral system. The Moore Proposal — named for Connecticut Senator Albert Moore, who had championed a joint congressional session to choose a full presidential ticket in contingent elections — was introduced to Congress as the proposed Second Amendment on April 28, 1893. Both houses passed it by June and sent it to the states. Some southern states resisted, fearing the changes would further disadvantage their preferred candidates, but by the late summer of 1894 the requisite 31 states and districts had ratified it. The Second Amendment stipulated that in the event no ticket receives a majority of electoral votes, Congress shall meet in joint session to choose from the top three tickets, with each senator and representative casting one individual vote rather than voting as state delegations.

Cabinet reorganization

Beck came to office believing that the republic's executive branch had grown into a tangle of overlapping responsibilities, with bureaus and offices distributed across departments based on administrative convenience rather than coherent policy. In a message to Congress in early 1894 he called for a comprehensive reorganization: "The machinery of government must evolve with the nation it serves. The departments of our fathers cannot carry the burdens of our sons."

His proposal created three new cabinet-level departments, authorized under Article III, Section 2 of the Constitution of 1861:

The Department of Health consolidated the Federal Health Bureau — established under Drake in response to the Italian Flu — with public welfare offices previously scattered under the Interior Department. The Department of Education elevated the Federal Office of Education, founded under Quincy, into its own cabinet department with authority to coordinate education policy among the states. The Department of Transportation unified oversight of the republic's railways, roads, and ports — functions previously divided between the FBI, USRC, and several smaller bureaus.

The legislation passed in stages throughout 1894 with broad bipartisan support and marked the first major expansion of the cabinet since the mid-nineteenth century. A proposed Department of Labor and Commerce was discussed but set aside as premature; the idea would return in later administrations.

Legalization of unions

Beck had long championed the rights of working people. In the spring of 1898, Congress passed legislation fully legalizing labor unions — a measure Beck had been pushing throughout his term. The Railroad Workers Union and the Postal and Telegraph Workers Alliance, which had surged in membership after Drake's cuts and the subsequent strike of 1881, were among the primary beneficiaries. The law was one of the most significant expansions of workers' rights in the republic's history.

The Sanders Amendment and women's suffrage

Beck had pledged in his inaugural address that women would have the vote by the time he left office. "If this truly is the land of the free," he told the crowds at Congress Hall, "all our people must be free to use their voice in government." He made good on that promise, if slightly later than he had hoped.

Working with a coalition of Federalist legislators, Beck supported the Sanders Amendment — formally the proposed Third Amendment to the Constitution of 1861 — introduced by Rhode Island Senator Elias Sanders. The text declared that the right to vote could not be denied or abridged on account of sex, with Congress empowered to enforce the article by legislation.

The road through Congress was long and contentious. Debate began in earnest in 1897, driven by public rallies and petitions from the national suffrage movement and sustained advocacy from veterans of the abolitionist cause. Opposition came primarily from the Republican Party, with additional resistance from conservative members of both the Democrats and the Federalists. Beck's administration shepherded the amendment through committee and onto the floor, and in the summer of 1898 Congress approved it and sent it to the states. Full ratification came in 1900 — the year after Beck left office — but the political and legislative achievement was unambiguously his.

The District of Dearborn

In 1896, Congress approved Beck's proposal to establish the District of Dearborn — the republic's third federal district, encompassing the twin cities of Calumet, Illinois and Calumet, Indiana. Beck argued that the divided jurisdiction had long impeded the city's growth and planning, and that a unified federally administered district would honor their shared heritage and serve as a model for cross-state cooperation. It was, characteristically, a practical solution to a practical problem — and a personal one, since Calumet had been his home for his entire adult life. Beck returned there after leaving office.

Race and the Harmony Laws

The closing years of Beck's presidency were shadowed by a troubling development in the Coastal South. Legislatures in Virginia, the Carolinas, Georgia, and Florida began enacting laws formalizing racial segregation in public facilities and curtailing the voting rights of Black citizens — the so-called Harmony Laws. Federalists condemned them; Democrats and Republicans in those regions defended or ignored them. Beck, officially neutral as an outgoing president, made clear in several late-term addresses that such policies were "an affront to the moral progress of the republic." The controversy set the stage for the increasingly charged racial politics of the following decade.

The Election of 1898

Beck did not seek reelection. The Federalist convention in Boston in July 1898 nominated Pennsylvania Governor Alexander Fleak — a former senator and prominent ally of Beck's reform agenda — with Alabama Senator Peter Landon, a respected orator and veteran of the Freedmen's Party, as his running mate. Landon became only the second Black American in history to be nominated for the vice presidency.

The Democrats nominated Missouri Senator Chase McIntire with Minnesota Governor Oscar Kent. The Republicans nominated Georgia firebrand Benjamin Norton with Florida's Henry Hobbs. The campaign was dominated by race — the Harmony Laws controversy gave the election an intensity not seen since the war era.

The result was among the closest in the nation's history. The Democrats and Republicans split the Midwest and interior South sufficiently to push the race to the wire, but late returns from Illinois and Iowa confirmed a narrow Federalist victory. Fleak and Landon won. Beck left office on March 4, 1899, and returned to the District of Dearborn.

Legacy

The Franklin Observers farewell summary of Beck's administration remains the most quoted assessment of his presidency: President Beck has given form to the modern republic — its departments organized, its rights extended, its cities bound together by iron and law alike. The Second Amendment, the cabinet reorganization, the legalization of unions, the Sanders Amendment, and the District of Dearborn were all products of the same systematic, engineer's approach to governance — identify the problem, design the solution, build it. He is consistently ranked among the great reforming presidents, alongside Hugo Brandt and Horace Littlefield, as one of the men who most shaped the republic's modern institutional character.
