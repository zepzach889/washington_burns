Matanzas Bay Compact
Signed 1864 · Matanzas, Spanish Cuba

The Matanzas Bay Compact was the founding instrument of the American exile enclave in Spanish Cuba, signed at Matanzas in 1864 by the heads of the planter households that had fled the United States rather than submit to abolition. A mutual-aid pact rather than a constitution, it bound its signatories to the common defence of their persons and property, established a legislature known as The Assembly, and committed the exile settlements to the preservation of the order they had lost.

It governed the communities at Matanzas, Port Hawthorne, and New Charleston for four decades, and supplied the organisational structure behind the gun-running campaign that ended in the Matanzas War.

The exile migration

Between fifteen and twenty thousand members of the defeated planter elite left the United States in the years after the Free State victory of 1860, at the close of the War Between the States — the largest single emigration in the republic's history. Most travelled through Havana; most settled within thirty miles of Matanzas Bay, where land was cheap, the sugar economy was familiar, and slavery remained lawful under the Spanish Crown.

The administration of President Hugo Brandt was aware of the migration and chose not to impede it. The Secretary of War's argument — that Cuba should be permitted to serve as an outlet for the most radical, so that they might not remain a scourge at home — carried the cabinet over the objection of the Exterior Secretary, who warned that a hostile population twenty thousand strong and ninety miles offshore would not cease to be an American problem by ceasing to be an American population. The judgement of history has been unkind to the Secretary of War.

The Compact

The document signed in 1864 was brief, and deliberately so. It bound each signatory household to the defence of every other, to the maintenance of the common settlements, and to the support of families left destitute by the flight. It made no claim to sovereignty, established no territory, and named no government — omissions that were not modesty but calculation. A compact among private persons could exist under Spanish law where a government in exile could not, and the exiles had no intention of giving the colonial authorities grounds to expel them.

What the Compact did assert, in its preamble and nowhere else, was purpose: the preservation of the social order its signatories had carried out of the American South. That sentence did the work of a constitution for forty years.

The Assembly

The Compact provided for a legislature in which each signatory household held one seat, with the three principal settlements each returning a presiding delegate. It met twice yearly at Matanzas under a Speaker elected for a term of two years. Its formal powers extended no further than the administration of the common funds and the settlement of disputes among signatories.

Its actual authority was considerably greater, and grew as the founding generation died and seats passed to heirs who had never seen the country their fathers had left. By the 1890s The Assembly functioned as the government of the enclave in everything but name, and American consular reports had begun referring to its inner leadership by the term that would follow them to the end — the Matanzas Bay Clique.

Under the Crown

The arrangement with Spain was never written down, which was its virtue for both parties. The colonial authorities treated the settlements as a private association of foreign residents, taxed them, and did not inquire closely into their affairs. The exiles paid, kept order among themselves, and made no political claims. For twenty years the accommodation held.

Spanish abolition ended the arrangement in law and left it substantially intact in practice. The emancipation measures of the 1880s applied to the exile settlements as to every other estate on the island, and the transitional apprenticeship The Assembly negotiated in mitigation expired within the decade. What replaced it was not compliance but substitution. Within a year of emancipation the settlements had established a system of labour contracts secured against advances, with debts that outlived the contract and passed to the children of the indebted, and with a register of obligations maintained by The Assembly itself. The freedmen of Matanzas Bay were at liberty to leave and almost none could afford to.

Those who saw both systems generally judged the second the worse of the two. A man who had owned another had at least an interest in keeping him alive; a man who merely held his contract had none. The arrangement was known to the colonial authorities, who continued not to inquire closely, and it was the principal reason Madrid refused every American request over the following twenty years to permit federal investigators into the settlements.

What changed after abolition was not the substance of the exiles' order but its standing. The thing they had crossed an ocean to preserve now existed nowhere in law and survived only on the forbearance of officials who could withdraw it at any time — and the generation then inheriting the Compact's seats had grown up being told that the fault lay ninety miles to the north. The Assembly's correspondence with separatist cells in the Coastal South, which federal reports had long dismissed as essentially harmless, became in the 1890s a sustained traffic in money and arms. The restoration they wanted could not be secured in Cuba. It had to be won back in the country they had left.

Dissolution

The Compact ended with the enclave. American forces took Matanzas on May 6, 1905, Port Hawthorne on May 19, and New Charleston on May 30, capturing part of The Assembly's leadership and scattering the remainder into the hills; the last holdouts were run down on June 16, 1906.

The Treaty of Copenhagen made no mention of the document, its signatories, or their descendants, who became by its operation either citizens of the United Provinces of Cuba or stateless.
