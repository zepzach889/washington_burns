Constitution of 1787
First federal constitution of the United States · In force 1789–1861

The Constitution of 1787 was the first federal constitution of the United States, framed at the Philadelphia Convention in the summer of 1787 and in force from 1789 until its supersession by the Constitution of 1861. Over its seven decades of operation it was amended sixteen times, governed the republic through its founding generation, the War of 1812, and the era of the Grand Compromise, and ultimately proved unable to contain the sectional crisis that produced the War Between the States. By the deliberate choice of the convention of 1861, its spirit and overall structure survive in the constitution that replaced it.

Framing and Ratification

The Philadelphia Convention of 1787 produced a federal charter of remarkable brevity: a republic of enumerated powers, with an elected president chosen through an electoral college, a bicameral Congress, and a national judiciary. The framers famously declined to include a specific enumeration of rights in the original document — a decision reversed almost immediately, as the first ten amendments, the Bill of Rights, were appended in 1791 as the price of ratification in several skeptical states. George Washington, who had presided over the convention, became the first president elected under the document, and his voluntary relinquishment of office after two terms established a precedent of limited tenure that the constitution itself did not yet require.

Structure

The 1787 document vested legislative power in Congress, executive power in a president originally elected to renewable four-year terms, and judicial power in the Supreme Court. Two of its features would prove especially consequential. Its Article V provided for amendment both by congressional proposal and by a convention called at the request of the states — the mechanism through which the document would eventually be replaced. And its treatment of citizenship was famously ill-defined, existing as a jurisdiction shared uncertainly between the states and the national government; the ambiguity lay dormant for decades before becoming one of the central legal battlegrounds of the slavery crisis.

Amendments

Beyond the Bill of Rights and the early amendments refining judicial immunity and the electoral ballot, the constitution's most significant revisions came in two waves. The first was the Grand Compromise of 1817, which produced three amendments in a single negotiated package: the 13th Amendment (ratified November 1817), establishing the mechanism for filling vice-presidential vacancies and clarifying presidential succession; the 14th Amendment (ratified December 1817), limiting the president to a single six-year term — a rule in continuous force ever since — and barring successive presidents from the same state; and the 15th Amendment (ratified September 1818), requiring a two-thirds vote of both houses to declare war or admit new states.

The second wave answered the dysfunctions of three-party politics. After two consecutive elections were thrown into the House of Representatives for want of an electoral-college majority, Congress passed the 16th Amendment on April 18, 1833, dropping the majority requirement and awarding the presidency to the candidate with the most electoral votes. Ratified by early 1834, the plurality rule governed every subsequent election under the document — including the razor-thin contest of 1838, decided by a single electoral vote — and its perceived failures, culminating in the elevation of presidents without popular mandates, became one of the grievances that fed the crisis of 1857.

Crisis and Supersession

The constitution's inability to resolve the slavery question destroyed it. When the War Between the States ended in 1860, the Confederation of American States made a constitutional convention — called under the 1787 document's own Article V — a condition of the peace, insisting that the war itself had been a symptom of the charter's dysfunction. The convention that met in 1861 divided immediately over whether to discard the document or merely amend it; the Jackson Compromise, proposed by Pennsylvania delegate Ronald Jackson and adopted 401–82, resolved that the new constitution would keep the spirit and overall structure of 1787 while remaining open to changes that would make parts of it feel brand new. The result — the Bill of Rights moved to the front as a new Article I, citizenship defined as a federal matter, slavery abolished, the electoral college replaced, and the office of Secretary General created — was ratified as the Constitution of 1861, and the document of 1787 passed into history.

Legacy

An original copy of the Constitution of 1787 has been displayed since 1879 in the Museum of the United States in Franklin, alongside the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution of 1861. Constitutional scholars treat the two documents as a single continuous tradition — the deliberate consequence of the Jackson Compromise — and much of the 1787 text survives verbatim in the modern constitution. Its innovations that endured, from federalism and judicial review to the single six-year presidency introduced by its 14th Amendment, remain the architecture of American government; its failures, above all on slavery and citizenship, defined by negative example what the refounding of 1861 was built to repair.
