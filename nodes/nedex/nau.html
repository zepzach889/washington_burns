The North American Union (NAU) is a supranational organisation comprising the United States, Canada, the Texas Federation, the Union of the Californias, the Empire of Mexico, the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi, the Federal Republic of Central America, Cuba, Puerto Rico, and Jamaica. It provides for coordinated economic policy, open borders and freedom of movement among member states, a common currency (the Continental, Ȼ), and coordinated military arrangements. Often described as the closest thing in world history to a continental governing union short of outright political merger, the NAU evolved over several decades from a series of diplomatic conferences into its present form.

Members retain full sovereignty and distinct governments, but share an increasing range of policy frameworks and institutions. Mexico has historically maintained the most reservations about deeper integration and retains the most opt-outs of any full member. The NAU maintains an elected Assembly — established in 1973 under President Langston — that provides democratic accountability across the member states and proposes Union-wide legislation.

History

The North American Conference (1925–1940)

The institutions that would eventually become the North American Union originated not in a single founding moment but in a series of diplomatic conferences beginning in 1925. Known collectively as the North American Conference (NAC), this framework brought together the United States, Canada, the Texas Federation, the Union of the Californias, and the Empire of Mexico to coordinate trade, mutual defence, and eventually freedom of movement across the continent.

The First North American Conference (1925–26) produced the framework agreement establishing the North American Trade Council. Mexico received the most opt-outs and special provisions of any participant — its conservative establishment deeply suspicious of American economic and political intentions, but unable to resist the pull of continental integration from a position of postwar economic weakness.

The Second North American Conference (1930) produced the North American Open Trade Agreement, establishing free trade throughout the continent with longer transition periods for Mexico. The Joint Continental Alliance (JCA) opposed it loudly; the Social Progressive Alliance supported it with reservations about labour standards.

The Third North American Conference (1932, emergency session) produced the North American Mutual Defense Pact — "attack on one is attack on all" — with deliberately vague wording designed to ensure maximum flexibility for each member's legislature. Canada was already effectively at war through its British imperial connection when the Pact was signed.

The Fourth North American Conference (San Francisco, 1940) produced the Pan-American Passport, establishing a common passport control zone and free movement of citizens across member borders.

The Global War and Wartime Integration

The Global War (1932–1939 in Europe; 1932–1940 in the Asian theater) accelerated the transformation of the Conference framework into a more permanent institutional structure. The wartime alliance demonstrated both the practical value and the political feasibility of deep continental coordination. The Continental Defense Command, formally established in spring 1942, codified military coordination arrangements that had evolved organically during the war, cementing the institutional integration of North American defence in peacetime.

Hawaiʻi — whose monarchy recognised China as an existential threat to Pacific trade — opened its ports to Allied forces in 1932, formally aligned with the NAC powers during the war, and was integrated into the Union framework in its aftermath. Cuba and Puerto Rico, independent since the Matanzas War of 1905–06, and Jamaica, which had achieved independence from Britain, joined the Union as part of the postwar continental integration process. The Federal Republic of Central America, established from the former Central American states, joined as the Union's geographic link between Mexico and the Caribbean members.

Formation of the Union (1945–present)

The Sixth North American Conference (Toronto, 1945) produced the formal proposal of the North American Union — combining all the NAC agreements into one streamlined organisation. The Union's governing structure established biannual Council meetings with appointed representatives from each member government making day-to-day decisions, and triennial executive conferences (summits for NAU leaders), with the presiding chair rotating among member states.

The postwar decades saw gradual deepening of Union integration. The NAU Assembly, established by treaty in 1973 under President Langston following the political momentum generated by the Chosŏn War, provided democratic accountability for Union decisions and became the primary vehicle for proposing Union-wide legislation. President Langston addressed its first session in late 1973 or early 1974.

The Continental (Ȼ / CTL), the Union's common currency, completed its transition during the early twenty-first century, replacing the individual member state currencies. The Continental is issued and managed by the Continental Reserve Authority and trades internationally under the ticker CTL.

Key Features

Free movement — citizens of member states may move and reside freely across member borders under the Pan-American Passport framework established in 1940.

Common currency — the Continental (Ȼ; plural: Continentals; casual: Conti; ticker: CTL) is the shared currency of all NAU member states, managed by the Continental Reserve Authority. The transition to the Continental was completed in the early twenty-first century.

Coordinated military — member state militaries operate under a coordinated framework through the Continental Defense Command (established 1942), deepened significantly during and after the Global War. The CDC is the institutional expression of the "attack on one is attack on all" Mutual Defense Pact of 1932.

Trade integration — the North American Open Trade Agreement (1930) established the foundation; subsequent agreements and the NAU Assembly have deepened economic integration across the member states.

Democratic accountability — the NAU Assembly, established by treaty in 1973, provides elected representation at the supranational level. Its creation was the defining institutional achievement of President Langston's administration and addressed long-standing concerns about the democratic accountability of Union-wide decisions.

Institutions

The NAU's principal institutions are the Council (appointed member-state representatives meeting biannually for day-to-day governance), the Executive Conference (triennial summit of member-state leaders, with a rotating presiding chair), the NAU Assembly (elected supranational legislature, established 1973), the Continental Reserve Authority (monetary policy and currency management), and the Continental Defense Command (coordinated military structure). For the Assembly's composition, procedures, and current membership, see the NAU Assembly.
