United States
United States of America · Federal presidential republic · Founded 1776

The United States of America (commonly the United States or the US) is a federal presidential republic occupying the central and eastern portion of the North American continent. Comprising 39 states and three federal districts, it is bordered by the Dominion of Canada to the north and by the Texas Federation and the Union of the Californias to the west and southwest, with coastlines on the Atlantic Ocean, the Gulf of Mexico, and the Pacific Northwest. Its capital is Franklin, District of Washington, and its largest city is New York City. The United States is a founding member of the North American Union and a founding Executive Board member of the Global Peace Council.

Founded by the Declaration of Independence of July 4, 1776, the republic was first organized under the Constitution of 1787 and comprehensively refounded by the Constitution of 1861, adopted at the close of the War Between the States, which abolished slavery and established the modern structure of American government — including the single six-year presidency and the office of Secretary General. In 2026 the nation is marking its semiquincentennial, the 250th anniversary of independence, with celebrations centered on Philadelphia and Franklin and a continental rail tour by President Sophia Nakamura, the 44th president and the first elected from the Green Party.

History

The United States declared independence from Great Britain in 1776 and secured it through the Revolutionary War, adopting its first federal constitution in 1787. The early republic was tested by the War of 1812, during which British forces burned the unfinished capital of Washington City in 1814. The Grand Compromise of 1817, negotiated by President William Crawford, resolved the postwar political crisis: it produced three constitutional amendments — including the single six-year presidential term — and relocated the national capital westward to a new federal district on the Ohio River. The city of Franklin received the government in 1827.

Decades of westward expansion deepened the sectional conflict over slavery, culminating in the War Between the States (1857–1860), in which the republic fractured into three rival governments before the victory of the Free States under Acting President Hugo Brandt. The war ended slavery and produced the Constitution of 1861, and its aftermath saw the admission of Kanasaw and Gigadohi in 1862 as the first Native-majority states in the Union. Reconstruction, the completion of the transcontinental railroad, and the organization of the western territories followed; the admission of Clark in July 1882 brought the Union to its present 39 states. The federal districts of Manhattan (1880) and Dearborn (1896) joined the District of Washington in this period, and the Third Amendment extended the vote to all American women in 1900.

The Matanzas War (1905–1906) against Spain ended Spanish rule in the Caribbean and established the independent republics of Cuba and Puerto Rico. The Fourth Amendment of 1911 replaced the old electoral mechanics with the modern runoff system, cementing the multi-party structure of American politics. The United States entered the Global War in 1933 after Spain declared the Mediterranean closed to neutral shipping, and under President Leon Palmer led the Allied coalition to victory in Europe. The Treaty of Geneva (1939) and the founding of the Global Peace Council made the United States a permanent pillar of the postwar international order, and wartime cooperation with its neighbors transformed the North American Conference of 1925 into the formal North American Union in 1945.

The postwar era brought sustained social transformation: Henry Freeman became the first elected Black president in 1940, Georgina Lincoln the first female president in 1959, and the long arc of racial and social reform reshaped national life across the century. The United States helped establish the NAU Assembly in 1973, adopted the common Continental currency, and participated in the continental space program that placed North Americans on the Moon. President Naomi Clearwater's steady handling of the Chinese Spring of 2019 and her groundwork for a formal NAU constitution defined the recent political era. The 2024 election produced the Green Party's first presidency under Sophia Nakamura, whose administration has enacted major climate legislation, opened a new chapter of NAU constitutional diplomacy, and presided over the semiquincentennial celebrations of 2026 — even as a joint NAU crew prepares for the first human voyage to Mars, scheduled to launch late this year.

Government and Politics

The United States is governed under the Constitution of 1861, which has been amended four times. The President serves as both head of state and head of government, elected to a single six-year term — a limit in continuous force since the Fourteenth Amendment of 1817. The Vice President is elected on the presidential ticket, while the Secretary General, a distinctive creation of the 1861 constitutional convention, is chosen by the House of Representatives from among its own membership and presides over the president's cabinet. Legislative power rests with the bicameral Congress — the Senate and the House of Representatives — seated at Congress Hall in Franklin, and judicial power with the Supreme Court.

Presidential elections operate under the runoff system established by the Fourth Amendment (1911): if no candidate wins an outright majority, the two leading candidates proceed to a national runoff. Five parties currently contest national elections — from left to right, the Social Progressive Alliance, the Federalist Party, the Green Party, the Democratic Party, and the Jeffersonian Constitutional Alliance. The Greens' 2024 victory under Sophia Nakamura broke a century-long pattern of four-party competition. The incumbent Secretary General is Diane Moss, and the Vice President is Miriam Katz.

Geography and Subdivisions

The United States spans from the Atlantic seaboard across the Mississippi basin and the northern plains to the Pacific coast of the Oregon country, sharing its longest border with the Dominion of Canada. Its western and southwestern frontiers meet the Texas Federation and the Union of the Californias, both independent since the 1840s. The republic comprises 39 states and three federal districts — Washington (home to Franklin), Manhattan, and Dearborn (the unified city of Calumet). Three states — Kanasaw, Gigadohi, and Lakota — operate under Native-majority constitutions featuring a Council of Nations upper chamber and communal land trusts, a constitutional innovation dating to 1862.

Foreign Relations

The United States is the largest economy of the North American Union, whose common institutions — the Continental currency, the free-movement zone descended from the Pan-American Passport, the NAU Assembly, and the Continental Defense Command — anchor American foreign policy. Relations with the United Kingdom, adversarial through the War Between the States era, were reconciled over the late nineteenth century and are today among the closest of any two powers. The United States holds a founding Executive Board seat on the Global Peace Council and maintains an increasingly warm relationship with the Empire of China following the Chinese Spring of 2019, symbolized by the Kāiming Emperor's state visit to Franklin in 2025. In late 2026, delegates from every NAU member state are expected to convene to draft a formal NAU constitution, the culmination of a diplomatic project spanning the Clearwater and Nakamura administrations.
