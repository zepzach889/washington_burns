The Union of German States (Union der Deutschen Staaten) was a federal political union of northern and central German states that existed from 1857 until its dissolution in 1938. Established by the Treaty of Potsdam as a successor to the dissolved German Confederation, the Union brought together the member states of the Zollverein customs union into a shared political structure with common currency, military, and civil service. Its formal headquarters were located at Potsdam, though effective political authority resided in Berlin, the capital of the dominant member state, the Kingdom of Prussia.

The Union comprised, at various points in its history, between sixteen and eighteen member states, ranging from the great Kingdom of Prussia to the small city-republics of Hamburg, Bremen, Frankfurt, and Lübeck. Its constitutional structure provided for an All-Union Assembly, in which seats were apportioned by population, and a Council of States, in which each member held equal standing. In theory this balance was intended to prevent any single state from dominating Union policy. In practice, Prussia's size ensured it commanded the Assembly, and the economic dependencies generated by Prussian infrastructure investment progressively eroded the Council's independence through the latter half of the nineteenth century.

The Union's history divides naturally into three periods: a genuine if Prussia-leaning federalism in its early decades (1857–1899); an accelerated centralisation driven by the emergency conditions of the Great European War and its aftermath (1899–1929); and the Nationalist capture of Union institutions under the government of Karl-Albrecht II that culminated in the Global War and the Union's dissolution (1929–1938). The Union was replaced by the United German Republic following the democratic revolution of 1937–1938 and the terms of the Treaty of Geneva.

Origins

The Dissolution of the German Confederation

The German Confederation, established at the Congress of Vienna in 1815 as a loose framework of thirty-nine German-speaking states under Austrian presidency, was never a functioning political union. Its sole organ, the Confederate Diet, required unanimous consent for any decision, ensuring that Austrian dominance could be maintained through obstruction as much as through positive action. The Revolts of 1847 and the subsequent chaos within the Austrian Empire stripped Austria of the political capacity to hold the Confederation together. Prussia and Bavaria jointly proposed formal dissolution in 1849, and the process was finalised in 1850.

The dissolution left a political vacuum in northern and central Germany that the Zollverein partially filled on economic grounds. The customs union, by the late 1840s encompassing most of the German states outside Austria and the South German Customs Union, had produced a degree of economic integration that created practical pressure for political coordination. The question was not whether some new political framework would emerge, but what form it would take and who would lead it.

The Potsdam Negotiations

In September 1853, Frederick William V of Prussia announced that he would invite the heads of all Zollverein member states to Potsdam to discuss converting the customs union into a political successor to the former Confederation. The negotiations that followed lasted nearly four years. The central tensions were predictable: smaller states feared Prussian dominance; Prussia was unwilling to accept institutional structures that genuinely constrained its freedom of action; and states like Hanover and Saxony, with their own distinct political traditions, sought guarantees of meaningful autonomy. The Grand Duchy of Thuringia, newly created during the negotiations themselves from the consolidation of several smaller central German principalities, was in the unusual position of being constituted as part of the Union's founding process rather than joining as a pre-existing entity.

The Treaty of Potsdam, signed in 1857 and brought into force in 1858, established the Union's fundamental structure. A common currency — the Union Thaler — replaced the various state currencies. A common military command structure was established, with the Prussian crown holding the position of Supreme Commander of the Union's armed forces — a provision that would prove the most consequential feature of the entire settlement. Member states retained their existing internal forms of government, their own legal systems for purely domestic matters, and their own civil services below the Union level.

The Kingdom of Bavaria, Baden, and Württemberg declined to join the Union, instead formalising their existing South German Customs Union relationship into the South German Confederation in 1859. Austria was not invited and did not seek inclusion.

Member States

At its founding, the Union of German States comprised the following members:

  • Kingdom of Prussia
  • Kingdom of Hanover
  • Kingdom of Saxony
  • Grand Duchy of Thuringia
  • Grand Duchy of Mecklenburg-Schwerin
  • Grand Duchy of Oldenburg
  • Duchy of Holstein
  • Electorate of Hesse (Hesse-Kassel)
  • Grand Duchy of Hesse (Hesse-Darmstadt)
  • Duchy of Nassau
  • City-Republic of Hamburg
  • City-Republic of Bremen
  • City-Republic of Frankfurt
  • City-Republic of Lübeck

Several smaller principalities were incorporated into the Grand Duchy of Thuringia during the Potsdam negotiations and thus joined the Union as part of that entity rather than as independent members.

Constitutional Structure

The All-Union Assembly

The All-Union Assembly was the primary legislative body of the Union, with seats apportioned among member states according to population. Prussia, as the largest and most populous state, held the largest bloc of seats — sufficient, in combination with states economically or politically aligned with Berlin, to pass most legislation without requiring the support of the medium and smaller members. The Assembly met in Potsdam, at the Union Hall, a purpose-built legislative chamber constructed between 1858 and 1862.

The Assembly's political composition reflected the range of currents across the Union's member states: conservative agrarian interests, liberal commercial and professional classes, Catholic Centre tendencies concentrated in the Rhineland and western Prussia, social democratic currents that grew steadily from the 1870s onward, and smaller nationalist and particularist factions in various states.

The Council of States

The Council of States provided the counterbalancing mechanism intended to protect smaller member states from simple majoritarian override by Prussia. Each member state, regardless of size, held one seat in the Council, and the Council's approval was required for all Assembly legislation to take effect. In theory this gave even the smallest city-republic a veto over Union-wide law.

In practice, the Council's independence eroded steadily through the second half of the nineteenth century. Economic dependency on Prussian infrastructure investment, the dominance of Prussian-trained civil servants in Union administrative positions, and the gradual social and professional integration of member-state elites into Berlin-centred networks all reduced the political will and capacity of smaller states to use their formal Council powers to resist Prussian preferences. By the 1890s, Council votes against Prussian-backed Assembly legislation had become rare. By the 1910s, they had effectively ceased.

Military Command

The Treaty of Potsdam vested supreme command of the Union's common military forces in the Prussian crown. In practice this meant that the Prussian general staff directed Union military planning, Union forces were trained and equipped to Prussian standards, and the officer corps of the smaller member states was progressively integrated — culturally and professionally — into a Prussian-dominated military hierarchy. This arrangement was the single most consequential structural feature of the Union for its long-term political evolution, as it gave Prussia direct coercive capacity that did not require Assembly or Council approval to exercise.

The Federalist Era (1858–1899)

The Union's first four decades were characterised by genuine, if Prussia-leaning, political diversity. The All-Union Assembly's early sessions reflected real disagreement among member state delegations. The Council of States saw substantive debate, with Hanover, Saxony, and Thuringia in particular maintaining active political traditions. Hanover, whose King Eduard I had made that state the leading reformist voice in German politics before the Union's establishment, regularly combined with Saxony to modify or delay Prussian initiatives.

The economic growth of the 1860s and 1870s, driven by the Zollverein's trade framework and rapid industrialisation, produced prosperity that broadly legitimised the Union's institutional structure. The social democratic movement grew steadily through the 1870s and 1880s, producing formal political organisations that won seats in the All-Union Assembly and member-state parliaments — and producing in Prussia a series of legislative attempts to restrict socialist organising that had mixed success and generated significant political conflict.

The gradual erosion of smaller states' practical autonomy during this period was largely invisible to contemporaries because it occurred through economic and social processes rather than formal constitutional change. The Potsdam treaty remained unamended; the Council of States continued to meet; member-state governments continued to exist. What changed was the informal calculus of political possibility.

The Great European War and Its Aftermath (1899–1907)

The Great European War transformed the Union's internal politics in ways that outlasted the conflict itself. When war broke out in 1899 — triggered by the assassination of Bavaria's young King Louis-Charles and the explosion of Franco-Prussian rivalry — the Union's common military command was placed under unified emergency direction from Berlin, and the All-Union Assembly voted through a series of emergency measures concentrating economic and administrative authority at the Union level.

The war lasted five years and cost the Union's member states, particularly Prussia, an enormous toll in lives and material. The conflict's outcome was deeply ambiguous: the Union's forces were not defeated in the field, but the communalist revolutions of 1904 that ended the fighting produced the Union of European Republics on the Union's western and southern borders — a development that many in Prussia regarded as a strategic catastrophe more damaging than a conventional military defeat would have been.

In the war's immediate aftermath, Berlin made no serious effort to restore the emergency powers to their pre-war footing. The argument was straightforward: the Union faced an unprecedented ideological and strategic threat from the UER, and the unity and resolve that had sustained the war effort must be maintained. The All-Union Assembly offered little resistance to this framing; the Council of States was similarly acquiescent. The founding of the International Peace Council in London in 1907 — attended by Prussian and Union representatives alongside delegates from more than a dozen other nations — represented the Union's formal entry into a broader framework of great-power diplomacy, though Prussian participation was always somewhat ambivalent.

The Rubber-Stamp Era (1907–1929)

By the time Karl-Albrecht I succeeded Frederick William V in 1907, the Union of German States had become, for practical purposes, an extension of the Prussian government. The All-Union Assembly met on schedule, debated legislation in form, and voted — almost invariably in accordance with the wishes of the Prussian Minister-President's government. The Council of States retained its formal existence and convened regularly, but meaningful dissent had become vanishingly rare. Senior Union civil servants were overwhelmingly Prussian by background and training. The common military's officer corps was indistinguishable, in culture and loyalty, from the Prussian army.

The smaller member states had not been annexed or formally subordinated — the Potsdam treaty remained the Union's constitutional foundation, and member-state governments continued to exercise their domestic authorities. What had been lost was political will and organisational capacity for independent action. A generation of member-state politicians had grown up in a system where serious resistance to Berlin was understood to be futile. Hanover and Thuringia retained somewhat more institutional memory of genuine federalism than most — their political cultures preserved traces of the reform tradition that had characterised the Union's earlier decades — but even they operated within the limits Berlin set.

The rise of the Revanchist Nationalist Party through the 1920s introduced a new and more volatile element into this stable pattern of subordination. The Nationalists were ideologically committed to a program of territorial revision and confrontation with the UER that went well beyond what Karl-Albrecht I's government was willing to contemplate. Their electoral growth in the All-Union Assembly — where, as a Prussian-origin party, they benefited from Prussia's dominant bloc of seats — gave them a platform from which to apply pressure on the Prussian government and, indirectly, on Union institutions. Karl-Albrecht I's death in 1929 removed the principal institutional brake on the Nationalist movement at the moment of maximum opportunity.

The Nationalist Era and the Global War (1929–1938)

Karl-Albrecht II's accession in 1929 marked the effective end of whatever independence the Union's institutions had retained. When the Revanchist Nationalist Party won parliamentary elections in the early 1930s and formed a governing coalition in Prussia — and, through Prussia's dominance of the All-Union Assembly, at the Union level — Karl-Albrecht II welcomed the development as the proper expression of German political will.

The Union's formal structures were not dissolved or suspended; they continued to operate. But their function had inverted entirely. Rather than providing even a nominal check on Prussian executive power, Union institutions now served as an amplifier and legitimising vehicle for Nationalist policy. Assembly votes approving Nationalist-backed measures were unanimous or near-unanimous. The Council of States had become a ceremonial body.

The Union's formal declaration of war against the Union of European Republics in 1932 was passed by the All-Union Assembly in a session lasting less than a day. Several member-state delegations — most notably from Hanover and Thuringia — recorded abstentions or procedural objections that were noted in the Assembly minutes and promptly ignored.

The Internal Revolt and Dissolution

By late 1937, the Global War's costs had produced the conditions for internal revolt. Mass protests spread across the Union's cities, and the organisational capacity for resistance — suppressed but not destroyed in the smaller member states — began to reassert itself. Hanover and Thuringia, whose political cultures had preserved more of the Union's original federalist tradition than most, provided crucial early leadership for the democratic coalition that emerged in opposition to the Nationalist government.

The collapse of the Nationalist parliamentary coalition and the abdication of Karl-Albrecht II in early 1938 opened the question of what political structure would replace the Union. The democratic coalition that had helped topple the Hohenzollerns — composed of representatives from across the Union's non-Prussian member states — proclaimed the United German Republic and entered armistice negotiations with the Allied powers. The act of proclamation was a conscious statement: Germany's democratic future would not be a Prussian successor state but a genuinely federal republic in which no member could replicate what Prussia had done.

The Union of German States was formally dissolved under the Treaty of Geneva (1939). Its common institutions were wound up, the Union Thaler was replaced by the new German Republic Mark, and the member states reconstituted themselves — or, in Prussia's case, were reconstituted by external settlement — within the framework of the United German Republic. Prussia itself survived as a separate isolated state until reunification with the German Republic in 1996.

Legacy

The Union of German States left an ambiguous legacy in German political memory. Its achievements were real: eighty years of relative peace among the northern German states, substantial economic development, the construction of a common infrastructure that outlasted the political structure built upon it, and the preservation of constitutional government in Prussia and most member states through several decades of European turbulence. Its failure was equally real: the gradual capture of its institutions by Prussian dominance, and their ultimate weaponisation by the Nationalist movement, made the Union not merely a passive victim of the Global War but an active instrument of it.

The Constitution of the United German Republic was drafted with explicit attention to the Union's structural failures. The distribution of power among successor states, the supermajority requirements for constitutional amendment, and the deliberate choice of Hannover — capital of one of the Union's most suppressed member states — as the seat of the new republic all reflected a determination that the Union's history not repeat itself.

Historians of the German republic in subsequent decades debated whether the Union's trajectory was inevitable from its founding — whether the population-weighted Assembly and the military command provision of the Potsdam treaty made Prussian dominance structurally predetermined — or whether different political choices at key moments might have produced a more genuinely federal outcome. This debate remained unresolved, as such debates tend to.
