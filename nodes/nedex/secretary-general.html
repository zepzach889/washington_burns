Secretary General of the United States
Presiding officer of the President's Cabinet and liaison to the House of Representatives
Contents

  2. Powers and role
  3. Appointment and tenure
  4. List of Secretaries General
  5. See also

The Secretary General of the United States is a senior officer of the federal government established by the Constitution of 1861. Chosen by the House of Representatives from among its own membership, the Secretary General serves as the presiding officer of the President's Cabinet and acts as the principal liaison between the executive branch and Congress.

The office is unique in that its holder simultaneously serves as a sitting member of the House of Representatives and as the head of the Cabinet — making the Secretary General the only federal officer with a formal role in both the legislative and executive branches. The position was created as part of the broader restructuring of the executive branch at the Second Constitutional Convention, and reflects the convention delegates' intent to establish a stronger, more collaborative relationship between the presidency and Congress.

Constitutional basis

The Secretary General is established in Article I of the Constitution of 1861, which states that "The House of Representatives shall choose their Speaker and other Officers; and shall place one of its Members as the Secretary General, who shall serve as the Head of the President's Cabinet." Article II further defines the Secretary General's role within the Cabinet, stipulating that the Cabinet shall preside over and coordinate the executive departments, and that the Secretary General shall chair its proceedings.

The House retains the power to remove the Secretary General — along with any Cabinet member — by a simple majority vote withdrawing its consent. This mechanism, sometimes called a "legislative removal" or informally a "withdrawal," has been invoked on a small number of occasions in the office's history and remains the most direct form of congressional check on executive branch composition.

The Secretary General is also fourth in the presidential line of succession, following the Vice President, the Speaker of the House, and the President pro tempore of the Senate.

Powers and role

In practice, the Secretary General's authority falls somewhere between a senior legislative leader and a cabinet coordinator. The office carries no independent executive power — the Secretary General cannot issue orders or direct policy without the support of the broader Cabinet — but the position's dual legislative and executive character gives its holder significant influence over the flow of legislation and the management of presidential priorities.

The Secretary General typically works closely with the Speaker of the House to manage the congressional calendar, negotiate the passage of administration-backed legislation, and communicate the Cabinet's priorities to the House membership. When relations between the presidency and Congress are strained — particularly during periods of divided government — the Secretary General often serves as the primary channel for negotiation between the two branches.

The Constitution explicitly forbids the Speaker of the House from simultaneously serving as Secretary General. This separation, not specified in the original text of the Constitution but established by convention practice in 1863 and later codified in law in the early twentieth century, arose from the early controversy surrounding Alexander Trent's retention of the Secretary General post when the 38th Congress convened. Though Trent sought to hold both positions, the House ultimately declined to grant him the Speakership, establishing a precedent that the two offices require separate persons.

Appointment and tenure

The Secretary General is chosen by a majority vote of the House of Representatives at the opening of each new Congress. Because the position is tied to House leadership, it generally turns over when party control of the House shifts — most Secretaries General serve for the duration of a single Congress or a period of stable majority control, and are replaced when an opposing party or coalition takes the majority. There is no constitutional limit on how many times the same individual may serve as Secretary General, and several holders of the office have been reappointed across multiple Congresses.

The Secretary General does not resign from their House seat upon appointment, and continues to represent their district throughout their tenure.
