Giles Brandon
9th President of the United States (1836-1839)

Giles Brandon ((1779–1849) was the ninth President of the United States, serving from May 9, 1836 to March 1839. A Georgia lawyer and politician who had served as governor, senator, and vice president under John C. Calhoun, Brandon became the first — but tragically not the last — vice president to ascend to the presidency following an assassination. He inherited a country at war, a nation in shock, and a constitutional question about his right to seek a full term that would occupy the courts and the country for much of his time in office.

Brandon's presidency was shaped almost entirely by circumstances not of his making. He concluded the First Mexican War, negotiated the Second Franklin Indian Treaty, oversaw the crucial expansion of the United States Railway Company, and fought — and ultimately lost — the political battle to win a full term in his own right. He left office in March 1839 having steadied the ship through an extraordinarily turbulent period, and returned to Georgia, where he died a decade later.

Early life and career

Giles Brandon was born in Georgia on April 22, 1779, and trained as a lawyer before entering politics. He attended Harvard and built a career as one of Georgia's most prominent Republican figures. He served as Governor of Georgia from 1821 to 1825, then as U.S. Senator from 1825 to 1833, during which time he became closely associated with John C. Calhoun — most notably as one of the key negotiators who helped broker the Franklin Indian Treaty of 1830. Brandon's persuasion of his fellow Southern leaders to accept Hamilton's compromise proposal at that conference was credited with averting a wider conflict.

When Calhoun secured the presidency in the 1832 Presidential Election, Brandon was his natural choice for vice president. He was confirmed and served in that role until Calhoun's assassination in May 1836.

Assumption of the presidency

Brandon was visiting family in Georgia when Calhoun was shot in Philadelphia on May 1, 1836. He was summoned back to the capital immediately and arrived to find the president's condition grave. On May 9, 1836, Calhoun died. Brandon was sworn in as the ninth president — the first to assume the office following an assassination.

The nation rallied behind its new leader. In New England, Federalists went out of their way to disavow the violence. The anti-war movement that had been growing through the spring essentially collapsed in the wake of the assassination, replaced by a surge of patriotic sentiment. Brandon addressed Congress on May 31, invoking Calhoun's memory and calling for national unity in what he called "a just cause to further the light of liberty." Congress responded by increasing funding for the war effort and issuing another draft order.

Also in June 1836, Congress established the Presidential Guard — an elite Army unit dedicated to protecting the president, their family, and senior members of the executive administration and congressional leadership. It was a direct response to Calhoun's murder and has remained one of the republic's key security institutions ever since.

Presidency

Ending the First Mexican War

Despite the initial surge in support, war fatigue had returned by the end of 1836. Casualty numbers remained high and the conflict felt increasingly inconclusive. The Empire of Mexico and the Republic of Mexico were similarly exhausted. When Ortega and Imperial General Raul Guerra agreed to a ceasefire in February 1837, the relief on all sides was palpable.

The following month, delegates from the United States, the Republic of Mexico, the Empire of Mexico, the Province of Texas, and the Texas Free State met in New Orleans to negotiate a final settlement. The two Mexican factions agreed to recognize each other's territorial holdings as permanent borders, dropping all formal claims on each other's territory. The Texas Free State agreed to remain within the Mexican Republic under a semi-autonomous arrangement. The Empire of Mexico accepted these terms on the condition that the United States pledge not to annex the Free State or any other Mexican territory — a pledge the Brandon Administration readily gave.

The Treaty of New Orleans was signed on March 3, 1837, and swiftly approved by the Senate. Jefferson had achieved statehood in the fall of 1836, giving the republic's southwestern border its new shape. The First Mexican War was over.

The Second Franklin Indian Treaty

With the war concluded, Brandon turned to the Native question — the unfinished business from Calhoun's term. The Second Franklin Indian Conference opened at Washington House on May 10, 1837, with Brandon presiding. The administration had already made a key concession to Federalist and Democratic opponents of renegotiation: one of the two Native districts would be preserved rather than forcing all Native peoples east of the Mississippi to relocate to Kanasaw Territory.

The question was which district to keep. The Cherokee initially demanded the preservation of their own district, but the argument ultimately prevailed to preserve the Choctaw-Chickasaw District — renamed the Native Nations District — on the grounds that it offered river access to the Mississippi and proximity to Kanasaw, encouraging voluntary westward relocation over time. Brandon made clear, in terms the tribal leaders understood as a threat, that if they refused the new arrangement, the still-mobilized postwar army could be used to enforce his will. The Cherokee and Creek nations accepted.

The Second Franklin Indian Treaty was signed on July 10, 1837. Cherokee and Creek relocation into the new Native Nations District was to begin within two years, with financial incentives offered to those who relocated before 1839. Neither tribe agreed to cross the Mississippi into Kanasaw, and the relocation process was not without violence — but the framework held.

The USRC Expansion Act

In October 1837, the Federal Bureau of Improvements and the United States Railway Company delivered a joint report to Congress confirming that the Boston-to-Brooklyn test line had been a success. They recommended Congress fund the creation of a national railway network over the next one to two decades, following existing and planned National Road routes.

After congressional deliberation — with members seeking adjustments to include their home districts — Congress passed the USRC Expansion Act in February 1838. It was one of the most consequential pieces of legislation of the era, laying the formal foundation for the national rail network that would transform the republic.

The reelection controversy

In the same month the USRC Expansion Act passed, Brandon shocked the nation by announcing he intended to seek a full presidential term in the upcoming 1838 election. Critics across all three parties immediately challenged whether this was constitutional — the Grand Compromise of 1817's 14th Amendment limited presidents to a single six-year term, and the question was whether Brandon's partial term counted against that limit.

A multi-partisan group filed suit. The case moved quickly through the courts and reached the Supreme Court, which ruled in July 1838 that Brandon could run — he had served less than half of a full term and was therefore eligible. Brandon won the Republican nomination in late August, though not without a significant internal party challenge.

The general election was another matter. The Democrats nominated the popular Pennsylvania governor Victor Yates with Kentucky Senator Wilbur Haines as his running mate. The Federalists nominated Massachusetts Governor Peter Gallup with Maine Senator Joshua Flint. Most historians believe that lingering resentment over the First Mexican War — and the sentiment that Brandon should not have run regardless of his legal right to do so — cost him the election. Yates won with a plurality, Brandon came second, and the Federalists came a distant third — losing New Jersey and Delaware for the first time since 1820 in what some in the party described as a near-existential moment.

Brandon left office in March 1839 and returned to Georgia.

Legacy

Brandon is not remembered as a great president, but he is remembered as a capable one in difficult circumstances. He ended a war he had not started, resolved — imperfectly but durably — the Native question that had consumed two previous administrations, and oversaw the expansion of the USRC into a national institution. His decision to seek reelection, legally justified but politically damaging, is the most controversial act of his presidency — a choice that overshadowed his genuine achievements and defined how the public remembered him.

He died in Georgia on November 10, 1849, at the age of 70.
