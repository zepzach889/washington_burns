The Empire of Russia, commonly known as Russia, is a constitutional monarchy occupying the eastern half of Europe and the northern expanse of Asia. One of the largest states in the world by territory, Russia is governed under a constitution first promulgated in 1854 by Tsar Michael II, which established the Imperial Duma as the empire's legislature and placed limits on the absolute authority of the throne. The constitution has been amended and significantly reformed over the subsequent century, most substantially through the settlement of 1908, which expanded the franchise, curtailed the tsar's veto powers, and removed the requirement that the Prime Minister be drawn from the hereditary nobility.

Russia's capital and largest city is Saint Petersburg, situated on the Gulf of Finland in the empire's northwest. The country encompasses a vast diversity of peoples, languages, and faiths, with the Russian Orthodox Church recognised as the empire's official religion while the constitution formally protects the right of individuals to practice other faiths. Russia has maintained a consistent policy of neutrality in the great continental conflicts of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries — sitting out both the Great European War and the Global War — a posture that has shaped its distinctive position among the world's major powers.

History

Imperial Reform and Emancipation (1814–1866)

At the conclusion of the Napoleonic Wars, Russia emerged as one of the dominant powers of continental Europe under Tsar Alexander I, whose reign was marked by a gradual and contested drift toward moderate reform. Alexander broke decisively with the conservative European order championed by Prince Metternich of Austria over the question of Greek independence in the 1820s, and in his final years turned his attention to the question of serfdom. Beginning in 1830, a series of measures allowed serfs on state and imperial lands to purchase their freedom, with the policy later extended to all serfs in the empire. Alexander died in November 1835 without completing the work he had begun.

His successor and younger brother, Tsar Michael II, continued and expanded the emancipation program with greater urgency. In 1837, serfs were declared no longer in personal bondage to their noble masters, and new restrictions on their treatment were codified with meaningful penalties for violations. The war of 1849–1851 against the Ottoman Empire and its British and French allies interrupted further reform but ultimately produced a modest Russian victory, codified in the Treaty of Berlin, which enhanced the tsar's domestic prestige and created the political conditions for further change. In 1852, it was announced that children of serfs born on or after January 1, 1853, would be born free. The following year, Michael convened a constitutional assembly in Saint Petersburg, which produced Russia's first formal constitution on September 1, 1854. On August 13, 1861, the tsar signed legislation mandating that all remaining serfs in the empire would be freed no later than December 31, 1866. Emancipation was completed on schedule, one of the defining achievements of Michael II's reign.

The Reign of Michael III (1864–1893)

Tsar Michael III, the elder son of Michael II, acceded to the throne in 1864 at the age of thirty-seven. He inherited a country mid-emancipation and a constitution barely a decade old, and his task was consolidation rather than further reform. Under his steady if unspectacular stewardship, the Imperial Duma was normalised as a functioning institution, the emancipation of the remaining serfs was completed in 1866, and substantial investment was directed toward railroad construction and industrial development. The first major rail connections between Russia's principal cities were completed during his reign, beginning the slow process of binding the empire's vast territory into a more unified economic whole.

The most dramatic event of Michael III's reign was Russia's entry into the War of Balkan Liberation in 1881. A major uprising among Slavic Christian peoples in the Ottoman Balkans — centred initially in Bulgaria and supported by widespread Pan-Slavic sentiment within Russia — drew the empire into open conflict with the Ottoman Empire for the second time in thirty years. The war was short and, for Russia, successful. The Treaty of Adrianople recognised the independence or autonomy of Bulgaria, Serbia, Montenegro, and Romania, granted Greece the region of Epirus and several Aegean islands, and extracted from the Ottoman government a formal diplomatic acknowledgment of Russia's right to raise the condition of Orthodox Christians within Ottoman territories. The war significantly enhanced Russia's prestige as the patron of Slavic nationalism and Orthodox Christianity in southeastern Europe, though it left the status of Macedonia deliberately unresolved and planted the seeds of future friction with Austria-Hungary.

Michael III died in 1893 at the age of sixty-six, having governed for nearly three decades. He left behind a more industrialised, more constitutionally settled country than he had inherited, though with nationalist pressures in Poland and Finland that were becoming increasingly organised and difficult to ignore.

The Reign of Alexander III and the Constitutional Crisis (1893–1928)

Tsar Alexander III acceded in 1893 at the age of thirty-five and would reign for thirty-five years, making him the longest-serving monarch since the constitutional era began. His reign was defined by the tension between a rapidly changing society and a political order he was temperamentally disinclined to reform but pragmatic enough to renegotiate when the alternative was worse.

The early decades of Alexander III's reign saw sustained industrial growth, the expansion of the urban working class, and the gradual maturation of Russia's political parties. The question of minority nationalism grew more acute, particularly in Poland and Finland, where organised movements advocating independence gained significant popular support. Russia's non-entry into the Great European War of 1899–1904 was among the most consequential decisions of Alexander III's reign and among the most contested domestically; Pan-Slavic nationalists argued strenuously for intervention on behalf of Russia's Slavic neighbours, while the tsar and his ministers concluded that the risks outweighed any conceivable gain. Russia stood aside as the great powers of western and central Europe tore themselves apart, emerging from the conflict intact and relatively strengthened.

The Russian Constitutional Crisis of 1907 represented the most dangerous moment of Alexander III's reign. Industrial unrest building through the early 1900s culminated in a wave of strikes in 1906, beginning among factory workers in Saint Petersburg and textile workers in Ivanovo before spreading through the Donbas mining region and to railroad workers across the empire. The Labor and Socialist Party, led by Grigori Antonov, coordinated and amplified the movement, while the Party of the Russian People moved to channel the unrest into constitutional demands it could manage. The tsar's Prime Minister, Count Pyotr Valuev-Stroganov, advised the dissolution of the Duma in early 1907 as a show of firmness. The decision backfired catastrophically, accelerating protests, producing two assassination attempts on senior officials, and drawing international attention to the empire's instability. Within months, Alexander III recognised the dissolution as an error. Valuev-Stroganov was dismissed, and negotiations opened with Alexei Lvov of the Party of the Russian People as the principal reform interlocutor. The resulting settlement, finalised in early 1908, significantly altered the constitutional order: the tsar's absolute veto was replaced with a suspensive veto overridable by Duma supermajority; the tsar's power to freely appoint the Prime Minister was curtailed; the franchise was extended to universal male suffrage; and the requirement that the Prime Minister be drawn from the Chamber of Peers was abolished. Lvov became Prime Minister and spent the following eight years embedding the new settlement into the normal functioning of government.

The latter portion of Alexander III's reign was marked by relative stability and by the gradual resolution of Russia's most pressing minority questions. In the 1920s, under Prime Minister Prince Yakov Trubetskoy, negotiated agreements established Poland and Finland in personal union with the Russian crown — retaining the tsar as head of state while granting both nations substantial domestic autonomy. The arrangements were imperfect and generated opposition from Russian nationalists and from independence advocates who considered personal union an inadequate substitute for full sovereignty, but they represented a workable equilibrium for the remainder of the imperial period. Alexander III died in 1928 at the age of seventy.

The Reign of Alexei II (1928–present)

Tsar Alexei II acceded in 1928 at the age of thirty-six, the first monarch to come of age entirely within the reformed constitutional order established in 1908. His reign opened with a further institutional development: in 1929, the Imperial Duma formalised a three-year electoral cycle, regularising the rhythm of Russian parliamentary life and shifting the balance of political initiative further toward the Chamber of Deputies.

The early years of Alexei II's reign coincided with a severe global economic contraction that tested the empire's finances and strained the coalition politics of the Duma. The Global War, which began in 1932 with Prussia's invasion of formerly German territories of the Union of European Republics, posed a more acute challenge — but Russia, like Scandinavia, Switzerland, and most of Latin America, maintained genuine neutrality throughout the conflict's entire course, never becoming a belligerent on either side. Russia's most consequential wartime contribution came at its conclusion: serving as the neutral intermediary that brokered the Asian theatre's ceasefire in 1940–41. In recognition of this role, Russia negotiated a permanent seat on the executive council of the Global Peace Council at its founding — a recognition of diplomatic utility rather than of battlefield sacrifice, and a status that gave Russia institutional weight in the postwar order disproportionate to its non-belligerent wartime record.

Russia's reputation for careful diplomatic positioning was tested severely by the Chosŏn War (1966–1968). When a Chinese-backed coup overthrew the legitimate Royal Council government of Chosŏn in early 1966, Russia extended diplomatic cover at the Global Peace Council, framing the coup as an internal constitutional dispute rather than the externally sponsored seizure of power that it plainly was. This cover — transparently thin but sufficient to prevent clean international condemnation in the war's early phases — held for roughly eighteen months, fraying steadily as the conflict ground on without resolution. Following the NAU's bombing of Beijing and Shanghai in August 1967, Russia withdrew its diplomatic cover, a shift communicated through abstentions and quiet back-channel signals rather than public announcement — but one that proved decisive in making the eventual settlement possible.

Russia paid a real diplomatic price for its role. In the negotiations that produced the Treaty of Delhi (1968), the Global Peace Council responded to Russia's material and diplomatic support for the coup government by doubling down on the cause of Finnish and Polish self-determination. The personal union arrangements established in the 1920s, already a source of domestic friction in both nations, hardened over the following years into something considerably closer to genuine independence — Poland and Finland retaining the Russian tsar as nominal head of state in a relationship now widely compared to the Dominion status of Canada or Australia within the British Commonwealth, but with full and unambiguous control over their own domestic and foreign affairs. See Foreign Relations, below.

Government

Russia is governed as a constitutional monarchy. The Tsar serves as head of state with significant but constitutionally limited executive authority. Following the reforms of 1908, the tsar retains a suspensive veto over Duma legislation, which may be overridden by a supermajority of the Chamber of Deputies. The tsar also retains meaningful influence over foreign policy and military command, though major decisions require ministerial countersignature.

The Imperial Duma is bicameral, consisting of the Chamber of Peers, composed of hereditary and appointed nobles, and the Chamber of Deputies, whose members are elected by universal male suffrage on a three-year cycle established in 1929. The Prime Minister is the head of government and is responsible to the Chamber of Deputies. Government is typically formed by coalition, as no single party has consistently commanded a Duma majority.

The principal political parties are the Party of the Russian People (liberal reformist), the Agrarian Union (rural and provincial conservative), the Labor and Socialist Party (urban working class), the Russian Imperial Party (nationalist conservative), and the Russian Modernist Party (economic modernisation).

Foreign Relations

Russia maintains diplomatic relations with all major powers and has pursued a broadly non-interventionist foreign policy through nearly the entire constitutional era, with the significant exception of the War of Balkan Liberation in 1881–1882. Russia sat out both the Great European War and the Global War as a genuine neutral, a posture that has defined its distinctive diplomatic character among the world's major powers. Its permanent seat on the Global Peace Council's executive council — secured through its role as neutral broker of the Asian ceasefire in 1940–41 — gives it institutional standing alongside states whose claim to great-power status rests on a very different foundation.

Russia's diplomatic standing suffered a genuine setback during the Chosŏn War (1966–1968), when its cover for the Chinese-backed coup government strained its relationships across the GPC framework and produced lasting consequences closer to home. Poland and Finland, which had existed in personal union with the Russian crown since the 1920s, moved decisively toward genuine independence in the settlement's aftermath — retaining the tsar as nominal head of state while exercising full control over their own affairs, in an arrangement now generally understood as comparable to Commonwealth Dominion status. Republican movements exist in both countries, arguing that even the nominal monarchical link should be severed entirely. The Finnish Republican movement has gained considerable and growing traction in recent years, supported by a generation with no living memory of direct Russian governance and an increasingly assertive Nordic regional identity; the Polish movement remains comparatively marginal, though it persists as an active current in domestic politics.

Relations with the Ottoman Empire have been persistently strained by the condition of Armenian Christians within Ottoman territory, a matter Russia has formally claimed the right to raise since the Treaty of Adrianople. Relations with Austria-Hungary historically carried residual friction from competing interests in the Balkans, a dynamic substantially altered by that empire's dissolution following the Global War.

Economy

Russia's economy is primarily agricultural, though substantial industrialisation has occurred since the mid-nineteenth century. The empire is a significant producer of coal, iron, and grain, and its railroad network has expanded considerably since the first Saint Petersburg–Moscow line was completed in the 1840s. Through the middle decades of the twentieth century, Russia's industrial capacity, while growing, remained approximately ten to fifteen years behind the most advanced economies of western Europe and North America — a gap that has narrowed only partially in the decades since.
