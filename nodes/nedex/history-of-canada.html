This article covers the history of the Dominion of Canada from the aftermath of the War of 1812 through the present day. For an overview of Canada's government, geography, and institutions, see Dominion of Canada.

British North America (1814–1862)

The territory that became Canada was home to numerous indigenous peoples for thousands of years before European contact. British North America, as the collection of Crown colonies in northern North America was known, expanded significantly following the War of 1812, in which Britain acquired portions of the American states of New York and Michigan, as well as the Maine District. In the decades that followed, the colonies developed their own distinct political cultures and institutions, with the movement for federation gaining strength from the 1850s onward.

Political unrest marked the 1830s across both Upper and Lower Canada. In Lower Canada, the Parti Patriote, led by Louis-Joseph Papineau, demanded responsible government and protections for the French-speaking Catholic majority. When London rejected these demands, large-scale protests erupted. On June 3, 1837, British soldiers fired on a crowd in Montreal, killing nearly one hundred people in what became known as the Montreal Massacre. Papineau and other leaders were subsequently exiled rather than executed, and the summer of 1837 saw widespread riots across Lower Canada. Simultaneous unrest in Upper Canada was likewise suppressed.

The British response, formalised in the Bagot Report of 1839 and the Canadas Act of 1841, established elected lower houses with genuine financial authority and executive cabinets responsible to those assemblies. Responsible government was subsequently extended to Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Newfoundland through the 1840s.

From the 1850s onward, the idea of federation gained momentum, driven partly by the desire to build a transcontinental railway to rival the United States. Two figures were central to this movement: Anthony Martin, a railroad entrepreneur from the Wisconsin district of Upper Canada, and Joseph Criswell, a politician and writer from Detroit. In 1857, they founded the Federation Party and began campaigning for political union across the colonies. The War Between the States in the United States, in which Canadian volunteers fought as part of the Army of Canada alongside British forces, provided a further unifying moment. By 1862, all colonies had passed resolutions calling for a constitutional convention.

The Littlefield Visit (1880)

The friendship forged between the United States and the nascent Canadian Dominion during the War Between the States was commemorated in dramatic fashion in 1880, when Canadian Prime Minister James Crawley extended an invitation for President Horace Littlefield to visit Canada to mark twenty years since the war's end. Littlefield's acceptance — announced in late 1879 — would mark the first time a sitting American president had ever travelled to foreign soil, generating considerable public debate and some political opposition in Congress, though no one could identify proper legal grounds to block the trip.

On April 7, 1880, Littlefield, his wife Augusta, their two eldest sons, and Exterior Secretary Elias Benjamin arrived at Niagara Falls, New York, and crossed the Peace Bridge into Niagara Falls, Upper Canada. At the midpoint of the span, the American party was met by Canadian Governor-General Sir Benjamin Heath and his wife, Princess Victoria — second daughter of Queen Charlotte I — along with Prime Minister Crawley, his wife Beatrice, and the Canadian Minister for Continental Affairs. American, Canadian, and British flags were waved by the thousands who turned out to witness the crossing. At Table Rock lookout, the President, Governor-General, and Prime Minister held a private discussion touching on border crossing arrangements and the possibility of Canada adopting the new standard time system the United States had recently implemented. A reception that evening at the Niagara Hotel saw toasts raised to "the two greatest democracies in North America."

Confederation (1863–1865)

The Montreal Conference, which opened on June 8, 1863, brought delegates from Columbia, Vancouver Island, Upper and Lower Canada, Newfoundland, Prince Edward Island, and Prince Rupert's Land together to agree on the broad outlines of what became the Montreal Plan. The Detroit constitutional assembly the following spring produced the Articles of Dominion, establishing the federal structure of the new country. Prince Rupert's Land was redesignated as the Interior Territory and the Northern Territory, while all other colonies were granted province status. Foreign affairs and judicial finality remained with London.

The British North America Act of 1864 formally approved the Articles of Dominion in Parliament. Sir Edmund Head was appointed as the first Governor-General, assuming office in a ceremony at York — now renamed Toronto — in March 1865. Elections that summer saw Joseph Criswell elected as Canada's first Prime Minister, with Anthony Martin appointed as the first Interior Minister.

The Trans-Canadian Railway (1865–1883)

The Trans-Canadian Railway was approved by Parliament in the fall of 1865 and construction began in 1866. The primary route ran from Toronto northward around the Great Lakes, then west across the Great Plains, over the Rockies, and ultimately to Pugetsburgh on the Pacific coast. A secondary line connected Tecumseh — on the border with the United States at Calumet — to the primary route at Fond du Lac. The project took nearly two decades and was completed in the early 1880s, opening the interior of the continent to mass settlement and becoming a defining expression of early Canadian nationalism.

Prairie Settlement and the Métis Question (1870s–1890s)

The completion of the Trans-Canadian Railway brought tens of thousands of settlers into the Interior Territory, bringing them into conflict with the Métis community of the Red River valley — a distinct people of mixed European and indigenous heritage, with their own language, Michif, and a French-Catholic religious tradition. The Métis held land according to a traditional river-lot system that was incompatible with the township survey grid being imposed by incoming settlers and federal administrators.

Following years of tension and periodic confrontation, the federal government negotiated a settlement resulting in the creation of the Province of Assiniboia in the late 1880s. Small in area but significant in symbolic importance, Assiniboia was the only province outside Quebec where French was the primary language of a majority of the population. Its boundaries were expanded to incorporate portions of the northern prairie that had previously fallen outside the original Red River district.

As agricultural settlement grew, the Interior Territory was further subdivided. The Province of Saskatchewan was carved out in the 1890s, followed by the Province of Athabasca and the Province of Alexandria in the early 1900s.

Alaska (1878–1902)

In 1878, Canada purchased Alaska Territory from the Russian Empire, extending its sovereignty to the northwestern tip of the continent. The territory initially incorporated the Yukon and the northern interior of the Pacific Northwest in addition to the Alaska peninsula itself. A major gold rush in the late 1890s rapidly expanded the territory's population, creating pressure for provincial status, which was granted around the turn of the century. Alaska became Canada's fifteenth province approximately 1898–1902.

The Capital Question (1880s–1900s)

Toronto served as the de facto capital of the Dominion in its early years, but the city's status was always understood to be provisional. The debate over a permanent capital was prolonged and at times bitter, with Toronto and Montreal interests each unwilling to concede the prize to the other. In the mid-1880s, Parliament resolved to construct a purpose-built capital on neutral ground, modelled loosely on the American example of Franklin, District of Washington.

The site chosen was Manitoulin Island in Lake Huron, constituted as the District of Manitoulin — a federal district separate from all provinces. The new capital city was named Aurora. Construction began in the late 1880s, and Aurora became the operational seat of government in the early 1900s. The District contains the Wikwemikong Unceded Indian Reserve, whose people did not sign the treaty under which the island was acquired by the federal government — a situation that has remained a live political and civil rights issue throughout the twentieth century.

The Great European War and Imperial Reform (1899–1910s)

The Great European War (1899–1904) did not directly involve Canada as a combatant, but its aftermath transformed the British Empire and Canada's place within it. The formal independence of Ireland, the creation of the Dominion of India, and the broader reform of imperial relationships shifted Canada toward greater autonomy in foreign affairs. The appointment of increasingly Canada-rooted Governors-General in the years that followed reflected this gradual shift.

Canada participated in the First World Olympic Games, held in Athens in 1910, and was represented at the First International Peace Council in London in 1907, which gave rise to the Oslo Accords and several lasting international institutions.

The North American Conference (1925–1932)

Canada was a founding participant in the First North American Conference (1925–1926), which produced the North American Trade Council — a framework for economic cooperation among Canada, the United States, the Texas Federation, the Union of the Californias, and the Empire of Mexico. Prime Minister William Mackay's government regarded the Trade Council as a signature achievement. Canada subsequently participated in the Second North American Conference (1930), which produced the North American Open Trade Agreement, deepening continental economic integration.

In June 1928, the Anglo-Canadian relationship was cemented in a highly personal way when Prince Charles of Britain married Lady Elizabeth Carlisle of Canada, daughter of Canadian Interior Minister James Carlisle, in a ceremony attended by President Hawkins of the United States.

The Global War (1932–1941)

When the Global War began in 1932, Canada was immediately drawn into the conflict through its imperial connection to Britain. The proximate cause of Canada's domestic political crisis was the sinking of a British passenger vessel by Prussian U-boats, believed to have been sailing to or from Canada. Prime Minister Frederick Hollis, who had been reelected in 1930 on an explicit platform of non-involvement, lost a confidence vote within weeks. George Alderton of the Federation Party took office and immediately formed a National Unity Government, with cabinet seats drawn from all three major parties.

Canada fought as part of the Allied coalition for the duration of the conflict. An uncontested continuation vote in 1937 confirmed the unity government for the war's remaining years. The war in Europe concluded in 1938 and the armistice with China followed in 1940. Pressure for a peacetime election built rapidly. Alderton held out until the formal peace settlement was signed in late 1941, calling an election within days of the treaty — and losing to a Labour landslide.

The Dunbar Era and Postwar Reconstruction (1941–1952)

Margaret Dunbar, a labour lawyer from Hamilton, Ontario, swept the 1941 election and became Canada's first female Prime Minister. Her government presided over postwar reconstruction, the implementation of the Geneva settlement, and the early framework of what would become the North American Union. Dunbar won reelection in 1946 and again in 1950. The Nolan and Priest administrations in the United States created real friction during this period; Dunbar was quietly but firmly contemptuous of their political direction and made no secret of it in diplomatic contexts. She resigned in 1952 due to failing health and died in 1958.

Mid-Century and the North American Union (1952–1983)

The postwar decades saw the gradual deepening of Canadian involvement in continental integration. Under Prime Minister Henri Lacombe — the first Francophone Prime Minister — relations with the United States warmed dramatically during the Lincoln era, and North American Union integration accelerated significantly. Quebec nationalism reached its height in the late 1960s and early 1970s, testing the national unity of successive governments without producing a definitive resolution. Prime Minister Thomas Adeyemi, Canada's first Black Prime Minister, presided over a decade of relative stability and continued integration through the 1960s and into the 1970s.

Late Twentieth Century (1983–2000)

The longest-serving Prime Minister in Canadian history, William Osei, held office for seventeen years from 1983 to 2000. A Ghanaian-Canadian born in Alexandria Province, Osei oversaw the full consolidation of the North American Union framework, Canada's emergence as a confident middle power, and the deepening of social equality legislation that in several areas outpaced parallel developments in the United States. His tenure represented a period of unusual political stability for Canada.

Contemporary Era (2000–Present)

The early twenty-first century has seen Canada navigate the tensions between deepening North American integration and the periodic resurgence of Dominion Party scepticism toward that project. The question of indigenous rights — and in particular the unresolved status of the Wikwemikong Unceded Indian Reserve within the District of Manitoulin — has become an increasingly prominent political issue. Prime Minister Claire Beaumont was the first to formally acknowledge the Wikwemikong situation as an unresolved injustice requiring resolution. Her successor, Rebecca Standingbear, Canada's first indigenous Prime Minister, made it a defining issue of her government.

The current Prime Minister is Priya Anand of the Labour Party, who took office following the 2025 election.
