William Crawford
5th President of the United States (1817-1821)

William Crawford (1772–1834) was the fifth President of the United States, serving a single four-year term from 1817 to 1821. A Georgia lawyer and senator who had served as Secretary of War and Secretary of the Treasury under James Madison, Crawford inherited a republic in crisis — economically battered, territorially diminished, and constitutionally unsettled after the catastrophe of the War of 1812. His single term is remembered almost entirely for one achievement: the Grand Compromise of 1817, which produced the constitutional amendments and political agreements that set the republic on a new course and established the framework under which it has operated ever since.

Crawford was the last president to serve under the original four-year term established by the Constitution of 1787. The constitutional changes produced by the Grand Compromise — including the single six-year presidential term — took effect for his successor, making Crawford a transitional figure between the founding era and the modern republic.

Early life and career

William Crawford was born in Virginia in 1772 and grew up in Georgia, where he trained as a lawyer and entered politics. He served as a U.S. Senator from Georgia from 1807 to 1813, during which time he built a reputation as a capable, pragmatic negotiator — a man more interested in getting things done than in ideological purity. He served as Secretary of War under James Madison and then as Secretary of the Treasury, and was widely regarded as one of the most able administrators in the federal government by the time he won the presidency in 1816.

His running mate, Daniel Tompkins, was killed in a carriage accident on December 19, 1816 — before Crawford had even taken office. This left the incoming president with no vice president and set in motion the chain of events that would produce the Grand Compromise.

Presidency

A republic in crisis

Crawford took office in March 1817 governing from Philadelphia, where the federal government had relocated following the destruction of Washington City in 1814. The republic was in poor shape. The Treaty of Ghent had cost the United States significant territory — parts of Maine, portions of New York, and the whole of Michigan — and the outgoing Madison Administration had negotiated a supplementary Wisconsin Treaty that proposed ceding still more territory to Britain in exchange for Mississippi River rights. The economy was struggling, Federalists in New England were openly discussing secession in the wake of the Hartford Convention, and the Democratic-Republican Party was fracturing along Southern and Western lines.

Crawford also faced an immediate constitutional problem: his vice president was dead, the Constitution provided no mechanism for filling a VP vacancy, and this was now the third consecutive time the vice presidency had been left vacant. Crawford initially proposed simply appointing James Monroe to the position, but found that many in Congress doubted the constitutional authority for such an appointment. He turned the problem into an opportunity.

The Crawford Conference and the Grand Compromise of 1817

After months of congressional deadlock on multiple fronts — the VP vacancy, the Wisconsin Treaty, Federalist demands from the Hartford Resolution, and the Western faction's desire for a new capital — Crawford invited congressional leaders to the executive residence in Philadelphia in May 1817. What emerged from those negotiations was the Grand Compromise of 1817, one of the most consequential political agreements in American history.

The Compromise had four main components, each satisfying a different faction:

  • For Crawford — the 13th Amendment established a mechanism to fill vice-presidential vacancies by presidential appointment with Senate approval. Monroe was immediately appointed and confirmed.
  • For the Federalists — the 14th Amendment limited the presidency to a single six-year term and prohibited consecutive presidents from the same state; the 15th Amendment required a two-thirds majority in both houses to declare war or admit new states.
  • For all parties — opposition to the Wisconsin Treaty was dropped, settling the boundary dispute with Britain.
  • For the Western Democrats — the federal government would establish a new capital city on the Ohio River, to be built from scratch and named Franklin in honor of Benjamin Franklin.

The Compromise was announced on May 31, 1817. The 13th Amendment was ratified by November, allowing Monroe's appointment to proceed. The 14th Amendment was ratified by December 20, 1817. The 15th Amendment followed in September 1818.

There was, however, a complication. The 14th Amendment's passage had depended in part on Crawford's pledge not to seek reelection in 1820. Legal experts noted that the amendment's language technically allowed Crawford to run again since it applied to the president elected in 1820 — and when the election approached, Crawford shocked the nation by announcing he was reneging on his pledge and considering a run. He ultimately decided against it, but the episode damaged his reputation and formalized the split of the Democratic-Republican Party into the Democrat Party and the Republican Party.

Building Franklin

With the constitutional questions settled, Crawford turned his attention to the practical work of establishing the new capital. In the spring of 1818, the Congressional Committee for the District of Washington held design competitions for the new city and its federal buildings. The winning plan — an amalgamation of proposals from Josiah Kemp of New York and Phillip Ogden of South Carolina — was unveiled on March 15, 1818. Crawford broke ground in May 1818, personally overseeing the clearing of what would become Union Avenue.

The capital bill set a ten-year deadline for completion — the city of Franklin was to be ready for the government no later than 1827, by the end of the next president's term. Crawford also worked to encourage westward settlement and rebuild trade relationships with France and Britain in his remaining years in office.

The 1820 election and the party schism

Crawford's announcement that he would attempt to seek reelection — coming after months of speculation that he might — formalized the split within the Democratic-Republican Party that had been building for years. The Western faction coalesced around Henry Clay and became the Democrat Party; the Southern faction, which Crawford had hoped to lead, rejected his bid and rallied behind James Monroe and became the Republican Party. The Federalists nominated John Cotton Smith of Connecticut. Clay won the election with 147 electoral votes, defeating Monroe's 74 and Smith's 53.

Crawford left office in March 1821 and returned to Georgia, where he became a prominent and outspoken member of the new Republican Party — a fact that made him an uncomfortable figure in Democratic Party mythology, given that he was nominally the last Democratic-Republican president and had set in motion the party's schism.

Legacy

Crawford's presidency is defined almost entirely by the Grand Compromise. It was not a triumph of vision — Crawford was a pragmatic operator, not a transformative leader — but it was a triumph of political management at a moment when the republic desperately needed someone capable of holding competing factions together long enough to reach agreement. The constitutional framework he helped produce has governed the United States for over two centuries.

He is also remembered, less flatteringly, for his pledge-breaking episode regarding the 14th Amendment — a moment that historians frequently cite as an early example of the tensions between constitutional design and political self-interest. He died in Elberton, Georgia, in 1834.
