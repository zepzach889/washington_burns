Treaty of New Orleans
Signed March 3, 1837 · Ended the First Mexican War

The Treaty of New Orleans was the agreement signed on March 3, 1837 that ended the First Mexican War. Negotiated by delegations from the United States, the Republic of Mexico, the Empire of Mexico, the Province of Texas, and the Texas Free State, it confirmed the partition of Mexico into two sovereign states, created an autonomous Texas Free State within the Republic, and bound the United States to a pledge against the annexation of Mexican territory that would govern American continental policy for seventy years.

The Senate approved the treaty within weeks of its signature. The settlement it produced held for six years, and every question it deferred returned in the Second Mexican War.

Background

Two years of attrition had exhausted both Mexicos and much of the American appetite for the war. The campaigns of 1836 produced no breakthrough for either side, and the assassination of President John C. Calhoun in May of that year, while it briefly revived enthusiasm in the South and West, did not change the military situation.

By the opening of 1837 President Manuel Ortega of the Republic and General Raul Guerra of the Imperialist faction had both concluded that the war could not be won at a price either could pay. A ceasefire was agreed in February, and delegates convened at New Orleans the following month.

The delegations

Five delegations attended, of markedly unequal standing. The United States and the two Mexican governments negotiated as principals. The Province of Texas, represented by the government Anthony Benton had built around Franklin-on-the-Brazos and San Antonio, and the self-styled Texas Free State of Stephen Austin attended as parties to a question rather than as sovereign powers.

The arrangement was without precedent, and the Imperial delegation objected to it throughout on the ground that seating the Texans conceded in form what the treaty was intended to deny in substance.

Terms

The territorial settlement assigned to the Republic of Mexico the provinces of California, Nuevo Mexico, Texas, Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Nuevo León, and Tamaulipas, with the remainder of the country to the Empire. Each government renounced all claim upon the territory of the other.

The line as drawn was more generous to the Republic than the position of the armies warranted, and the Empire never fully vacated the southern districts of the five northern provinces it was assigned to surrender — a discrepancy understood at New Orleans and left unaddressed.

The Texas settlement separated the eastern districts from the Province of Texas and constituted them as the Texas Free State, nominally part of the Republic but possessed of near-total autonomy within its borders. Austin became its Chief Executive. Benton was left governing the remainder of the province from Franklin-on-the-Brazos.

The Empire's price for agreement was an American undertaking never to annex the Free State or any other Mexican territory. The administration of President Giles Brandon gave it without hesitation, having spent two years insisting that the war was not one of expansion.

Ratification and reception

The Senate approved the treaty swiftly. Emperor Raul I, proclaimed that same year, maintained publicly and for the remainder of his reign that the borders had been unjust and imposed upon his country by the Americans — a grievance that supplied the justification for the invasion of 1843.

Legacy

The non-annexation pledge proved the treaty's most durable element. It was renewed at New Orleans in 1847 in a form covering two new republics, and cited again at Copenhagen in 1906, by which time it had hardened from a concession extracted by a defeated adversary into settled American doctrine.

The Texas settlement proved its failure. It created within the Republic an autonomous polity governed by men who had wanted independence and now held most of its substance, alongside a rump province governed by the man who had stopped them. The arrangement survived Benton's assassination in January of 1839 by a week of fighting in Galvesport, San Antonio, and Franklin-on-the-Brazos, and outlasted Ortega by months.
