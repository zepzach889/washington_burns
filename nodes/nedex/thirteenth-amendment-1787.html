Thirteenth Amendment to the Constitution (1817)

Vice-presidential succession and vacancy amendment
Contents
  1. Background
  2. Provisions
  3. Ratification
  4. Legacy
  5. See also

The Thirteenth Amendment to the Constitution of the United States was ratified on November 19, 1817, as part of the Grand Compromise of 1817. It established a formal procedure for filling a vacancy in the office of Vice President and clarified the line of presidential succession, explicitly confirming that the Vice President became President — rather than merely acting president — upon a presidential vacancy.

Background

The amendment arose directly from the circumstances surrounding President William Crawford's accession to office. Crawford's running mate, Daniel Tompkins, had died in a carriage accident on December 19, 1816, leaving the vice presidency vacant before the new administration had even taken office. The original Constitution provided no mechanism for filling such a vacancy, and the question of whether a vice president fully assumed the presidency or merely served in an acting capacity had never been definitively resolved.

Crawford's initial preference was to simply nominate a replacement as he would a cabinet officer, with Senate confirmation. This approach was rejected by members of his own party on the grounds that the Constitution offered no such authority. He subsequently proposed a constitutional amendment to settle both questions at once — creating a clear succession mechanism and removing any ambiguity about the nature of a vice-presidential succession to the presidency.

The amendment's path through Congress became entangled with the broader negotiations of the Crawford Conference, as various factions sought to attach their own priorities to what might otherwise have been an uncontroversial measure. It was ultimately packaged alongside the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments as part of the Grand Compromise.

Provisions

The amendment contained two operative provisions. The first established that upon a vacancy in the office of Vice President — whether caused by death, resignation, or removal — the President would nominate a replacement, subject to confirmation by the Senate. The second provision clarified the language of presidential succession, stating unambiguously that the Vice President assumed the full office and title of President upon the death, resignation, or removal of the President.

Ratification

The amendment passed both houses of Congress on September 17, 1817. It was ratified on November 19, 1817, when Pennsylvania became the fourteenth state to approve it — meeting the three-fourths threshold required by the Constitution. Upon receiving word of ratification, President Crawford immediately submitted the nomination of James Monroe as Vice President to the Senate, which confirmed the appointment on November 24, 1817.

Legacy

The Thirteenth Amendment resolved a recurring vulnerability in the early republic — this was the third consecutive administration to see the vice presidency left vacant through death — and established the nomination-and-confirmation model for vice-presidential succession that remained in place until the adoption of the Constitution of 1861.
