Alexander Hamilton, Jr.
7th President of the United States (1821-1827)

Aleander Hamilton, Jr. (1786–1873) was the seventh President of the United States, serving a single six-year term from 1827 to 1833. The son of Alexander Hamilton — the first Secretary of the Treasury and one of the founders of the republic — he was the first president inaugurated in the new capital of Franklin, District of Washington, the first Federalist to win the presidency since John Adams, and one of the most controversial presidents of his era. His victory owed more to the contingent election process than to his party's national standing — he had received the fewest electoral votes of the three major candidates — and the shadow of illegitimacy that followed cast a pall over his entire term.

Hamilton's presidency was ambitious in intent and troubled in execution. He inherited a city still under construction, a republic fractured by the second consecutive contingent election, and a Native American policy debate that would consume much of his six years in office. He left behind a road network considerably expanded, a capital somewhat more finished, a landmark treaty with the Native nations, and a political culture even more convinced that the electoral system needed fixing — a conviction that produced the 16th Amendment to the Constitution of 1787 under his successor. Despite his troubled tenure, the Federalist Party would go on to become one of the republic's two dominant parties in the decades following the War Between the States, vindicating in the long run the constitutional ideals Hamilton championed even when he could not always act on them.

Early life and career

Alexander Hamilton, Jr. was born in New York in 1786, the son of Alexander Hamilton and Eliza Schuyler Hamilton. He trained as a lawyer — following his father's profession — and entered politics as a congressman from New York, where he built a reputation as a capable if not especially prominent Federalist legislator. His family name was both his greatest asset and a complicated inheritance: the Federalist Party had been founded in large part by his father, and his nomination for the presidency in 1826 was at least partly a product of the symbolic weight of that name rather than his own political achievements.

The Election of 1826

The 1826 Presidential Election produced the second consecutive contingent election in American history. The three candidates — Hamilton for the Federalists, John C. Calhoun for the Republicans, and Thomas Worthington for the Democrats — each carried their regional base without any achieving a majority. Calhoun received 93 electoral votes, Hamilton 92, and Worthington 89 — a near-perfect three-way split.

Under the Constitution, the House of Representatives selected the president from the top three candidates, with each state delegation casting one vote. Calhoun, having received the most electoral votes, expected to be chosen. But Thomas Worthington threw his support behind Hamilton, and the House voted overwhelmingly for the Federalist candidate. Republicans cried foul immediately — and their anger deepened when Hamilton appointed Worthington as his Secretary of War, which they characterized as a corrupt bargain. They called it the Devil's Bargain, and the charge would hound Hamilton throughout his presidency.

Presidency

Inauguration in Franklin

When Hamilton took the oath of office on March 4, 1827, he became the first president inaugurated in the new capital. The ceremony was held on the steps of Congress Hall, whose gleaming facade, twin domes, and central rotunda impressed the assembled crowd — though the interiors were still largely unfinished. Much of the city was in a similar state: roads had been laid out, private lots sold, and some buildings completed, but most of Franklin remained a worksite. Union Avenue had been cleared on both sides of the Ohio River, but paving existed only near Congress Hall and Washington House, and the promised bridge linking the two banks was still decades away. Despite these conditions, the crowd was reportedly exuberant that the new capital was finally being occupied.

Infrastructure and road building

Hamilton inherited Henry Clay's ambitious road-building agenda and worked to advance it, though with more congressional resistance than he had hoped. The Federal Bureau of Improvements under Director Josiah Colfax — a holdover from the Clay Administration — proposed extending the First National Road all the way to St. Louis, while simultaneously beginning work on the Second National Road from Cumberland to Boston and the Third National Road from Cumberland to Charleston.

The St. Louis extension became the subject of a prolonged congressional fight. Colfax's plan routed the road through Indiana and Illinois on the north bank of the Ohio River; Southerners wanted it routed through Kentucky. The compromise that eventually emerged kept the northern route but required a resolution of the Arkansas Territory question as part of the deal. Arkansas Territory was split in two — the eastern portion became the State of Arkansas, admitted to the Union in 1828, while the western portion was set aside to eventually become the State of Jefferson, whose statehood was delayed until after Hamilton left office. Republicans in Congress also secured increased funding for the Third National Road. The upper section of Iowa Territory was simultaneously broken off to form Minnesota Territory, while the lower section became the State of Iowa, admitted to the Union in 1830 — bringing the total number of states to 26.

The other roads Hamilton had envisioned — connecting Franklin to Atlanta, Franklin to Calumet, St. Louis to New Orleans, and Charleston to Mobile — never left the drawing board, consumed by the political battles that dominated his term.

The Native American Question and the Franklin Indian Treaty

The issue that most thoroughly defined Hamilton's presidency was the growing conflict over Native American peoples in the southern states. In 1828, Georgia passed a series of laws stripping non-English-speaking, non-Christian "non-citizen residents" of the right to own land — a direct attack on the Cherokee Nation. The resulting Cherokee Nation v. Georgia case reached the Supreme Court in 1830. In one of the last major decisions of the Marshall Court, the justices ruled 5-2 in favor of the Cherokee: states had no power to enforce laws on members of Native nations, and the federal government needed a permanent solution to the question of dual jurisdiction.

Southern Republicans were outraged. Senator John C. Calhoun introduced legislation demanding the removal of the Native nations west of the Mississippi. The bill narrowly failed. Georgia Governor Clarence Hinds announced the state would not comply with the ruling and began plans to parcel out Cherokee lands to white settlers. Hamilton ordered federal troops into Georgia to enforce the court's decision — placing the federal government and the southern states on a collision course.

As the summer of 1830 began, federal troops stood off against Georgia militiamen on the border of Cherokee Country. Delegates from Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi gathered in Savannah and issued the Savannah Declaration, demanding troop withdrawal and federal removal legislation, with language echoing the old Hartford Resolution and implying the possibility of secession. Militia groups slipped past army patrols and attacked Cherokee towns; the Cherokee retaliated.

Hamilton summoned southern leaders to Washington House and negotiated a compromise. The result — the Franklin Indian Treaty, signed September 1, 1830 — established two protected Native districts: the Cherokee-Creek District in Georgia and Alabama, and the Choctaw-Chickasaw District in northern Mississippi, each roughly one-quarter the size of the original tribal lands. Natives living outside those districts had one year to relocate or forfeit tribal citizenship and integrate into white society. The treaty also transferred all Native relations from the War Department to the State Department, creating the Office of Indian Relations and allowing each tribal nation to send a diplomatic representative to Franklin.

No party was fully satisfied. Violence accompanied the relocation process. Republicans continued to attack the agreement as insufficient. But the treaty averted outright war and established a framework for Native-federal relations that endured for decades.

The shadow of the Devil's Bargain

Throughout his six years in office, Hamilton was never fully able to escape the charge that his presidency was illegitimate. The appointment of Worthington as Secretary of War gave the charge a surface plausibility that Republicans exploited at every opportunity, and Hamilton's reliance on Democratic support for most major legislation reinforced the perception that he governed on borrowed legitimacy rather than his own electoral authority.

The Election of 1832

The Federalists nominated Vice President Hiram Gillett with John Quincy Adams as his running mate. The Democrats nominated Indiana Representative Samuel Luther with Ohio Governor Blane Cartwright. The Republicans renominated John C. Calhoun with Georgia Senator Giles Brandon as his vice-presidential candidate.

The result surprised New Englanders but few others: the Federalists came in third with 103 electoral votes, the Democrats second with 145, and the Republicans first with 161 — still 44 short of an outright majority. Once again, the House of Representatives would choose the winner of the presidential election. The House met in February of 1833, and selected Calhoun on the first ballot, finally giving him the presidency he felt he had been denied in 1826. There had been serious behind-the-scenes pressure to avoid a repeat of the 1827 Devils Bargain, so there had been no serious attempt to give the presidency to Luther or Gillett. The 16th Amendment to the Constitution of 1787, passed under Calhoun in 1833, replaced the majority requirement with a plurality, ending the era of contingent elections.

Legacy

Hamilton's presidency is remembered with mixed feelings. He advanced the road network, completed the move to Franklin, maintained the Federalist Party's commitment to constitutional governance and opposition to Native removal in the face of considerable pressure, and negotiated the Franklin Indian Treaty at a moment when outright war seemed possible. But his administration was defined more by its embattled character than its achievements — governing under the constant shadow of the Devil's Bargain, never fully master of the political situation he inhabited.

His son, Alexander Hamilton III, later served as the appointed administrator of the District of Manhattan during the post-war reconstruction period — another generation of the Hamilton family leaving its mark on the republic's physical landscape.
