This article lists the monarchs of the Empire of Russia from the reign of Alexander I through the present day. The constitutional era began formally with the promulgation of the Constitution of 1854 under Tsar Michael II. All tsars are listed with biographical notes on their reigns' defining events. ✦ denotes a monarch who died in office.

Biographical Notes

Alexander I (r. 1801–1835)

Alexander I emerged from the Napoleonic Wars as one of the dominant figures of continental Europe, having broken decisively with the conservative order championed by Prince Metternich over the question of Greek independence in the 1820s. His most consequential domestic initiative was the beginning of the serf emancipation program in 1830, which allowed serfs on state and imperial lands to purchase their freedom — a process that would take his successors decades to complete. He died in November 1835 without finishing the work he had begun.

Michael II (r. 1835–1864)

Michael II continued and expanded the emancipation program with greater urgency than his brother. In 1837 serfs were declared no longer in personal bondage to their noble masters; in 1852 he announced that children born on or after January 1, 1853 would be born free; and on August 13, 1861 he signed legislation mandating that all remaining serfs would be freed no later than December 31, 1866. His most enduring achievement was the convening of a constitutional assembly in Saint Petersburg that produced Russia's first formal constitution on September 1, 1854, establishing the Imperial Duma and placing meaningful limits on the tsar's absolute authority for the first time.

Michael III (r. 1864–1893)

Michael III inherited a country mid-emancipation and a constitution barely a decade old, and his task was consolidation rather than further reform. The emancipation of the remaining serfs was completed in 1866 under his watch, and substantial investment was directed toward railroad construction and industrial development. The defining foreign policy event of his reign was the War of Balkan Liberation of 1881, in which Russia intervened against the Ottoman Empire on behalf of Slavic Christian peoples in the Balkans — a short, successful war that recognised the independence or autonomy of Bulgaria, Serbia, Montenegro, and Romania and significantly enhanced Russia's prestige as the patron of Slavic nationalism. He died in 1893 having governed for nearly three decades.

Alexander III (r. 1893–1928)

Alexander III's thirty-five-year reign was the most eventful of the constitutional era. He presided over sustained industrial growth and kept Russia out of the Great European War (1899–1904) — a decision deeply contested domestically. The Russian Constitutional Crisis of 1907, precipitated by Prime Minister Valuev-Stroganov's disastrous dissolution of the Duma, nearly toppled the political order; Alexander III recognised the error, dismissed Valuev-Stroganov, and negotiated with Alexei Lvov of the Party of the Russian People a reform settlement in 1908 that substantially curtailed the tsar's appointment powers and extended the franchise to universal male suffrage. In the 1920s, under Prime Minister Trubetskoy, his government negotiated the personal union arrangements with Poland and Finland that gave both nations substantial domestic autonomy while retaining the tsar as head of state. He died in 1928 at the age of seventy.

Alexei II (r. 1928–1970)

Alexei II came to the throne in 1928 at thirty-six, the first tsar to have grown up entirely within the reformed constitutional order. His early reign saw the Duma Electoral Reform of 1929, which formalised the three-year electoral cycle and shifted political initiative decisively toward the Chamber of Deputies. Russia maintained genuine neutrality throughout the Global War (1932–1940), and it was Alexei II's government — under Prime Minister Belov — that brokered the Asian ceasefire of 1940–41 as a neutral intermediary, securing Russia a permanent seat on the Global Peace Council's executive council in recognition of that role. The Chosŏn War (1966–1968), during which Russia extended diplomatic cover to the Chinese-backed coup government in Korea before withdrawing it, occurred in the final years of his reign; the diplomatic price — GPC pressure escalating Polish and Finnish autonomy toward genuine Dominion-style independence — was among the most consequential developments of his time on the throne. He died in 1970 at the age of seventy-eight, having reigned for forty-two years.

Alexei III (r. 1970–1999)

Alexei III reigned for twenty-nine years through the full transition of Poland and Finland from personal union to something functionally indistinguishable from independent statehood — a process that accelerated under GPC pressure following the Chosŏn War's aftermath and hardened through the 1970s and 1980s. The Finnish Republican movement grew in strength and visibility during his reign; the Polish equivalent remained more marginal but persistent. Further details to be established.

Michael IV (r. 1999–present)

Michael IV ascended to the throne in 1999 and remains the incumbent tsar, now into his twenty-seventh year of reign as of 2026. His tenure has continued the constitutional monarchy's role as a stabilising institution above party politics in a period of continued GPC engagement and evolving relationships with Poland and Finland. His daughter and heir apparent has in recent years become an increasingly public figure, widely understood to be preparing for the day she will become Russia's first reigning Tsarina — a prospect that has drawn both public fascination and, from some corners of the Russian Imperial Party, the same dynastic anxieties that have accompanied female succession questions across the empire's history.
