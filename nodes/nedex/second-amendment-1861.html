Second Amendment to the Constitution of 1861
The Moore Proposal · Ratified 1894
Contents
  1. Background
  2. Provisions
  3. Ratification
  4. Legacy
  5. See also

The Second Amendment to the Constitution of 1861, ratified in the late summer of 1894 and commonly known as the Moore Proposal, reformed the contingent election system for choosing the president and vice president. It replaced the old split procedure — the House voting by state delegation for president, the Senate voting separately for vice president — with a single special joint session of Congress voting for a full presidential ticket, each member voting individually.

Background

The amendment answered the constitutional embarrassment of the election of 1892. With no ticket winning a majority of the electoral college, the House of Representatives elected Colin Beck, a Federalist, on its second ballot — while the Senate, voting separately, chose the Democratic vice-presidential candidate, Richard Towns, over Beck's own running mate Dale Hamlin, 47 votes to 33. For the first time since 1797, a president took the oath beside a vice president from an opposing party, and the capital spent the spring of 1893 debating how to make sure it never happened again. Rival proposals abounded: abolition of the vice presidency itself (which drew pushback from every party, led by Representative Milton Cargill of Jefferson), Senator James Fraser's scheme of fifty "at-will" electors, and — dismissed at the time as fringe ideas worth only a few days of newspaper discussion — the abolition of the electoral college and a national runoff election.

Provisions

The proposal of Senator Albert Moore of Connecticut prevailed: in any future contingent election, Congress would meet in a special joint session and vote for a complete ticket — president and vice president together — with each representative and senator casting an individual vote rather than voting as part of a state delegation, and any of the top three tickets eligible to receive votes.

Ratification

The Moore Proposal was presented to Congress on April 28, 1893, with the endorsement of both President Beck and Vice President Towns — the very men whose mismatched pairing had inspired it. Both houses passed the amendment by June and sent it to the states. Ratification required 31 of the 41 states and federal districts; several southern states resisted, fearing the changes would further disadvantage their preferred parties, but the threshold was reached by the late summer of 1894.

Legacy

The joint-session mechanism governed presidential contingencies for less than two decades: the Fourth Amendment of 1911 replaced the contingent election altogether with the modern runoff system. The Moore Proposal's deeper legacy was its principle — that the presidency and vice presidency form a single democratic choice — which the runoff era inherited intact. It is also remembered as a rare monument to bipartisanship under duress: an amendment jointly championed by a president and the opposition vice president he never chose.
