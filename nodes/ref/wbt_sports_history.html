Section One
The Sports Landscape
The Major Sports

By the mid-20th century, WBT's "major sports" landscape consists of five sports with genuinely distinct regional and cultural roots — no single sport occupies the cultural space that gridiron football occupies in OTL America. Two are US-only; three are continent-wide, spanning the USA, Texas, California, Mexico, and Canada.

National Pastime · US Only

Baseball — National Baseball Association (NBA) Hard Canon

The first sport to nationalize, spreading rapidly in the years following the War Between the States (1857–60) and becoming an early cross-class pastime. Cornelius Roosevelt II (b. 1861) grew up playing it alongside rugby and football/soccer. Early professional structure (specific clubs, leagues) TBD for a future pass. Note the deliberate echo: "NBA" here refers to baseball, not basketball — a different sport altogether claims a different OTL acronym below.

Continent-Wide · USA · Texas · California · Mexico · Canada

Football (Association Football) — North American Football Association (NAFA) Hard Canon

Arrives via British trade and immigration to industrial port cities and mill towns from the 1860s onward. Cross-racial and cross-class from its earliest American days — with no Jim Crow, the working communities where it took root were already diverse. Picked up by elite/college youth by the 1870s–80s (the "still new" sport of Cornelius Roosevelt's youth), without ever losing its broad popular base. That breadth gives football its unifying, "everyone's game" character, cemented by international legitimacy via the WOC and the 1910 Olympics.

Three Regional Traditions · Not Yet Unified

Rugby Hard Canon

Unlike football, rugby never coalesced into one national sport. Following Britain's 1871 RFU formalization, it takes root in America through three separate traditions — institutional, Appalachian, and Californian (detailed below) — each strong within its own context, with no shared governing body and no pressure to unify. Whether and when these traditions eventually converge into a continent-wide sport is a question for a much later era.

Native-State Rooted · US Only · Integrated from Origins

Stickball — All-Nation Stickball Association (ANSA) Hard Canon

Originates in Gigadohi (Choctaw/Chickasaw-rooted, statehood 1862), where it is already a living community tradition predating WBT's divergence. Spreads to Kanasaw and Lakota in the 1870s–80s, then to neighboring Mississippi Valley states through the 1880s–1900s. By 1910, "nationally known, regionally played." Integrated from its origins, given the close historical ties between Native and Black communities in and around Gigadohi. Professionalizes later, in the radio-boom 1920s. "All-Nation" doubles as a reference to the Native Nations (Gigadohi, Kanasaw, Lakota, and others) that anchor the sport — not literally "all nations of the continent."

Continent-Wide · USA · Texas · California · Mexico · Canada · Invented 1900, Texas

Aroball ("Wallball") — Major League Aroball (MLA) Hard Canon

Invented in 1900 by a Texas gym teacher — likely a husband-and-wife team, names TBD Open — inspired by an article describing both the ancient Mesoamerican ballgame (ōllamaliztli) and its surviving folk form (ulama, still played by a small number of practitioners in a few Sinaloa communities, unchanged by this invention). The new sport adds hand-play and ring/hoop scoring on the court's distinctive sloped side walls — innovations of the inventor(s), not a "revival" of the ancient game; the traditional ulama tradition continues to exist separately. Spreads to California and northern Mexico alongside Texas through the 1900s–10s (the close Texas–California relationship), broader Southwest/border consolidation in the 1920s–30s, continental spread in the 1940s–50s.

Naming: "Arobol" (Spanish, from aro = hoop/ring) anglicizes to "Aroball" as the sport's proper name; "Wallball" persists as a common English-language nickname referencing the court's sloped walls. Note the deliberate echo: "Major League ___" here refers to Aroball, not baseball — paired with the NBA/baseball swap above, both OTL-acronym winks point at different sports than expected.

Rugby's Three Traditions
Institutional

Elite-Institutional Rugby Soft Canon

Arrives via direct exchange/touring relationships between specific American and British schools and universities — a transplanted tradition, not a broadly-absorbed one. Specific institutions TBD.

Appalachia

Appalachian Rugby Soft Canon

Brought by Welsh coal-mining immigrant communities. A working-class, industrial-interior identity for the sport — genuinely unexpected relative to OTL's rugby geography.

California

Californian Rugby Soft Canon

Brought by Commonwealth immigrants (Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Fiji) as part of California's broader Pacific-facing, cosmopolitan post–Gold Rush population. Since California competes as its own Olympic delegation, this gives California international rugby rivalries distinct from the rest of North America.

International & Governing Bodies
Founded 1907

World Olympic Committee (WOC)

Founded by Ashcroft (Great Britain), von Altenburg (Prussia), and Bellamonte (Italy). American delegate Peter Sinclair sent by President Cornelius Roosevelt II. First Games: Athens, 1910.

Founded 1911

America Games / USOC

Cornelius Roosevelt II becomes USOC head and creates the "America Games" — a domestic mini-Olympics whose winners feed the World Games team. The model spreads internationally by mid-century. Marks the transition into the Olympic Era & Professionalization (1910–1918).

Section Two
Origins (1860s–1910)
Post-War Foundations — 1860s–1870s

Baseball spreads rapidly in the years following the War Between the States (1857–60), quickly establishing itself as an early cross-class pastime. In Gigadohi — which achieves statehood in 1862, its population rooted in Choctaw and Chickasaw communities — stickball is already a living tradition predating WBT's divergence entirely, now with a state platform of its own.

Football (association football) begins arriving via British trade and immigration to American industrial centers — port cities, mill towns — where it takes root among working communities. Because these neighborhoods are already racially mixed under WBT's no-Jim-Crow framework, football is cross-racial and cross-class from essentially its first days in America, not something that integrates later.

Rugby's three traditions begin taking root in roughly this same window, following Britain's 1871 RFU formalization: direct exchange ties bring rugby to certain American schools and universities; Welsh mining immigrants carry it to Appalachia; Commonwealth immigrants carry it to California. The three traditions develop independently, with no shared governing body and no expectation that there should be one.

Cornelius Roosevelt II's own youth (b. 1861) offers a window into elite multi-sport culture of the period — he grows up playing baseball, rugby, and football, with football still a relatively new pursuit for someone of his background even as it's already well-established elsewhere.

Expansion and Diversification — 1870s–1890s

Stickball spreads from Gigadohi to Kanasaw (statehood also 1862) and to Lakota (statehood 1882) over the course of the 1870s and 1880s, becoming established across all three Native states.

Football shifts from a working-class import to a genuinely cross-class sport during this period — elite and college youth adopt it (Cornelius Roosevelt's "still new" experience of it) without it ever shedding its broad popular base. This dual foundation — popular and elite simultaneously — is what gives football its "everyone's game" character going forward.

Rugby's three regional and institutional traditions solidify as distinct identities during these decades. There is no unification pressure and no shared body connecting them — each tradition is simply itself, fully formed within its own context.

By the 1880s, stickball begins spreading beyond the three Native states into neighboring Mississippi Valley states, the start of a process that continues into the new century.

Turn of the Century — 1890s–1910

By 1910, stickball has become "nationally known, regionally played" — its reputation extends across the country even though organized play remains concentrated in the Mississippi Valley. Full professionalization is still a generation away.

Section Three
Olympic Era & Professionalization (1910–1918)
The America Games & the World Stage

With WBT's Great European War (1899–1904) already over and the Global War (1932–40) decades away, 1910–1918 is a period of uninterrupted institutional growth for American sport — no major conflict disrupts the decade's Olympic cycles.

1911
Cornelius Roosevelt II, as USOC head, establishes the America Games — a centralized, more organized version of Olympic trials, scoped entirely to Olympic core disciplines (track and field, swimming, gymnastics, rowing, tennis, boxing, weightlifting, fencing, shooting, cycling). The Big Sports — baseball, football, stickball, Aroball, rugby — sit outside this system entirely, at least for now.
1913
First America Games held, selecting the US team for the 1914 London Games — the year-prior model giving the team a full season to prepare.
1914
1914 London Olympics. Modest, organic growth from 1910 Athens: roughly 55–60 events (up from 45), an American team of roughly 110–120 athletes (up from 76), and roughly 24–28 medals (up from 18).
1917
Second Summer America Games, selecting the US team for 1918 Rome. The WOC announces plans for a separate Winter Olympics cycle, with the inaugural Winter Games to be held in 1922.
1918
1918 Rome Olympics. Continued incremental growth: roughly 65–70 events, an American team of roughly 140–155 athletes, and roughly 28–32 medals.
The Regional League Era

Separately from the Olympic/America Games track, the Big Sports spend this decade somewhere on a path from regional/inter-city leagues toward national or continental coordination — but each sport sits at a different point on that path. Specific franchises and cities are deliberately left open for a later pass; this section establishes the structural shape only.

Consolidation · Most Mature

Baseball — The National Baseball Association Forms

Baseball's regional/inter-city leagues, dating back to the 1870s–80s, coordinate and consolidate into the NBA during this decade — the most advanced sport completing the regional-to-national transition first.

Parallel Regional Leagues · No Coordination Yet

Football — Multiple Regional Leagues Continue

Football's head start (broad popular base since the 1860s–70s, elite/college adoption by the 1870s–80s) means it already has several parallel regional leagues by 1910 — likely an industrial Northeast circuit, a Great Lakes/Midwest circuit, a Pacific Coast circuit, and a Gulf South circuit. These continue operating independently through this decade. Their eventual coordination into NAFA is the opening beat of the next era.

New Regional League · Precursor to ANSA

Stickball — Mississippi Valley League Forms

A new regional league forms in this decade, pulling together teams from Gigadohi, Kanasaw, and Lakota alongside the neighboring states that picked up stickball in the 1880s–1900s. This league is the seed ANSA will eventually grow from in the 1920s.

New Regional League · Precursor to MLA

Aroball — Southwest/Borderlands League Forms

A new regional league forms spanning Texas, California, and northern Mexico — the seed MLA will eventually grow from as Aroball's continental spread continues through the 1920s–50s.

Three Separate Structures · First Contact, Not Unification

Rugby

Each of rugby's three traditions develops its own regional structure during this decade: an intercollegiate conference for the elite-institutional tradition (folding into the broader collegiate track below), a regional league for the Appalachian mining-town tradition, and a regional competition for the Californian tradition.

The decade's notable rugby event: a touring Commonwealth side (Australia/New Zealand/South Africa/Fiji) visits North America and plays matches against both a Californian club and an institutional or Appalachian side. The three traditions still don't engage each other directly — but for the first time, they're measured against the same outside standard. Nothing is resolved yet, but the seed is planted for the unification pressure that will eventually arrive.

The Collegiate Track

Alongside these professional/regional developments, a general intercollegiate athletic body — name TBD, working placeholder "Intercollegiate Athletic Association" — forms during this decade, organizing college-level competition across baseball, football, and other sports. Unlike OTL's NCAA precursor, this body doesn't emerge from any violence-and-reform crisis — it's simply the natural organizational counterpart to the professional leagues developing in parallel. Rugby's elite-institutional tradition is already inherently collegiate, so its conference effectively becomes part of this broader body from the start.

The Big Sports in the Boom

Where the previous era left each Big Sport at a different point on the regional-to-national pipeline, the Boom is when several of them complete (or substantially advance along) that journey — driven by the radio boom of the mid-1920s, by a decade of uninterrupted growth, and in NAFA's case, by sheer time.

Founded ~1918–20 · Growing Through the Decade

NAFA — From Two Members to Four

Football's parallel US regional leagues coordinate with Canada's into NAFA around 1918–1920. Roughly a decade later, in the late 1920s, Texas and California join — the "initial big members" complete. Mexico's membership remains an open thread for a future era.

Mid-1920s · Radio Boom

Stickball Goes National: ANSA

The radio boom of the mid-1920s carries the Mississippi Valley stickball league beyond its founding footprint and into the All-Nation Stickball Association — the first time stickball is genuinely national (still US-only), not merely "nationally known."

Growing Footprint · Still Regional

Aroball — Beyond the Borderlands

The Southwest/Borderlands league matures within its Texas–California–northern Mexico core, while Aroball begins appearing as an early foothold in US states bordering its two host nations: Louisiana and Jefferson (adjoining Texas) and Oregon and Brandt (adjoining California). These remain early footholds rather than league members for now — the seeds of MLA's eventual continental form in the 1940s–50s.

Riding the Wave

Baseball/NBA — Radio-Driven Growth

Baseball rides the same radio boom as everyone else — increased popularity, increased revenue, and likely some expansion of the NBA during the decade. Nothing especially divergent here; baseball simply benefits from the era's general prosperity and new media the way every sport does.

Three Traditions Become Two

Rugby

The Boom is when rugby's three traditions narrow to two distinct scenes. The Appalachian tradition grows from a regional mining-town circuit into a genuine US professional rugby scene. Simultaneously, the elite-institutional tradition spreads beyond its original handful of schools into a broader US collegiate rugby scene — possibly aided by Coastal South schools entering the wider collegiate athletic world as Morrison v. South Carolina (1920s) ends segregation as previously implemented.

As both US-based tracks grow in parallel, pressure builds to reconcile their rules — and by the early 1930s, US professional and collegiate rugby rules consolidate. Meanwhile, the Californian tradition grows into its own separate professional scene, on its own track. By 1932, there are two rugby scenes — American (consolidated, pro and college) and Californian — rather than three. Full continental unification remains a question for later eras.

Section Five
The Global War (1932–1940)
The America Games & Olympics — An Eight-Year Gap
1932
The Global War begins in Europe and Asia. Canada, through its British imperial ties, is effectively at war from this point.
1933
The Summer America Games is held — the last edition before the war reaches American shores, since the United States itself is not yet at war. The athletes who earn places on the team have, in effect, nowhere to go: word that Toronto 1934 is cancelled arrives during or shortly after the Games. The Winter America Games is also held, but 1934 Toronto Winter is similarly cancelled.

1937
Both the Summer and Winter America Games lapse entirely. The US is deep in the Pacific theater with neither the capacity nor the appetite for a celebratory trials season.
1938
1938 Madrid Olympics — CANCELLED (Spain at war, world at war). 1938 Madrid Winter Olympics — CANCELLED.

Shooting stops late in the year.
1941
The America Games returns — and it's a celebration. Eight years of pent-up demand produce record turnout and interest: the first real taste of normalcy.
1942
1942 San Francisco Olympics — the first Games in 12 years, postwar celebration energy throughout. Hawaii potentially participates as a North American associate member for the first time.
The Big Sports on the Home Front

Across all five Big Sports, the pattern is reduced, not suspended. Rosters thin as players are drafted, seasons may shorten, but every league continues to operate throughout 1932–1940 — sport as a home-front morale institution, continuing the tradition of presidential engagement with American athletics that Roosevelt established decades earlier.

NAFA faces the most complex situation of any league: with the USA, Canada, Texas, and California — all four members — mobilized by 1934, continent-wide competition continues in reduced form across nations actively at war. Mexico, providing economic support and logistics rather than direct mobilization, likely operates closer to a normal football season throughout, becoming something of an anchor for NAFA's continuity during these years. That anchor role becomes formal: Mexico joins NAFA before the war's end, completing a five-nation league just as the postwar era begins.

NBA, ANSA, Aroball's regional league, and rugby's American and Californian scenes all follow the same broad pattern — diminished but unbroken, with specific wartime rosters and standings left open for a later pass.

Women's Professional Sports — A Wartime Institution That Lasts

As men are drafted in unprecedented numbers, women's professional leagues emerge to fill rosters and maintain play — baseball, riding the NBA's prominence, becomes the flagship. From its first season, the league is integrated, consistent with the broader no-Jim-Crow framework — there is no OTL equivalent of the AAGPBL's whites-only restriction to begin with.

Unlike OTL's AAGPBL, which folded in 1954 once the war's social conditions receded, WBT's women's professional league does not fold after the war. Given the trajectory already underway — suffrage since 1900, women's increasing presence across public and professional life — the league persists as a genuinely lasting institution rather than a wartime curiosity. Specific structure, name, and postwar evolution: open for a future pass.

Aroball's Wartime Diffusion

The war becomes the mechanism behind Aroball's already-charted postwar continental spread. As American, Canadian, Texian, and Californian forces serve and fight alongside one another throughout the Global War, soldiers for whom Aroball is already a hometown sport introduce it to soldiers from across the rest of the continent. This cross-regional wartime exposure — rather than spreading for no particular reason — is the seed from which Aroball's 1940s–50s continental boom grows.

Section Six
Postwar Expansion (1941–1947)
A Celebration Already Underway

This era opens mid-celebration: the 1941 America Games and the 1942 San Francisco Olympics are one continuous moment of postwar relief, the first real return to normalcy after eight Olympic-less years. The host nation is the Union of the Californias, not the USA — a reminder of how this hemisphere's sporting institutions span genuinely separate countries. California's own delegation surges as host, but that's California's story to tell. For the USA specifically, 1942 is a postwar-celebration jump rather than a host-boosted one: roughly 95–105 events (a global figure, not US-specific), an American team of roughly 290–320 athletes, and roughly 50–58 medals — reflecting both the natural growth curve and the sheer pent-up demand of the gap. Hawaii's participation as a North American associate member for the first time adds to the sense of a wider, more connected hemisphere finally at peace. • The 1942 Lake Tahoe Winter Olympics — also hosted by California — runs alongside, the first Winter Games in eight years.

Into this exact moment, NAFA launches its championship.

The American Cup

NAFA's five members — the USA, Canada, Texas, California, and now Mexico, whose wartime anchor role (Section Five) became formal membership before the war's end — have by now coalesced into the continent-spanning league that postwar institution-building (the Pan-American Passport, the Global Peace Council, all dated to 1940) reflects in miniature. The American Cup launches in 1941–42 as the championship of this five-nation league: a genuinely international event in the sense that its competitors are separate sovereign nations, not just regions of one country. The "American" in the name reads hemispherically — the Americas — fitting a trophy contested between nations rather than states.

Continuing the tradition of presidential engagement with American sport that Roosevelt established decades earlier, President Freeman's attendance at early American Cup matches becomes part of the new championship's origin story — a postwar president at a postwar event.

Established Traditions, Named at Last

This era is a natural point to give the two oldest Big Sport championships their names and acknowledge the history they already carry. The Diamond Classic — baseball's championship, dating to the NBA's consolidation in the Olympic Era — has roughly two decades of tradition behind it by 1941, the oldest and most established championship of any Big Sport. The All-Nation Championship — stickball's, dating to ANSA's formation during the radio boom — has roughly fifteen years of history. Both are now genuinely traditional institutions rather than recent inventions, even though this is the first point at which this bible has named them.

Aroball's Continental Boom — MLA Forms

This is Aroball's defining era. The wartime diffusion seeded in Section Five — American and Canadian soldiers serving alongside Texians and Californians, for whom Aroball was already a hometown sport — comes home with the veterans. Postwar interest in Aroball spreads well beyond the Southwest/Borderlands core and the scattered prewar footholds in Louisiana, Jefferson, Oregon, and Brandt.

In the mid-1940s, the Southwest/Borderlands league and this new continental demand coalesce into MLA (Major League Aroball) — the continental major league envisioned since the sport's invention in 1900. Following the pattern set by the Diamond Classic and All-Nation Championship (both of which took roughly 15–20 years after their leagues formed to develop marquee championship traditions), MLA's own championship is a story for a later era — the league itself forming now is the headline.

Rugby's Two Scenes, Quietly Established

Both of rugby's two scenes have their own championships by this point: the American Rugby Championship, within the consolidated US pro/college scene, and the Golden Cup, within California's homegrown professional scene. Neither carries the cultural weight of the Big Sports' championships, but both are established, ongoing institutions — rugby continuing on its own track, continental unification still a question for later.

The Global Cup — 1952

The World Football Association (WFA), founded in 1940 in the immediate postwar moment, spends its first decade establishing governance infrastructure and negotiating with the WOC over the Olympic football tournament's status. That negotiation resolves cleanly: the WOC agrees to open Olympic football to professional athletes; the WFA agrees that the Olympic tournament IS the de facto world national-team championship. No separate "World Cup" is needed or established.

What the WFA establishes independently is the Global Cup — a quadrennial tournament for club teams, not national teams, drawing the best franchise sides from NAFA, European leagues, South American leagues, Asian leagues, and beyond. The first Global Cup is held in London, 1952, with 32 club teams from regional leagues worldwide. NAFA sends 6–8 teams, including the reigning American Cup champion as automatic qualifier. Europe sends the largest allocation (~10–12), with South America, Asia, Africa, and Oceania filling the remainder proportionately.

The Global Cup cycle — every four years from 1952 — eventually syncs with Winter Olympics years, giving the sporting calendar a major football event (Global Cup or Olympic tournament) every two years in alternation. NAFA's American Cup takes on an extra layer of stakes: winning it is the surest path to Global Cup qualification.

NAFA at Five, Quietly

With Mexico's membership formalized at the close of the Global War (Sections Five and Six), NAFA enters this era already complete as a five-nation league. The American Cup, barely a few editions old as this era begins, continues as a five-nation championship throughout — one of the few continental institutions that simply isn't a live political question during Nolan/Priest. It's already settled, and settled things don't make headlines.

Aroball's Quiet, Steady Growth

As this era begins, Aroball is still mostly not an American sport in practice — MLA's core remains Texas, California, and Mexico, with perhaps only one or two USA-chartered teams (likely drawn from the border-state footholds of the Boom era: Louisiana, Jefferson, Oregon, Brandt) having joined MLA prior to 1947. Over the following twelve years, that USA presence grows — gradually, not explosively — as more of those border-state footholds mature into full MLA franchises.

This growth happens largely under the radar of the era's politics. Aroball isn't a question of race, gender, or sexuality — the three axes Nolan/Priest-era conservatism actually cares about — so its slow continental spread simply continues, neither helped nor hindered by the broader political climate. By the end of this era, the USA represents a growing but still minority share of MLA's membership, with further growth still ahead.

The Women's League — Barely

This is the era's real domestic sports story. The women's professional baseball league, established during the Global War and never intended as a wartime curiosity, faces its sharpest test yet under Nolan/Priest's social conservatism — real pressure on funding, attendance, and the league's basic legitimacy in a political climate that, per the era's character, is concerned above all with gender and sexuality. The league survives — but barely, and not comfortably. Specific details of how it survives (key franchises, figures, turning points) are left for a future pass, but the headline is real: this is the closest the institution comes to not making it.

Race — A Non-Story

In sharp contrast to the cinema industry's experience of this same era (where Black creative participation narrows under Nolan/Priest), sports' integration — 70 to 90 years old by this point, depending on the sport — proves resilient. The era's conservatism is fundamentally about gender and sexuality, not race, and sports' much longer integration history gives it little purchase here. Nothing to report.

For the first time, there's serious talk of the American and Californian scenes eventually coming together — not yet accomplished, just genuine discussion. After three traditions narrowed to two scenes by 1932, and those two scenes each achieved internal standardization by now, a single continental rugby body is, for the first time, a live conversation rather than a hypothetical.

The Choson War — A Light Touch on Sport

Unlike the Global War, the Choson War (1966–67) does not significantly disrupt sports. It is short, geographically distant, and does not trigger the kind of continent-wide mobilization that halved rosters and shuttered leagues in 1934–40. Some athletes serve; some seasons feel slightly thinner; the diplomatic accountability debates that follow the CDC's bombing decision ripple quietly through the sporting press, particularly in NAFA countries where collective military commitment has become a contested political question. But the leagues run, the championships are contested, and by 1968 the war's direct shadow on sport has largely lifted.

The outcome is modest — a minor concession, a quiet precedent — but the precedent is real. In later eras, these unions will be much harder to ignore.

The conversation that began under Lincoln becomes action under Langston. The most likely milestone: the first real collective bargaining agreement in one of the Big Sports — again probably the NBA, still the most organizationally mature league. The seed planted under Lincoln has become something that can't be easily ignored.

What the 1970s add is simply more US teams, more US cities with genuine Aroball fanbases, and a sport that feels continental in practice rather than just in theory.

Dale Mercer & James Colby — 1979

The most significant sports story of this era — and arguably of the entire post-war period — begins not with a press conference or a political gesture, but with a pitcher who wants to acknowledge the man he loves.

Dale Mercer, a starting pitcher for the Calumet NBA franchise, comes out publicly in 1979. Mercer is midwestern in every sense — raised on a farm, unpretentious, the kind of player whose career had been defined by quiet consistency rather than headline-making. His partner, James Colby, works in the franchise's own corporate and public relations office — already quietly out in professional circles, "straight-passing" at first glance to the broader public, known in certain team contexts without it ever having become a story. The relationship isn't a surprise to everyone. It becomes a story because Mercer decides he wants to stop pretending it doesn't exist.

The reception is exactly as complicated as the moment deserves. Within the Calumet franchise, some teammates are vocally and immediately supportive; others are not, and the internal friction is real before it gets smoothed over by public solidarity. The players' association — now a mature institution with real power, its labor identity forged under Lincoln and Langston — backs Mercer publicly and without equivocation, whatever its internal debates. Road games in more conservative markets bring crowd reactions that range from cold to hostile; certain franchises in the league's southern and border circuits become particularly fraught venues. In Calumet itself, the reception is largely warm — a union city, a northern industrial city, a city whose sports culture has been shaped by exactly the kind of working-class solidarity the players' association represents.

Mercer never becomes a spokesperson by choice. He is simply a pitcher who came out because he wanted to marry the man he loved — and who, by doing so, became the first openly gay active professional athlete in any of the Big Sports. What follows his career, what happens to James Colby, and what their lives look like after marriage equality passes under Pike six years later: all open for future development.

The Early Interlink — Sports Goes Online, Slowly

The Interlink's global integration completing in the early 2000s doesn't mean streaming or instant global fandom — capability-wise, this is roughly equivalent to OTL's mid-to-late 1990s web. What it does mean is that the sports section of the morning paper starts getting competition. Fans begin checking Diamond Classic standings, NAFA tables, and ANSA results on Interlink nodes rather than waiting for the next day's print edition. Sports results pages are among the earliest and most popular Interlink destinations — scores and standings translate naturally to text-based digital formats in a way that match footage does not.

Nobody is streaming the 1998 Barcelona opening ceremony. But a growing number of people are reading about it the same evening rather than the next morning. The shift is generational and gradual — sports media as an industry is beginning to feel the ground move, without yet knowing quite where it's going.

Women's League — Mature and Established

The women's professional baseball league that barely survived Nolan/Priest and recovered under Lincoln is, by 1989, simply a fact of American sporting life. Real stars, real attendance, real media coverage — still secondary to the NBA in profile, but meaningfully less secondary than OTL's equivalent. No major new beat here; the story of this era is simply continued, uncontested institutional maturity. The survival question is three decades in the past.

Global Cup — Growing Prestige

The Global Cup, now four decades old and into its tenth and eleventh editions during this era, is firmly established as the premier club football competition in the world. NAFA's American Cup winner going to London or São Paulo or Tokyo to represent North America gives the domestic club season genuine global stakes — a dimension OTL's NFL or MLB never achieved. The Global Cup's prestige reinforces NAFA's own standing: winning the American Cup matters not just continentally but worldwide.

Union Cup — Established Continental Prestige

The Union Cup, now roughly two decades old and fully settled as a continental rugby championship, builds genuine prestige and tradition through this era. No major structural changes — just an institution becoming more itself, with established rivalries, celebrated matches, and a growing place in the continental sporting calendar alongside the American Cup and Diamond Classic.

The Interlink Broadcasting Revolution

This is the era's defining structural story for sport. By the mid-2000s the Interlink has matured enough that live sports streaming becomes realistic — not perfect, not universal, but genuinely real. The shift that began in the late 1990s (sports results replacing the morning paper) accelerates into something much larger: dedicated sports broadcasting nodes, live match coverage, highlight packages available within minutes of a final whistle.

Telecinema sports coverage — which has dominated sports broadcasting since the postwar era — faces serious competition from Interlink streaming for the first time. TC networks respond by doubling down on production values and live event coverage, but the audience is fragmenting in ways that can't be reversed. A fan in Tokyo can follow a NAFA match in real time. A viewer in Gigadohi can watch an ANSA championship without waiting for the TC broadcast. The Global Cup, which had always been a genuinely global competition, becomes a genuinely global viewing event for the first time during this era — 2008 and 2012 are the first editions where international audiences outside the host nation follow along in real time at scale.

Sports media as an industry transforms accordingly: dedicated Interlink sports nodes, fantasy league platforms, real-time statistics, fan communities forming across national and continental boundaries. The business model of sports — broadcast rights, advertising, ticket revenues — begins its long renegotiation.

The 2009 NAFA Work Stoppage

The first major work stoppage in any of the Big Sports arrives in 2009, in NAFA — and its five-nation structure makes it unlike any labor dispute in purely domestic leagues. The NAFA players' association, representing players from the USA, Canada, Texas, California, and Mexico, must coordinate solidarity across five separate labor law frameworks, five governments with different union traditions, and five fan bases with different expectations of what a work stoppage means. Texan labor law is not American labor law; Mexican union tradition is not Canadian union tradition.

The stoppage — length and specific trigger (revenue sharing, free agency rules, salary caps) left open for a future pass — costs at least part of a season. The cross-national solidarity holds, imperfectly but substantially: players from all five nations honor the stoppage, though the legal mechanisms by which they do so vary by country. The NAU's open-borders framework, deepening through this era, provides some of the connective tissue that makes continental labor action possible in ways it couldn't have been even twenty years earlier. Resolution eventually comes; the CBA that follows reshapes NAFA's economics for the next decade.

MLA — Genuinely Continental

By 2013, Aroball has completed its long journey from a Texas schoolyard invention in 1900 to a genuinely continental sport. MLA's US presence has grown enough that American cities feel like real Aroball cities rather than outposts of a foreign sport — though Texas, California, and Mexico remain the cultural heart, and US teams remain a minority of MLA franchises. The Interlink era helps: Aroball's visually distinctive court (sloped walls, side-mounted rings) translates exceptionally well to streaming video, giving the sport a natural hook for new audiences discovering it online.

2022 Shanghai — China's Olympic Moment

Three years after the Chinese Spring's resolution, Shanghai hosts the Summer Olympics. The timing is not accidental — China hosting was awarded before the coup attempt, but the coup's failure and the constitutional reforms that followed give the Games an entirely different meaning than they might otherwise have had. This is a newly democratizing China — free press, competitive elections, genuine civil society emerging — with the Zhongxing Emperor as a beloved constitutional figurehead who survived an attempt to depose him, putting its best foot forward before the world.

The atmosphere is celebratory in a way that carries genuine political weight: China-NAU relations are warmer than at any point since before the Global War, and the Olympic opening ceremony in Shanghai is watched globally with an attention that goes beyond sports. For the WOC, which has spent a century building exactly the kind of cooperative international institution that the Chinese Spring's resolution represents, the 2022 Games feel like a vindication.

Clearwater & Stickball — Ambient Cultural Weight

Naomi Clearwater of Lakota — the first president from a Native state — brings no direct sports policy to the ANSA or any other league. But the ambient cultural weight is real: a Lakota president attending an All-Nation Championship final is not a routine presidential appearance. It's a closing of a circle that began when stickball spread from Gigadohi to Kanasaw to Lakota in the 1870s and 1880s, through a league that named itself for exactly the nations the president comes from. Clearwater makes at least one such appearance during her term; the moment is noted without being made into a policy statement.

Rugby — Talk of a Global Body

The Union Cup, now over forty years old, is a fully mature and prestigious continental championship. During the Hudson and Clearwater years, serious conversation begins about a WFA-parallel global rugby body — an international governing organization that could organize something equivalent to the Global Cup for rugby club teams, and coordinate the existing continental rugby bodies (the Union Cup's governing structure, European equivalents, Southern Hemisphere bodies). Nothing is founded yet; this is the "talk" stage, paralleling where football governance was in the late 1930s before the WFA formed in 1940. The founding, when it comes, belongs to a future era.

Women's League — Approaching Parity

The women's professional baseball league that barely survived Nolan/Priest is, by 2025, closer to par with the men's NBA than at any prior point — real stars with genuine national profiles, real broadcast deals, real attendance, real salary history. Not quite equal billing; the gap that existed in 1989 has narrowed significantly but not closed. The trajectory is clear enough that "closing" feels like a matter of time rather than a question.

The Present-Day Landscape (2026)

As of 2026 — the Sesquicentennial year, the Philadelphia Olympics, the NAU Constitution ratification process — WBT's sports landscape is fully mature. Five Big Sports, each with its own championship tradition, its own broadcasting infrastructure, its own labor history. A global football ecosystem (WFA, Global Cup, NAFA, American Cup) that is genuinely novel relative to OTL. A continental rugby body (Union Cup, WRC) approaching its own global competition. A women's professional baseball league that survived everything and is now simply part of the furniture. And Aroball — invented in a Texas schoolyard in 1900, continental by the 1950s, globally known by the 2020s — still with its cultural heart in the borderlands where it was born.

The franchise pass that follows establishes the current teams, cities, and competitive structure of each league as of the 2026 present day.
