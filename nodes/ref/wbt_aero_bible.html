Governing principles WBT aviation diverges from OTL not by being uniformly behind, but by being differently shaped. The absence of a 1910s general war removes the single greatest accelerant in OTL aviation history, and the presence of a large, helium-fed lighter-than-air industry gives the early twentieth century a long-haul air transport option that OTL never had at scale. The result is a civil aviation sector that is slower, smaller, later-forming and more institutionally regulated than OTL's — and simultaneously world-leading in airways infrastructure, safety certification, meteorology, large lightweight structures and long-endurance engines. Nothing in this document requires airships to be better than they were. It requires airplanes to be worse for longer.
Framework
The Heavier-Than-Air Lag
1901–1970

The point of origin is fixed. Hard Canon

On June 14th, 1901, two machinists from the small community of Red Fork — Oscar Vaughn and Levi Harcourt — fitted a lightweight kerosene motor to a canvas-and-wood biplane glider and coaxed it into a brief, steady flight of several hundred feet… the state of Jefferson proudly claims the moniker "First in Flight."
Six causes of the lag

The lag is deliberately over-determined so that no single mechanism carries suspicious weight. These divide into capability causes, which slow the technology itself, and market causes, which shrink and reshape the industry without making the machines worse.

  1. No 1910s forcing function. (capability) The Great European War ends in 1904; the Matanzas War in 1906; the Global War does not begin until 1932. That is roughly twenty-six years of peace covering exactly the window in which OTL's WWI drove aero engines from ~80hp to ~400hp in four years. This cause alone accounts for most of the deficit.
  2. No 1919 surplus dump. (capability) No cheap war-surplus airframes or engines, no demobilised pilot pool, no trained mechanic generation, and therefore no barnstorming culture — see below.
  3. No mature mass-market gasoline engine industry. (capability) WBT's automobile market never consolidated on internal combustion; steam, gasoline and electric hold durable separate niches. American aviation cannot draw on an OTL-scale gasoline engine sector in the 1900s–1920s because one does not exist.
  4. The V&H patent thicket, ~1906–1932. (capability) See §United States.
  5. USRC owns short and medium haul. (market) An excellent, publicly beloved, politically unassailable state railway with passenger priority. Domestic aviation's natural market is occupied from the start.
  6. USP&T flies its own mail. (market) In OTL, federal airmail contracts to private carriers were the seed capital of the entire American airline industry. In WBT that seed is never planted.

Minor, seventh: gasoline distribution is regional, expensive and strategically exposed until NAOTA (1930), while kerosene — the airship fuel — has a nationwide retail network from the lamp-oil era. A real advantage, but second-order: ground handling, not fuel, is the binding constraint on airship networks.

Counter-pressureWBT's airship industry is much larger than OTL's and employs aerodynamicists, structural engineers, metallurgists and meteorologists at scale from the 1900s. A meaningful fraction of that knowledge transfers to large aircraft. The net figures below already account for this spillover.

The lag never closes and never widens, stabilising at five to seven years. Two splits govern it:

  • Military aviation catches up fully by ~1950. Fission weapons (1951–52), the China rivalry and the Choson War (1966–68) supply as much pressure as OTL. No military lag survives contact with a nuclear rival.
  • Civil aviation carries the lag. Cabin pressurisation is the load-bearing item: worked on but not solved in service during the Global War, reaching military high-altitude aircraft ~1938–40 and not becoming a usable civil product until the late 1940s at the earliest. An unpressurised airliner cannot climb over weather, so 1940s WBT air travel is low, bumpy, frequently cancelled and genuinely unpleasant.

Cultural consequence: gliding, not barnstorming

With no surplus dump, OTL's barnstorming culture has no fuel supply and does not occur. Its replacement arises from the same constraint that killed it: V&H's patents make powered flight expensive and legally hazardous, while gliders are cheap, buildable in a barn, and commercially uninteresting to litigate over. Once the core patents lapse around 1922, gliding is entirely free while powered flight remains costly.

American amateur aviation in the 1920s is therefore a soaring movement — clubs, ridge sites, duration and altitude records, a homebuilt tradition. Bluffs and ridgelines instead of cow pastures; quieter, more middle-class, more club-based, less carnival. It produces the pilot pool that would otherwise be missing in 1932, and it adds an edge to V&H's legacy: the firm that made flight expensive is the reason a generation learned to fly without engines.

1920s American commercial aviation is correspondingly institutional, professional and elite — manufacturer teams and federal pilots, not stunt fliers at county fairs. Air racing exists as a corporate showcase.

Framework
The Fuel Split

WBT aviation divides permanently by fuel, on physical grounds.

Heavier-than-air

Gasoline

Spark-ignition piston engines need octane. Kerosene vaporises poorly in a carburettor, forces low compression and caps power. The Prairie Hawk runs kerosene in 1901 because that is what every general store sold — but the moment anyone wants real horsepower, gasoline is the only road.

Lighter-than-air

Kerosene / diesel

Airships are weight-indifferent and need multi-day endurance and fuel economy; volatile fuel beside a gas envelope is a poor idea. Diesel is the correct airship fuel and WBT's industry funds its development for decades.

NAOTA (1930) is the hinge. Texian crude floods in, gasoline becomes cheap and widely distributed, and the airplane's fuel-logistics disadvantage collapses — two years before the Global War forces the issue. The kerosene lineage never dies, because the airship industry keeps kerosene infrastructure commercially important right through the era when OTL America consolidated on gasoline.

Open Canvas — TurbinesWith the automotive sector explicitly not entering turbine development, WBT's turbine metallurgy descends from naval steam turbines and aero-engine hot-section work, as OTL. Turbines therefore proceed roughly on schedule, but civil adoption is turboprop-first and jets arrive military-first, late and grudgingly. Detailed timeline deferred to a postwar session.
Lighter-Than-Air
Three Centers, Three Philosophies

Rigid airship development is genuinely parallel, but the three centers differ by function and constraint rather than by simultaneous identical invention. Ferdinand von Zeppelin is not transplanted to Texas; the older draft's version of that origin is retired.

Prussia — the engineering rival

Hydrogen

Military-origin, descended directly from Great European War balloon experience. High performance, structurally aggressive, better lift-to-size, worse safety. Prestige-driven. The UER inherits and continues a parallel line after 1904.

Prussia has no helium and never will.

Texas Federation — the builder

Helium

Helium-first from the start because that is what lies under the Panhandle. Ships are bigger and more conservative for the same payload. German-Texan machine shops are the industrial substrate — canon since the 1830s, reinforced by the 1841 refugee wave. Strong semi-rigid and non-rigid tradition alongside the rigids.

United States — buyer & regulator

Not an independent inventor

The US buys (chiefly through the Navy), certifies, and provides the continental market. Texian firms build American subsidiaries inside the tariff wall on the Oststein precedent. Texas builds them; America regulates and flies them.

Permanent limits — do not soften Speed ceiling ~80–100 mph, forever. Weather-limited permanently: routes and seasons, not schedules; radar and modern forecasting make them safer, not more punctual. Ground handling never becomes casual — masts, sheds, 100–200 crew — which alone bars airships from point-to-point service and caps North America at roughly 6–12 terminals ever. Short and medium haul are lost immediately and permanently. Payload fraction is poor: airships beat roads that do not exist, carrying outsize loads to places without runways. They never compete with ships on bulk or rail on anything.
Helium does not make airships safe It prevents one failure mode out of several. Large rigids break up in weather because they are enormous, lightly built, and cannot outrun or outclimb a storm. WBT's airship industry carries a real and continuous accident record — a major loss every few years across the 1920s–50s, with fatalities. It is a premium mode with a genuinely bad safety record, accepted because the alternative is six days at sea.

They are the beneficiary of the HTA lag, not a cause of it — an industry of this size cannot suppress a continent's demand for pressurised airliners, and is not asked to.

Lighter-Than-Air
Helium Politics & the Prussian Disaster
1900–1931
Geology The Hugoton–Panhandle field

The world's only significant helium source, shared between the United States (Jefferson and Kansas) and the Texas Federation, and weighted toward Texas. The Oklahoma Panhandle is Texian territory in this timeline. Neither nation can exploit the field alone, which manufactures a decades-long technical and regulatory partnership out of pure geology and gives the North American Conference framework a material rationale that predates its trade logic.

1900s–20s Export open — Prussia buys North American helium

Transatlantic shipment in cylinders is brutally expensive, so Prussia can afford it only for flagship passenger and state airships. Prussian aviation is consequently split: prestige ships on imported helium, military and second-line ships still on hydrogen.

1930 Restriction under NAOTA

Helium export is curtailed as part of the North American Open Trade Agreement, in reaction to escalating rhetoric from Berlin. The counterparty on the Texian side is the Texian Rare Gas Consortium. Prussia's best ships lose their gas.

Disaster
1931
The Prussian airship disaster — A prestige demonstration flight during the height of Nationalist consolidation, in the same year Prussia formally demands referenda in UER German territories and begins its military buildup. The ship was designed for helium and completed as a hydrogen ship because the gas never arrived after the 1930 curtailment. It burns. Lost aboard: a senior Nationalist party figure — the airship programme's political champion — and a minor royal.

Consequences. The Prussian LTA programme collapses; with its patron dead nobody will defend it, and Prussia enters the Global War in 1932 with no credible lighter-than-air arm while North America has a mature helium airship capability. Prussian U-boats will face an airborne threat they cannot answer in kind.

And the moral complication. Prussian propaganda blames North America for the deaths, and is not entirely wrong. The embargo is the proximate cause of the ship flying hydrogen. This is deliberate: the pre-war buildup should carry genuine culpability rather than clean lines.

Lighter-Than-Air
The Texian Airship Industry
1889 onward
Geography
  • Panhandle — helium extraction, purification and storage. Roughly 600 miles from the yards; the cylinder rail link is the industry's critical strategic vulnerability in wartime.
  • New Frankfurt — construction, sheds, operations, headquarters. A major city in the state of Austin, centred somewhat inland rather than on Trinity Bay. There is no Houston in this timeline. The name carries the German-Texan settlement history that produced the industry, and echoes the 1839 Frankfurt republic whose collapse drove the 1841 refugee wave.
  • Texopolis — political operation and government relations.
The 1900 hurricane — founding trauma

A Galveston-analogue storm strikes with the firms already in existence and their sheds on the bayside, southeast of the city. The sheds are destroyed. The industry rebuilds north of New Frankfurt, away from the worst of any landfall — a move that is a scar rather than a plan, and from which everything afterward descends.

  • Hurricane season is a permanent operating constraint. Ships are walked or flown inland on warning. Gulf and Caribbean service becomes effectively a winter-and-spring product; insurance and scheduling are built around the season.
  • Texian meteorology becomes world-leading, privately funded from the 1900s by an industry whose survival depends on forecasting hurricanes days out — decades before OTL states took forecasting seriously. Storm warning is a public good, so the institution likely becomes quasi-public, and is a natural candidate for early NAU-era continental science.
The firms
1889 Weiss Machine Works / Weiss Maschinenwerke Gustav Weiss

Founded in southeast New Frankfurt near Trinity Bay. Dual-named in German and English, as was common in pre-twentieth-century Texas. German-Texan machine shop descent; the prestige house.

Renamed Weiss Aeronautical Engineering (WAE) in 1902 following the post-hurricane relocation to the north side of the city — the company's name changes because its address did, and both changed because of the storm.

1903 Vanguard Aeronautics Raymond Patterson

Patterson — an Anglo-Texian engineer inside a German-Texan machine-shop culture, and therefore structurally an outsider — had warned about the bayside siting and been ignored. The storm vindicated him. He was then ignored again on the rebuild. He departs, raises outside capital, and founds a rival firm in New Frankfurt.

The two firms compete for both rigid and non-rigid contracts, permanently and head to head. The rivalry has a grievance at its root and the ethnic, generational and commercial fault lines all run the same direction.

~1917–25 Texian Rare Gas Consortium

A regulated cartel under Texian charter, Panhandle-based, formed once helium's strategic value became obvious. Not a shipbuilder — deliberately separated from the yards so that no single entity both sells the gas and builds the ships. The counterparty for all helium export policy from the 1920s onward, and unaffected by the later collapse of passenger service.

The operators
WAE subsidiary · 1902–04

Texian Airship Service (TAS)

Founded as Trinity Airship Service, renamed 1904. Holds a bundle of small standing government contracts: trunk and ocean mail, coastal and maritime patrol, aerial survey and mapping, storm reconnaissance, frontier patrol in Nuevo Mexico and Alta Chihuahua, and official transport.

No single subsidy exists — nobody in Texopolis ever votes for one, which suits a laissez-faire federation. But TAS enters every season with revenue already booked. It is a pseudo-subsidy nobody has to defend, and an incumbency accident that hardened into structure.

Vanguard-backed · 1906

Trinity-Gulf Airline → TGA

Founded by Vanguard's financial backers. Fully private, no cushion, mixed rigid and non-rigid passenger service. Competes on price and service because it cannot compete on politics — which sharpens the engineering rivalry rather than turning it into a lobbying war.

By the 1950s the name has contracted to TGA and the letters stop standing for anything.

Support model

Rigids are lumpy business, with years between hulls. The industry is carried by US Navy procurement — foreign money, deep pockets — combined with internal cross-subsidy from non-rigid volume. Texas's most glamorous industry is therefore structurally dependent on decisions made in Franklin, and quietly hostage to any US–Texas friction.

The industry's labour history — Austin abolished slavery in the 1870s, within a few years of the War Between the States, so the workforce of the 1889–1900 founding era is a full generation past emancipation; no OTL Southern industrial pattern should be imported.
United States
Firms & Federal Structure
V&H Aeronautics

Vaughn and Harcourt's partnership holds. Red Fork Machine Works becomes V&H Aeronautics by ordinary commercial contraction. Canon establishes them as machinists supplying engines and parts, so the firm's natural descent is as an engine house that broadens into airframes later — engines being the highest barrier to entry in aviation and the reason a 1901 machine shop is still a major firm in 2026.

They are also, deliberately, the patent aggressors. They hold the foundational claims, litigate ferociously, and layer improvement patents as core claims lapse. They get rich. They stunt American heavier-than-air growth for a generation.

PeriodState of the thicket
~1903–08Core claims granted. Enforcement begins.
~1906–22The hard years. Maximum suppression — anyone building airframes in America is paying V&H or in court with them. This is the window that must be starved to put WBT at OTL-mid-1920s levels in 1932.
~1922–30Core patents lapse; V&H sustain the fight on improvement claims. Grip weakening, litigiousness undiminished. By 1925 they are widely understood to be litigating on fumes and doing it anyway. The 1920s boom begins.
1932The Preparation Act imposes a war-production patent pool — less a liberation than a coup de grâce on a fading position.

Because patents are federal, licensees cannot escape by relocating. The industry that emerges is small and concentrated — a handful of licensed firms rather than OTL's hundreds of garage builders. V&H's legacy is permanently complicated, and "Jefferson: First in Flight" acquires a rueful edge: there is a live historiographical argument in 2026 about whether the men who invented flight also held it back a generation.

Federal structure
~1922–26 onward

USP&T Air Service

The Post & Telegraph operates federal air mail with its own aircraft plus contracted private work, and maintains a uniformed federal pilot corps. Later than OTL's 1918 start because there is no war-surplus aircraft and no military pilot pool.

By 1932 this corps is the largest pool of trained American aviators in the country — a major mobilisation asset. WBT's wartime air arm is built substantially out of postal pilots.

Late 1920s

Federal Airways System (FAS)

Created by USP&T. Owns the ground: airfields, lighted beacons, emergency fields, weather reporting, navigation. Private carriers license access. Substantially cheaper to build than its OTL counterpart thanks to the Rural Electrification Act of 1910.

1929–31 · King administration

Bureau of Air Commerce

The capstone regulator, arriving as the culmination of thirty years of federal infrastructure-building rather than as a bolt from the blue. Built on the Boiler Safety and Certification Act of 1919 model: federal minimum airworthiness standards plus reciprocal recognition of state certification — not a federal inspectorate.

Naming note: replaces the working title "Federal Aviation Bureau," which collided with Fleak's Federal Arbitration Bureau (~1900).

Explicitly not

USRC has no air arm

The state railway does not enter aviation. Its passenger priority and political untouchability instead squeeze domestic aviation out of short and medium haul entirely.

International airworthiness is handled by bilateral treaty between the United States and Texas, not by the Bureau, which is a domestic body. A continent-wide air commerce regulator arrives later — either through NAOTA machinery or, more likely, with the postwar establishment of the NAU.

Rail–air intermodal

Coordinated between USRC, USP&T and FAS. Fly by day, rail by night. In OTL this model lasted about two years because aircraft improved fast enough to kill it; in WBT aircraft do not, and it becomes the way Americans cross the continent for a generation. Joint ticketing; airfields sited adjacent to rail termini; purpose-built transfer halls. It survives into the 1950s and leaves permanent marks on WBT transport geography after it fades.

American airship use
  • Navy — long-range maritime patrol; scouting for the battle fleet, including rigid "flying carriers" operating parasite scout aircraft, viable far longer than OTL because carrier aviation sits at OTL-1925 levels in 1932; convoy escort and ASW 1932–40; postwar picket and early warning into the 1960s.
  • Federal Airways System — beacon and station resupply into remote country; airships as survey platforms for building the airway itself.
  • Federal Parks Service and geological survey — aerial mapping of the West.
  • Heavy and outsize cargo into roadless country — Alaska, the northern Rockies. The niche that never dies.
  • Home Guard — flood and hurricane survey, relief delivery, consistent with its FEMA-equivalent role.
  • Not a dense domestic passenger network. Americans encounter airships as ocean crossings, freight to hard places, and things the Navy flies.

Unresolved
consequence
The Global War spans the HTA transition: it begins as a WWI-style air war and ends as a WWII-style one. Fabric biplanes over Bavaria in 1933; all-metal monoplanes by 1939. Two consequences require deliberate decisions: strategic bombing is feeble until the last two or three years, so European cities likely emerge less destroyed than OTL's, with knock-on effects for postwar reconstruction and the cinema bible; and naval aviation is weak, so battleships stay dominant longer and the Pacific theatre against China is fought substantially by surface fleets.
Long Arc
The 1960s Shift
~1958–1975

The shift is not caused by airships getting worse. It is caused by the passenger disappearing. By the mid-1960s WBT civil aviation reaches roughly OTL-1958 — pressurised, long-range, reliable — and the transatlantic run drops from two and a half days to hours. The business traveller and anyone who simply needs to arrive leaves in a rush.

What remains is the passenger who was never buying transport: the crossing itself, the observation lounge, the dining room, three days of being nowhere. That customer barely shrinks. The collapse in volume is also a clarification of what the industry was actually selling.

The precedent is exact and is the reason this is credible: OTL jets killed scheduled transatlantic liner service in the same decade, and the liner companies did not vanish — they became the cruise industry. Same ships, same crews, same ports, entirely different product.

PeriodStage
~1958–65Erosion. Business traffic goes first; load factors fall on the fast routes. Both operators cut frequency and begin marketing the experience rather than the schedule — a defensive move that turns out to be the future.
~1965–70The break. Scheduled point-to-point rigid service ends. Not by announcement; routes are withdrawn one at a time until nothing is left. The last scheduled crossing is a news item nobody planned.
~1968–75The new product. Circuits and cruises rather than routes: Caribbean and Gulf in winter, the Pacific Northwest, Alaska and the northern parks in summer — which conveniently inverts the hurricane problem, since the ships are elsewhere when the Gulf is dangerous. Fewer, larger, more comfortable ships; fares aimed at people with time.
Who survives, and why

The decisive divergence has a date decades earlier than the collapse. TAS could not diversify because its advantages did not transfer — its entire position was a bundle of government contracts written around airships, for a state that already had an incumbent, and its parent built airships, so a fixed-wing subsidiary would have competed with WAE's own product line. TGA diversified because it had nothing to protect. Its first fixed-wing feeder routes appear in the 1950s, initially framed as connecting traffic to its airship trunk routes.

By the time the break arrives, TGA is already half an airline and TAS is still purely an airship operator, shifting to cruising only at the last moment and unable to compete. The company that was hostage to its own success dies of it.

1960s TAS folds; Vanguard absorbs WAE

TAS folds first. With its operator gone, WAE is doomed, and Vanguard buys the firm — acquiring the yards, the sheds and the naval contracts. What Vanguard is buying is the non-rigid utility line, which is still valuable; the prestige rigid business is the liability.

Vanguard shifts all rigid construction to the old Weiss yards, operating as Weiss-Vanguard Rigid Airship Works, and converts its own primary yards to semi-rigid and non-rigid volume production. All other Vanguard output carries the primary name. The founder's name survives on the prestige line specifically — Patterson's company keeping the name it beat, on the product he was originally denied a voice in. The 1889 name is still on a shed in 2026.

Large rigids become rare, not extinct. The cruise market sustains only a handful of hulls and rigids last decades, so new construction runs to roughly one hull every ten to fifteen years. A keel laid at Weiss-Vanguard is national news in Texas, and the yard spends most of its life on refits and overhauls rather than building — which makes keeping rigid-building expertise alive between hulls a genuine and permanent workforce problem.

What TGA becomes

A major continental passenger airline headquartered in New Frankfurt, flying throughout the NAU — a Texian flag carrier by accident, having never taken a dollar of state support, which is the joke at TAS's expense. It retains a heritage rigid cruise division, so the company still flies the thing it was founded to fly, as a boutique line inside a modern airline. The 2010s cargo revival gives it a third airship generation.

Sixty years, end to end, from a founder ignoring his engineer to that engineer's company owning his.
