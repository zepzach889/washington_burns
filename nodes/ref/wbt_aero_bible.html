Governing principles WBT aviation diverges from OTL not by being uniformly behind, but by being differently shaped. The absence of a 1910s general war removes the single greatest accelerant in OTL aviation history, and the presence of a large, helium-fed lighter-than-air industry gives the early twentieth century a long-haul air transport option that OTL never had at scale. The result is a civil aviation sector that is slower, smaller, later-forming and more institutionally regulated than OTL's — and simultaneously world-leading in airways infrastructure, safety certification, meteorology, large lightweight structures and long-endurance engines. Nothing in this document requires airships to be better than they were. It requires airplanes to be worse for longer.
Framework
The Heavier-Than-Air Lag
1901–1970

The point of origin is fixed. Hard Canon

On June 14th, 1901, two machinists from the small community of Red Fork — Oscar Vaughn and Levi Harcourt — fitted a lightweight kerosene motor to a canvas-and-wood biplane glider and coaxed it into a brief, steady flight of several hundred feet… the state of Jefferson proudly claims the moniker "First in Flight."
Six causes of the lag

The lag is deliberately over-determined so that no single mechanism carries suspicious weight. These divide into capability causes, which slow the technology itself, and market causes, which shrink and reshape the industry without making the machines worse.

  1. No 1910s forcing function. (capability) The Great European War ends in 1904; the Matanzas War in 1906; the Global War does not begin until 1932. That is roughly twenty-six years of peace covering exactly the window in which OTL's WWI drove aero engines from ~80hp to ~400hp in four years. This cause alone accounts for most of the deficit.
  2. No 1919 surplus dump. (capability) No cheap war-surplus airframes or engines, no demobilised pilot pool, no trained mechanic generation, and therefore no barnstorming culture — see below.
  3. No mature mass-market gasoline engine industry. (capability) WBT's automobile market never consolidated on internal combustion; steam, gasoline and electric hold durable separate niches. American aviation cannot draw on an OTL-scale gasoline engine sector in the 1900s–1920s because one does not exist.
  4. The V&H patent thicket, ~1906–1932. (capability) See §United States.
  5. USRC owns short and medium haul. (market) An excellent, publicly beloved, politically unassailable state railway with passenger priority. Domestic aviation's natural market is occupied from the start.
  6. USP&T flies its own mail. (market) In OTL, federal airmail contracts to private carriers were the seed capital of the entire American airline industry. In WBT that seed is never planted.

Minor, seventh: gasoline distribution is regional, expensive and strategically exposed until NAOTA (1930), while kerosene — the airship fuel — has a nationwide retail network from the lamp-oil era. A real advantage, but second-order: ground handling, not fuel, is the binding constraint on airship networks.

Counter-pressureWBT's airship industry is much larger than OTL's and employs aerodynamicists, structural engineers, metallurgists and meteorologists at scale from the 1900s. A meaningful fraction of that knowledge transfers to large aircraft. The net figures below already account for this spillover.

The lag never closes and never widens, stabilising at five to seven years. Two splits govern it:

  • Military aviation catches up fully by ~1950. Fission weapons (1951–52), the China rivalry and the Choson War (1966–68) supply as much pressure as OTL. No military lag survives contact with a nuclear rival.
  • Civil aviation carries the lag. Cabin pressurisation is the load-bearing item: worked on but not solved in service during the Global War, reaching military high-altitude aircraft ~1938–40 and not becoming a usable civil product until the late 1940s at the earliest. An unpressurised airliner cannot climb over weather, so 1940s WBT air travel is low, bumpy, frequently cancelled and genuinely unpleasant.
Capability shape — structurally modern, underpowered

The lag is not uniform across the aircraft. OTL 1925 already had all-metal cantilever monoplanes in airline service; what it lacked was the military monoplane revolution of 1933–36. WBT is further complicated by the airship industry, whose core competency — duralumin and large lightweight stressed-skin structures — transfers directly to airframes.

WBT aircraft in 1932 are structurally modern and underpowered — clean metal monoplanes flying on engines that cannot exploit them. Military aviation is transitional, with monoplanes displacing biplanes rather than biplanes dominating. When engine power finally arrives during the Global War, aircraft improve very fast, because the airframes were already waiting: this is the mechanism behind the 1932–40 compression.

Cultural consequence: gliding, not barnstorming

With no surplus dump, OTL's barnstorming culture has no fuel supply and does not occur. Its replacement arises from the same constraint that killed it: V&H's patents make powered flight expensive and legally hazardous, while gliders are cheap, buildable in a barn, and commercially uninteresting to litigate over. Once the core patents lapse around 1922, gliding is entirely free while powered flight remains costly.

American amateur aviation in the 1920s is therefore a soaring movement — clubs, ridge sites, duration and altitude records, a homebuilt tradition. Bluffs and ridgelines instead of cow pastures; quieter, more middle-class, more club-based, less carnival. It produces the pilot pool that would otherwise be missing in 1932, and it adds an edge to V&H's legacy: the firm that made flight expensive is the reason a generation learned to fly without engines.

1920s American commercial aviation is correspondingly institutional, professional and elite — manufacturer teams and federal pilots, not stunt fliers at county fairs. Air racing exists as a corporate showcase.

Framework
The Fuel Split

WBT aviation divides permanently by fuel, on physical grounds.

Heavier-than-air

Gasoline

Spark-ignition piston engines need octane. Kerosene vaporises poorly in a carburettor, forces low compression and caps power. The Prairie Hawk runs kerosene in 1901 because that is what every general store sold — but the moment anyone wants real horsepower, gasoline is the only road.

Lighter-than-air

Kerosene / diesel

Airships are weight-indifferent and need multi-day endurance and fuel economy; volatile fuel beside a gas envelope is a poor idea. Diesel is the correct airship fuel and WBT's industry funds its development for decades.

NAOTA (1930) is the hinge. Texian crude floods in, gasoline becomes cheap and widely distributed, and the airplane's fuel-logistics disadvantage collapses — two years before the Global War forces the issue. The kerosene lineage never dies, because the airship industry keeps kerosene infrastructure commercially important right through the era when OTL America consolidated on gasoline.

Open Canvas — TurbinesWith the automotive sector explicitly not entering turbine development, WBT's turbine metallurgy descends from naval steam turbines and aero-engine hot-section work, as OTL. Turbines therefore proceed roughly on schedule, but civil adoption is turboprop-first and jets arrive military-first, late and grudgingly. Detailed timeline deferred to a postwar session.
Lighter-Than-Air
Three Centers, Three Philosophies

Rigid airship development is genuinely parallel, but the three centers differ by function and constraint rather than by simultaneous identical invention. Ferdinand von Zeppelin is not transplanted to Texas; the older draft's version of that origin is retired.

Prussia — the engineering rival

Hydrogen

Military-origin, descended directly from Great European War balloon experience. High performance, structurally aggressive, better lift-to-size, worse safety. Prestige-driven. The UER inherits and continues a parallel line after 1904.

Prussia has no helium and never will.

Texas Federation — the builder

Helium

Helium-first from the start because that is what lies under the Panhandle. Ships are bigger and more conservative for the same payload. German-Texan machine shops are the industrial substrate — canon since the 1830s, reinforced by the 1841 refugee wave. Strong semi-rigid and non-rigid tradition alongside the rigids.

United States — buyer & regulator

Not an independent inventor

The US buys (chiefly through the Navy), certifies, and provides the continental market. Texian firms build American subsidiaries inside the tariff wall on the Oststein precedent. Texas builds them; America regulates and flies them.

Permanent limits — do not soften Speed ceiling ~80–100 mph, forever. Weather-limited permanently: routes and seasons, not schedules; radar and modern forecasting make them safer, not more punctual. Ground handling never becomes casual — masts, sheds, 100–200 crew — which alone bars airships from point-to-point service and caps North America at roughly 6–12 terminals ever. Short and medium haul are lost immediately and permanently. Payload fraction is poor: airships beat roads that do not exist, carrying outsize loads to places without runways. They never compete with ships on bulk or rail on anything.
Helium does not make airships safe It prevents one failure mode out of several. Large rigids break up in weather because they are enormous, lightly built, and cannot outrun or outclimb a storm. WBT's airship industry carries a real and continuous accident record — a major loss every few years across the 1920s–50s, with fatalities. It is a premium mode with a genuinely bad safety record, accepted because the alternative is six days at sea.

Lighter-Than-Air
Helium Politics & the Prussian Disaster
1900–1931
Geology The Hugoton–Panhandle field

The world's only significant helium source, shared between the United States (Jefferson and Kansas) and the Texas Federation, and weighted toward Texas. The Oklahoma Panhandle is Texian territory in this timeline. Neither nation can exploit the field alone, which manufactures a decades-long technical and regulatory partnership out of pure geology and gives the North American Conference framework a material rationale that predates its trade logic.

1900s–20s Export open — Prussia buys North American helium

Transatlantic shipment in cylinders is brutally expensive, so Prussia can afford it only for flagship passenger and state airships. Prussian aviation is consequently split: prestige ships on imported helium, military and second-line ships still on hydrogen.

1930 Restriction under NAOTA

Helium export is curtailed as part of the North American Open Trade Agreement, in reaction to escalating rhetoric from Berlin. The counterparty on the Texian side is the Texian Rare Gas Consortium. Prussia's best ships lose their gas.

Disaster
Apr 1931
LSL-44 Hohenzollern — Returning from a state visit to Vienna, the ship explodes on approach to the Berlin landing field, in full view of press cameras including invited foreign press. 112 aboard; approximately 40 survive. Lost: Ernst Reimers, Royal Prussian Minister of Aeronautics and the airship programme's political champion, and Prince Joachim Hohenzollern, 26, cousin to Karl-Albrecht II. She was designed for helium and completed on hydrogen because the gas never arrived after the 1930 restriction.

The builder. Luftschiffbau Schütz-Lehmann, founded c. 1900–1905 out of Great European War balloon work; Prussia's dominant airship builder, with at least one rival firm. Ships carry the mark LSL-## — Luftschiff, builder letters, sequential number — so the 1931 loss is the forty-fourth hull.

No coverup. The disaster happens in front of cameras and cannot be suppressed. State propaganda blames hydrogen, and blames North America for forcing Prussia onto it.

Why that line is more effective than a lie. It is not an admission of inferiority but a claim of victimhood, and it is substantially true — the restriction is the proximate cause of the ship flying hydrogen. So the regime never has to concede that its engineering was worse, and it acquires an honourable exit from rigids: no German will be sent up in hydrogen while the Americans hoard helium. The programme dies without anyone admitting failure, which is far more plausible than a programme dying of embarrassment. Helium becomes a standing Prussian grievance in the eighteen months before the referendum demands, and an effective one, because foreign audiences can see the point.

The founding martyr image of the Nationalist era. A minister and a prince, on film, blamed on foreigners. The ship named for the dynasty, killing a prince of that dynasty, before the new king's capital, in the second year of his reign.

Military consequence. The Prussian LTA programme collapses; with its patron dead nobody will defend it. Prussia enters the Global War in 1932 with no credible lighter-than-air arm while North America has a mature helium capability — so Prussian U-boats face an airborne threat they cannot answer in kind. North America's partial culpability is public and internationally argued, not merely known to the reader.

European aftermath
UER

Civilian use withdrawn

The UER flew rigids only for military, scientific and exhibition purposes — never scheduled passenger service. Civilian use is withdrawn after April 1931.

As a North American ally from 1932, the UER gains helium access during the Global War — flying safe airships while Prussia flies none. Lifting gas maps exactly onto the alliance structure. Postwar it retains helium and wartime experience but has no passenger tradition to revive, so its airships stay institutional.

Britain & Canada

The Canadian loophole

Britain adopts a similar restriction — then finds the gap. Canada is inside NAOTA and can buy. A Canadian-registered operator or Canadian subsidiary obtains gas London cannot purchase directly.

A live diplomatic irritant with no hostility behind it: an integration framework doing something nobody intended. It puts Canada in an awkward triangulation — NAU member, British dominion, and the pipe through which British strategic material flows — and forces Franklin and Texopolis to choose between policing Canadian resale and tolerating a hole. Once Britain is an ally in 1932, the loophole retroactively looks like foresight.

Open CanvasWhether Schütz-Lehmann is Berlin-adjacent or sited on the Baltic or in Silesia; the name of its rival. Whether neutral intermediaries attempt to transship "Canadian" helium eastward — the mechanism that would force the United States and Texas to tighten Canadian resale terms.
Lighter-Than-Air
The Texian Airship Industry
1889 onward
Geography
  • Panhandle — helium extraction, purification and storage. Roughly 600 miles from the yards; the cylinder rail link is the industry's critical strategic vulnerability in wartime.
  • New Frankfurt — construction, sheds, operations, headquarters. A major city in the state of Austin, centred somewhat inland rather than on Trinity Bay. There is no Houston in this timeline. The name carries the German-Texan settlement history that produced the industry, and echoes the 1839 Frankfurt republic whose collapse drove the 1841 refugee wave.
  • Texopolis — political operation and government relations.
The 1900 hurricane — founding trauma

A Galveston-analogue storm strikes with the firms already in existence and their sheds on the bayside, southeast of the city. The sheds are destroyed. The industry rebuilds north of New Frankfurt, away from the worst of any landfall — a move that is a scar rather than a plan, and from which everything afterward descends.

  • Hurricane season is a permanent operating constraint. Ships are walked or flown inland on warning. Gulf and Caribbean service becomes effectively a winter-and-spring product; insurance and scheduling are built around the season.
  • Texian meteorology becomes world-leading, privately funded from the 1900s by an industry whose survival depends on forecasting hurricanes days out — decades before OTL states took forecasting seriously. Storm warning is a public good, so the institution likely becomes quasi-public, and is a natural candidate for early NAU-era continental science.
The firms
1889 Weiss Machine Works / Weiss Maschinenwerke Gustav Weiss

Founded in southeast New Frankfurt near Trinity Bay. Dual-named in German and English, as was common in pre-twentieth-century Texas. German-Texan machine shop descent; the prestige house.

Renamed Weiss Aeronautical Engineering (WAE) in 1902 following the post-hurricane relocation to the north side of the city — the company's name changes because its address did, and both changed because of the storm.

1903 Vanguard Aeronautics Raymond Patterson

Patterson — an Anglo-Texian engineer inside a German-Texan machine-shop culture, and therefore structurally an outsider — had warned about the bayside siting and been ignored. The storm vindicated him. He was then ignored again on the rebuild. He departs, raises outside capital, and founds a rival firm in New Frankfurt.

The two firms compete for both rigid and non-rigid contracts, permanently and head to head. The rivalry has a grievance at its root and the ethnic, generational and commercial fault lines all run the same direction.

~1917–25 Texian Rare Gas Consortium

A regulated cartel under Texian charter, Panhandle-based, formed once helium's strategic value became obvious. Not a shipbuilder — deliberately separated from the yards so that no single entity both sells the gas and builds the ships. The counterparty for all helium export policy from the 1920s onward, and unaffected by the later collapse of passenger service.

The operators
WAE subsidiary · 1902–04

Texian Airship Service (TAS)

Founded as Trinity Airship Service, renamed 1904. Holds a bundle of small standing government contracts: trunk and ocean mail, coastal and maritime patrol, aerial survey and mapping, storm reconnaissance, frontier patrol in Nuevo Mexico and Alta Chihuahua, and official transport.

No single subsidy exists — nobody in Texopolis ever votes for one, which suits a laissez-faire federation. But TAS enters every season with revenue already booked. It is a pseudo-subsidy nobody has to defend, and an incumbency accident that hardened into structure.

Vanguard-backed · 1906

Trinity-Gulf Airline → TGA

Founded by Vanguard's financial backers. Fully private, no cushion, mixed rigid and non-rigid passenger service. Competes on price and service because it cannot compete on politics — which sharpens the engineering rivalry rather than turning it into a lobbying war.

By the 1950s the name has contracted to TGA and the letters stop standing for anything.

Support model

Rigids are lumpy business, with years between hulls. The industry is carried by US Navy procurement — foreign money, deep pockets — combined with internal cross-subsidy from non-rigid volume. Texas's most glamorous industry is therefore structurally dependent on decisions made in Franklin, and quietly hostage to any US–Texas friction.

Open CanvasWhether the 1900 storm is the same event as OTL's Galveston hurricane or a near-analogue. Ship classes and named hulls. The industry's labour history — Austin abolished slavery in the 1870s, within a few years of the War Between the States, so the workforce of the 1889–1900 founding era is a full generation past emancipation; no OTL Southern industrial pattern should be imported.
United States
Firms & Federal Structure
V&H Aeronautics

Vaughn and Harcourt's partnership holds. Red Fork Machine Works becomes V&H Aeronautics by ordinary commercial contraction. Canon establishes them as machinists supplying engines and parts, so the firm's natural descent is as an engine house that broadens into airframes later — engines being the highest barrier to entry in aviation and the reason a 1901 machine shop is still a major firm in 2026.

They are also, deliberately, the patent aggressors. They hold the foundational claims, litigate ferociously, and layer improvement patents as core claims lapse. They get rich. They stunt American heavier-than-air growth for a generation.

PeriodState of the thicket
~1903–08Core claims granted. Enforcement begins.
~1906–22The hard years. Maximum suppression — anyone building airframes in America is paying V&H or in court with them. This is the window that must be starved to put WBT at OTL-mid-1920s levels in 1932.
~1922–30Core patents lapse; V&H sustain the fight on improvement claims. Grip weakening, litigiousness undiminished. By 1925 they are widely understood to be litigating on fumes and doing it anyway. The 1920s boom begins.
1932The Preparation Act imposes a war-production patent pool — less a liberation than a coup de grâce on a fading position.

Because patents are federal, licensees cannot escape by relocating. The industry that emerges is small and concentrated — a handful of licensed firms rather than OTL's hundreds of garage builders. V&H's legacy is permanently complicated, and "Jefferson: First in Flight" acquires a rueful edge: there is a live historiographical argument in 2026 about whether the men who invented flight also held it back a generation.

Federal structure
~1922–26 onward

USP&T Air Service

The Post & Telegraph operates federal air mail with its own aircraft plus contracted private work, and maintains a uniformed federal pilot corps. Later than OTL's 1918 start because there is no war-surplus aircraft and no military pilot pool.

By 1932 this corps is the largest pool of trained American aviators in the country — a major mobilisation asset. WBT's wartime air arm is built substantially out of postal pilots.

Late 1920s → 1932

Division of Air Navigation → AirNav

Created inside USP&T to serve its own mail flying, then opened to all. Owns the ground and nothing else: airfields, lighted beacons, radio ranges, weather stations, emergency landing grounds. It does not fly. The analogue is the lighthouse service or USRC's track, not an airline.

Substantially cheaper to build than its OTL counterpart thanks to the Rural Electrification Act of 1910. Split off during the Global War as the Federal Office of Air Navigation under the Department of Transportation (est. 1894, President Beck) — universally called AirNav.

1929–31 · King administration

Bureau of Air Commerce

A direct federal inspectorate — explicitly not the 1919 standards-and-reciprocity model. Aviation is the point where that architecture fails: a boiler stays in one state, but an aircraft is in five before lunch, and most states would never develop airworthiness expertise at all. Reciprocal recognition of certifications that mostly do not exist is not a regime.

Founding mandate is safety and infrastructure standards only — airworthiness certification, pilot licensing, airway and airport standards. No route or fare authority until after the war.

Naming note: replaces the working title "Federal Aviation Bureau," which collided with Fleak's Federal Arbitration Bureau (~1900).

Explicitly not

USRC has no air arm

The state railway never operates aircraft. It sells the through-ticket and subcontracts the flying — later taking equity in its main contractor, and divesting in 1949–50.

⚠ The first centralised agency The Bureau is WBT's first genuinely centralised federal regulator, and a precedent for everything later that needs one. That makes its creation a live constitutional fight in 1929–31 — landing hardest on Jefferson, the aviation industry's home state and JCA country, with Sen. Andrew VanNess (JCA-Jefferson, 1928 VP nominee) already in place to object.

International airworthiness is handled by bilateral treaty between the United States and Texas, not by the Bureau, which is a domestic body. A continent-wide air commerce regulator arrives later — either through NAOTA machinery or, more likely, with the postwar establishment of the NAU.

Rail–air intermodal, and what it left behind

Fly by day, rail by night. In OTL this model lasted about two years because aircraft improved fast enough to kill it; in WBT aircraft do not, and it becomes the way Americans cross the continent for a generation. Joint through-ticketing sold by USRC, with the flying subcontracted to small carriers who thereby acquire guaranteed traffic and total dependency on a state railway. By the late 1920s USRC has taken equity in its main contractor to secure the relationship.

What frays it is range, not comfort. Wartime transports entering service 1941–45 can fly the continent in stages without the rail night-leg; the day-legs join up. Flying is still miserable — just miserable all the way through instead of miserable in shifts. Through-ticketing withers in the late 1940s, and USRC is required to divest after President Nolan takes office in 1947, by 1949–50. It is selling a declining asset rather than defending a prize: a footnote in the statute rather than its centrepiece.

⭐ The residue — rail-connected airportsAirfields were sited adjacent to rail termini with purpose-built transfer halls, built 1910s–30s for a business that ended around 1950. In OTL, airport rail links are expensive late retrofits and most American cities never built one, because airports were sited in the 1940s–50s on cheap fringe land reachable by new highways. In WBT they are inheritances. Some cities keep and grow the terminus site; some outgrow it and extend rail to the new field, because the expectation that airports have trains was already baked in; a resented minority rebuild without. Compounds with no interstate programme, streetcar suburbs and USRC passenger priority — every structural fact points the same way. In 2026 you land, walk to a platform, and take a train into the city, and nobody finds it remarkable. Knock-ons: smaller airport parking business, rental cars less central, airport hotels clustered at rail junctions, and earlier noise regulation because airports are nearer people.
American airship use
  • Navy — long-range maritime patrol; scouting for the battle fleet, including rigid "flying carriers" operating parasite scout aircraft, viable far longer than OTL because carrier aviation sits at OTL-1925 levels in 1932; convoy escort and ASW 1932–40; postwar picket and early warning into the 1960s.
  • AirNav — beacon and station resupply into remote country; airships as survey platforms for building the airway itself.
  • Federal Parks Service and geological survey — aerial mapping of the West.
  • Heavy and outsize cargo into roadless country — Alaska, the northern Rockies. The niche that never dies.
  • Home Guard — flood and hurricane survey, relief delivery, consistent with its FEMA-equivalent role.
  • Not a dense domestic passenger network. Americans encounter airships as ocean crossings, freight to hard places, and things the Navy flies.

Lighter-Than-Air
Terminal Geography

Facilities exist in tiers, and the distinction is load-bearing. A rigid shed for a large ship runs roughly 800 feet long, 180 feet high and 250 feet wide — a cathedral that dominates any site it occupies and costs more than the rest of the field combined. A mooring mast is trivial. Confusing the two produces a network airships cannot operate.

The timing runs airship-first. Rigid terminals date from the 1910s–20s; Air Navigation fields are late 1920s; commercial HTA airfields at scale are 1930s. Pre-1930 sites are therefore airship-first and the airfield grows up beside them — attracted by flat land, road and rail access, fuel storage, a weather office and a trained aviation workforce already in place. Post-1930 sites are airfield-first with a mast added. This leaves a permanent physical residue: major North American airports oddly shaped, oddly sited or oddly named because they grew around an airship field.

Operational constraint A rigid can only overnight, shelter or undergo maintenance where there is a shed. That hard-limits where a rigid can go in bad weather, and is part of why the accident record is what it is.

Three Pacific doors, not redundancy. With California independent, the American, Canadian and Californian coasts each require their own facility. The same logic makes San Diego non-negotiable for California — its second city and its film money cannot use Astoria.

Government & prestige facilities

Texopolis, Franklin and Aurora. All three are capitals, all three are inland, and all three are superbly served by rail. There is no commercial case — only a political one. One shed apiece, state visits, official transport.

The Caribbean — an ascending arc

Havana and San Juan both carry airship service, on a trajectory opposite to everywhere else. Ancillary during the Golden Age with barely serviceable facilities; premier once the Cruise Age begins, when both see substantial upgrades to handle the upscale market. The terminals that mattered least become the ones that matter most, and the 1970s upgrade wave is a visible physical event in both cities.

Secondary — mast or non-rigid shed at a commercial airfield

San Diego, Toronto, El Paso, Santa Fe, Mexican ports including Veracruz, and others as routes require. Airfield-first, post-1930. Some carry a non-rigid shed; most carry only a mast. ⚠️ Altitude note: Santa Fe and El Paso sit high enough that thinner air costs lift — ships arrive with reduced payload — and mountain flying is where airships die. Mooring stations at best.

Where sheds exist, their afterlife matters: they become curiosities, warehouses, or are demolished. Calumet's rigid shed is the likeliest casualty — cruising is a coastal product, so an interior cathedral-scale structure goes from busy to purposeless faster than any coastal shed, and is the natural subject of a 1970s preservation fight.

Cargo masts — the 2010s revival

Different in kind from everything above. No sheds and no permanent terminals: hybrid air vehicles land on unimproved ground. Facilities are temporary and often private — a mine, a construction project or a resource camp builds a pad, uses it for three years and abandons it. The permanent nodes are southern railheads where cargo transfers from rail to air: northern Alberta or Saskatchewan, an Alaskan interior hub, a Labrador or Nunavut node.

This is NAU Arctic policy infrastructure, not commercial aviation. ⚠️ Note that this generation has almost nothing in common operationally with the 1920s rigids — different craft, different infrastructure, different customer. The continuity is corporate and industrial, not technical, which is what keeps the revival from reading as nostalgia.

Global War
The Air Campaign
1932–1940

By 1940 bombing capability is roughly OTL 1939–40: twin-engine mediums, no four-engine heavies at scale, no radar navigation, no bombsight precision, no thousand-plane raids, bomb loads in single tons. The force required to destroy a city from the air does not exist before the shooting stops. No firestorm is possible at any point in the war.

Bombing therefore means ports, rail junctions, marshalling yards, oil, factories, dams and canals — hit repeatedly and inaccurately, with real civilian casualties as spillover from aiming at something rather than as the objective.

1932–33 Saint-Dié — the Prussian atrocity

A Prussian raid on a Vosges town of some 20,000, southeast of Nancy in French Lorraine — which remained French, and then UER, throughout this timeline. Nominally aimed at the rail line through the Vosges passes. It missed. 400–700 dead; the historic centre largely destroyed. No fortress, no garrison, no excuse available.

Saint-Dié is where America was named — the 1507 Cosmographiae Introductio, printed there, first put the word in print. That predates the point of divergence by three centuries and is true in WBT exactly as in OTL.

So the interventionist argument in Franklin acquires a name and a place, running for two years before the 1934 declaration — not an abstract appeal to sympathy for European civilians but a direct claim on American identity.

Area bombing — tried, and abandoned

Precision bombing aims at a specific target and kills civilians by missing. Area bombing aims at a district — housing, workers, morale — and kills civilians on purpose. In OTL the second was adopted because the first had failed and abandoning bombing altogether was politically impossible.

WBT reaches the same fork. Through 1933–36 precision bombing achieves little because the aircraft cannot hit what they aim at. Around 1936–37 someone proposes aiming at districts instead, and it is tried a handful of times.

Atrocity
1937
Hamburg — A joint British–UER–American mission; America participates but does not lead. Aimed at the docks and the yards. 2,000–3,000 dead. The docks kept working. Not cruel and effective — cruel and pointless, with a ledger small enough that everyone could read it. The doctrine is abandoned the same year.

The rhyme. The war opens and closes with the same failure: bombers that cannot hit what they aim at, killing civilians instead. Prussia called Saint-Dié an accident. Nobody can call Hamburg anything. And American aircraft flew at Hamburg five years after Saint-Dié had been the argument for intervention.

Hamburg's inversion is deliberate: in WBT it is the raid that stopped area bombing rather than the raid that epitomised it. The city hosted the 1926 Olympics eleven years earlier.

The joint command problem

Because no single government owns the decision, there is an accountability vacuum. Three national inquiries reach three conclusions and none satisfies anybody. A permanent historiographical fight follows: the UER blames British doctrine, Britain blames UER fury over the invasion, America says it flew a mission it was handed — and all three are partly right.

For America the position is the worst possible one for memory: neither innocent nor responsible. That ambiguity is exactly why it can be relitigated forever, and it will be — when the argument reopens against China in 1939–40, and again in 1966.

The postwar lesson runs beyond air power: joint command diffuses accountability. That becomes a live argument in the design of GPC and later NAU military structures.

The late-war escalation

Europe's war ends earliest — armistice autumn 1938 — just as capability is arriving. The force built for a 1939 European campaign never flies it; the Prussian revolution ends the war first. That capability transfers to the Pacific, where fighting runs to 1940 against an undefeated China, and the argument reopens: newest aircraft, different enemy, and everyone who won in 1937 obliged to win again. The ceasefire arrives before it is settled.

So restraint is a matter of timing rather than virtue, and everyone knows what the next war would look like.

⚠ Racially loaded, and should be A restraint argument won in Europe and reopened against an Asian adversary is a pattern with a name. WBT's own canon — Freeman's presidency, the postwar civil rights arc, the Choson War's political volatility — supplies the tools to examine it rather than let it pass. This is the most morally serious thread in the aviation arc.
Downstream consequences

Combined with existing canon — the sun bomb arrives in 1951, after the war, with no Hiroshima or Nagasaki analogue — the result is that WBT's twentieth century has no image of a destroyed city. No Dresden, no Hamburg as OTL knows it, no Tokyo, no Hiroshima. The defining visual of OTL's modern era does not exist.

  • The sun bomb is abstract. Nobody has seen what it does to a populated place. Deterrence rests on a threat no one has witnessed; nuclear fear is speculative rather than remembered. ⚠️ A genuine strategic-stability question for the Cold War analogue.
  • Reconstruction is economic and political, not physical. European housing stock, industrial plant and city centres largely survive.
  • The Prussian revolution of 1937–38 happens in intact cities. A population that has not been bombed flat revolts differently, and the German Republic inherits functioning infrastructure — likely making it more stable than OTL's equivalent.
  • Douhet is tested and found wanting. Postwar air forces emphasise tactical support, interdiction and maritime patrol. The Air Force created under Freeman is institutionally sceptical of strategic bombing in a way OTL's emphatically was not, with consequences for CDC's structure and self-image.
  • War films have no rubble. The visual vocabulary of twentieth-century war is ground, sea and air combat. ⚠️ Relevant to the cinema bible's Global War cluster.
  • The war is remembered as grinding and exhausting rather than apocalyptic. Eight years of attrition, not a moral abyss.
  • A precedent for 1966. The Choson War bombing of Beijing and Shanghai becomes the argument finally lost — twenty-six years later, against the same country. Chinese memory of it runs back to 1940. Not an isolated decision but the end of a long slide, which makes the resulting accountability reforms legible.

Naval aviation and the surface war remain unresolved: carrier aviation at OTL-1925 levels in 1932 means battleships stay dominant longer and the Pacific theatre is fought substantially by surface fleets.
Global War
The Naval War
1932–1940

Carrier aviation at OTL-1925 levels means biplane torpedo bombers, no dive bombing doctrine, no fighter direction, no radar, and carriers understood as scouting auxiliaries — which was the actual doctrine in 1925. Nobody believes aircraft can sink a battleship at sea, because they cannot. OTL's carrier revolution took the whole of the 1930s to build; WBT enters its war before that decade of development and has to do it under fire.

So battleships are genuinely dominant in 1932 — not by authorial fiat but because the aircraft cannot yet threaten them.

PeriodCharacter
1932–37The surface war. Gun engagements, night gunnery, destroyer torpedo work, shore bombardment, blockade, convoy protection. Closer to Jutland-with-better-ships than to Midway. Engagement ranges are shorter than OTL throughout — worse scouting, shorter strike legs — so the Pacific is fought in contested straits and coastal waters rather than across blue water. Rigid airships are the fleet's only long-range eyes, operating parasite scouts: not a curiosity but the central scouting instrument of the war's first six years.
1938–39The demonstration. Chinese land-based aircraft destroy Allied capital ships at anchor and in coastal waters. It is a defeat. The battleship age ends on the receiving end and the lesson is learned the expensive way.
1939–40Transition under fire. Aircraft finally capable; the first successful carrier strikes; a carrier building scramble that takes longer than the war has left. The carrier age is roughly one year old at the ceasefire.
The postwar navy is unsettled
  • The battleship is not discredited — it is interrupted. A real postwar faction argues the demonstrations were flukes of surprise and poor air defence. In OTL that argument was crushed by four years of carrier warfare; in WBT it is not. Battleship construction plausibly continues into the late 1940s and the transition is slow and contested.
  • Carrier doctrine is immature — no accumulated experience of fleet carrier operations. WBT's 1950s navy sits roughly where OTL's 1945 navy was.
  • Combined with the strategic bombing failure: a postwar military sceptical of strategic bombing, uncertain about carriers, and still attached to guns. Fission weapons arrive in 1951 into a force that has not settled its conventional doctrine — which shapes CDC's early character.
  • ⭐ The Choson War (1966–68) finally settles it. The battleship faction is put to bed by a second war, a full generation later.
Airships at war
The workhorse

Non-rigids

Hundreds of them, on convoy escort and ASW against Prussian U-boats and Spanish raiders. Nearly all survive — blimps are hard for submarines to fight. This is what keeps the yards hot through the war and makes the patronage bridge work.

Radius ~700–1,000 miles, endurance one to two days, no parasite aircraft. Genuine scouting, but coastal and near-fleet: they search a line close to home and come back tomorrow.

Two-stage withdrawal

Rigids

Radius ~2,500–3,000 miles, endurance four to five days, and four or five parasite scouts multiplying search width enormously. Nothing else can do this until 1938.

~1936 — out of contested forward waters. Losses to weather and improving fighters make forward operation unaffordable; non-rigids and carrier scouts absorb the coastal work. 1936–38 — retained for deep-ocean work in safe areas: mid-Atlantic convoy routes, eastern Pacific approaches, home waters. 1938–39 — the demonstration ends it. Fully rear-area thereafter: heavy logistics, trans-Pacific lift, training.

⭐ The symmetry The airship's most important military moment and its obsolescence are the same event. Aircraft good enough to sink battleships are aircraft good enough to kill airships.

Attrition is permanent. Rigids pulled from forward waters in 1936 are not worth replacing at wartime priority — they are slow to build and the war needs blimps and aircraft faster. The military rigid fleet shrinks by neglect across eight years and is never rebuilt. Rigids are permanently scarce afterward, and the Cruise Age is flown on refitted 1920s and 1930s survivors — which is why the 1960s collapse is total rather than gradual.

Airlines
HTA Passenger Service
⭐ The surplus dump is 1941, not 1919 Everything OTL's airline industry was built on — cheap war-surplus transports, thousands of demobilised pilots and mechanics, a national airfield network built for military use, and a public that had watched aviation work — arrives in WBT twenty-two years late, after a war far larger than WWI. American commercial aviation is therefore not merely 5–7 years behind technologically; it is roughly two decades behind in formation. The 1920s and 30s are prehistory. The founding era is 1941–1950.
Before 1932

Scheduled service exists but is regional, hoppy, uncomfortable and unglamorous — short hops on routes where geography beats rail, flown unpressurised, low, loud and frequently cancelled. A business traveller's time-saver, not a pleasure. Alongside it runs the intermodal day-leg business: guaranteed traffic, sold by USRC, flown by subcontractors who are connecting operators rather than independent airlines.

The 1920s expansion canon describes is real, but it is USP&T mail flying, Air Navigation airway construction, charter and survey work, aircraft manufacturing and the gliding movement — not a passenger industry.

The formation sequence
PeriodEvent
1929–31Bureau of Air Commerce founded with safety authority only. Certification means you must be fit to fly, not that you hold an economic franchise. Routes and fares are nobody's business.
1932–40War. Aviation directed by military necessity; commercial regulation moot. Transport commands, pilot training at scale, aircraft production, airfields everywhere, and a public that watches aviation become normal.
1941–47The trigger. Surplus aircraft, abundant pilots, hundreds of new entrants and no economic regulator. Fare wars, undercapitalised carriers failing mid-route, stranded passengers, safety corners cut under competitive pressure.
1949–50The Nolan settlement. The Bureau acquires route and fare authority by statute. Prewar regionals are consolidated into trunk carriers; USRC is required to divest its airline equity. The industry that exists in 1955 is a structured trunk system — but it got there by being a mess first, and the people who remember the mess wrote the rules.
NAU liberalisation — phased, and settled

Pan-American passport union dates from 1940, but free movement of people and open aviation markets are separate fights. The opening is a process, not an event.

First opening, 1960s. More gateway cities, expanded route rights, the right to carry beyond a first stop. Not cabotage. Enough to hurt an international carrier badly while leaving the domestic trunks untouched.

Final settlement, 1980s, closed by the early 1990s at the latest. Cabotage resolved, cross-border ownership rules set. ⭐ By 2026 the NAU air market is one of the older pieces of integration machinery — boring, functional, unarguable. TGA flies American domestic routes and nobody finds this remarkable; the generation that fought it is retired.

Airlines
The Carriers

Four origin pools feed the postwar majors: prewar regional hoppers (marginal, but holding operating experience and Bureau certification from 1929–31); intermodal contractors (best capitalised, national route knowledge, a state railway's blessing); wartime operators (fleets, crews, long-range experience); and postwar startups (surplus aircraft and cheap money, most of which die).

1910s Pan-Continental Airways — "PanCon" Brooklyn

Founded on Texian-built airships with American capital — the same relationship the US Navy has with New Frankfurt. The oldest passenger operator in America. International and long-haul only, because domestic short and medium haul belonged to USRC and the trunks: the flag carrier has no domestic feeder traffic, which is the necessary consequence of the airship niche rather than a business mistake.

⭐ It survives the 1960s opening on the airship inheritance — a thirty-year-old brand, established ground infrastructure at Brooklyn, New Orleans and the Caribbean gateways, and standing relationships in every port it serves. It is not a startup that grew fast on landing rights; and when TGA comes at it, it is fighting a rival it has known since the 1900s.

Panorama Class — the premium cabin, named for a famous early rigid. A 2026 passenger books it without knowing they are naming a ship that last flew in 1968.

1910s–20s Columbia Calumet

The intermodal contractor. USRC equity from the late 1920s until the 1949–50 divestiture, and inheritor of the intermodal route map, the terminal positions and the rail relationships built up over thirty years. The largest domestic carrier. Survives to 2026.

1920s–30s Frontier Astoria, Oregon

Began connecting the sparse western states — mountain West, high desert, long distances between small places, routes rail served badly. A wartime transport operator 1932–40: the war turned a company good at long empty distances into one good at oceans. Transpacific. Survives to 2026.

1949–50 Coronado St. Augustine

Assembled by the Bureau out of prewar regionals in the Nolan settlement: a stitched-together company with no single founding culture, a route map that made sense to a regulator and to nobody else, and the weakest balance sheet of the trunks. It was also the most stylish carrier in America — distinctive livery, good food, a famous terminal, a period when flying it was the point.

⚠ Style is expensive, and a carrier that spends on identity while running the weakest balance sheet has a countdown on it. The 1980s settlement kills it, c. 1988–92 — not the 1960s opening, which hit international carriers.

⭐ The nostalgia object. In 2026 the livery is still recognised, the merchandise sells, an aircraft is preserved, and a terminal somewhere is still called by its name.

c. 1946 Standard St. Louis

A postwar startup out of the surplus boom that survived the shakeout. Low-fare, plain, disliked, durable. The name was a promise in 1946 and is faintly ironic by 2026. Survives.

1906– TGA New Frankfurt, Texas

⚠ A foreign incumbent, not a new entrant. TGA served American cities by airship from the 1910s–20s — flights to the United States from Texas, the Caribbean and California, never between two American ports, which was domestic business. It has American ground infrastructure, staff, agents and customers going back decades.

That makes the postwar franchise question genuinely hard: when the Bureau begins awarding routes in 1949–50, TGA has been serving American cities for thirty years, and nobody drafting the original airship arrangements imagined the conversion to fixed-wing. Beyond-rights follow in the 1960s; full access with the 1980s settlement.

Long Arc
The 1960s Shift
~1958–1975

The shift is not caused by airships getting worse. It is caused by the passenger disappearing. By the mid-1960s WBT civil aviation reaches roughly OTL-1958 — pressurised, long-range, reliable — and the transatlantic run drops from two and a half days to hours. The business traveller and anyone who simply needs to arrive leaves in a rush.

What remains is the passenger who was never buying transport: the crossing itself, the observation lounge, the dining room, three days of being nowhere. That customer barely shrinks. The collapse in volume is also a clarification of what the industry was actually selling.

The precedent is exact and is the reason this is credible: OTL jets killed scheduled transatlantic liner service in the same decade, and the liner companies did not vanish — they became the cruise industry. Same ships, same crews, same ports, entirely different product.

PeriodStage
~1958–65Erosion. Business traffic goes first; load factors fall on the fast routes. Both operators cut frequency and begin marketing the experience rather than the schedule — a defensive move that turns out to be the future.
~1965–70The break. Scheduled point-to-point rigid service ends. Not by announcement; routes are withdrawn one at a time until nothing is left. The last scheduled crossing is a news item nobody planned.
~1968–75The new product. Circuits and cruises rather than routes: Caribbean and Gulf in winter, the Pacific Northwest, Alaska and the northern parks in summer — which conveniently inverts the hurricane problem, since the ships are elsewhere when the Gulf is dangerous. Fewer, larger, more comfortable ships; fares aimed at people with time.
Who survives, and why

The decisive divergence has a date decades earlier than the collapse. TAS could not diversify because its advantages did not transfer — its entire position was a bundle of government contracts written around airships, for a state that already had an incumbent, and its parent built airships, so a fixed-wing subsidiary would have competed with WAE's own product line. TGA diversified because it had nothing to protect. Its first fixed-wing feeder routes appear in the 1950s, initially framed as connecting traffic to its airship trunk routes.

By the time the break arrives, TGA is already half an airline and TAS is still purely an airship operator, shifting to cruising only at the last moment and unable to compete. The company that was hostage to its own success dies of it.

1960s TAS folds; Vanguard absorbs WAE

TAS folds first. With its operator gone, WAE is doomed, and Vanguard buys the firm — acquiring the yards, the sheds and the naval contracts. What Vanguard is buying is the non-rigid utility line, which is still valuable; the prestige rigid business is the liability.

Vanguard shifts all rigid construction to the old Weiss yards, operating as Weiss-Vanguard Rigid Airship Works, and converts its own primary yards to semi-rigid and non-rigid volume production. All other Vanguard output carries the primary name. The founder's name survives on the prestige line specifically — Patterson's company keeping the name it beat, on the product he was originally denied a voice in. The 1889 name is still on a shed in 2026.

Large rigids become rare, not extinct. The cruise market sustains only a handful of hulls and rigids last decades, so new construction runs to roughly one hull every ten to fifteen years. A keel laid at Weiss-Vanguard is national news in Texas, and the yard spends most of its life on refits and overhauls rather than building — which makes keeping rigid-building expertise alive between hulls a genuine and permanent workforce problem.

What TGA becomes

A major continental passenger airline headquartered in New Frankfurt, flying throughout the NAU — a Texian flag carrier by accident, having never taken a dollar of state support, which is the joke at TAS's expense. It retains a heritage rigid cruise division, so the company still flies the thing it was founded to fly, as a boutique line inside a modern airline. The 2010s cargo revival gives it a third airship generation.

Sixty years, end to end, from a founder ignoring his engineer to that engineer's company owning his.
