Notes on Contemporary Landscape

Bolt & Metro

The two dominant genres, both 70+ years old and still evolving. Bolt continues working class character with new iterations each decade. Metro fully normalized post-Pike — no longer coded, Aether as its artistic wing.

Zinga & Brisa

Both benefit from NAU integration and Long Boom prosperity. Zinga's Gulf roots and Brisa's Pacific character benefit from Chinese Spring (2019) opening new cultural exchanges. Firmly mainstream without being chart-dominant.

Blaze, Ragstep & Hop

Nostalgia tier — beloved, critically respected, never gone. Blaze has jazz-festival equivalent prestige. Ragstep and Hop have periodic revivals tied to anniversary moments. "Your grandparents' music" with genuine artistic weight.

Pulsar / Pulse

The youngest major genre, still growing its audience. The sound of the under-30 crowd. Interlink-native in how it spreads and is discovered. Post-Chinese Spring adding new sonic influences.

Track Three
Key Cultural Moments
Late 19th C — Early 1900s
Ragtime and Blaze emerge. New Orleans unambiguously the recording capital of the country by 1905–1910. Crescent Records and MBC's New Orleans roots deeply intertwined with Blaze's rise. Ragtime spreading nationally via early audiograph recordings.
1910s–1920s
Radio era begins. Blaze goes national through MBC. Ragstep emerges as Ragtime absorbs Blaze influence and evolves toward big-band sophistication. James "Sunny" Broussard defines the Blaze golden age on radio.
1920s–1930s
Ragstep golden age. Glenn Monroe, James Wolf, and Irene Hale define the era. Big band orchestras, dancehall culture, national radio programming. Celestine Beaumont reaches the artistic peak of Blaze.
1932–1939
The Global War. Music becomes a wartime institution. Monroe and Wolf both enlist, lead military bands. Irene Hale becomes the Forces' Sweetheart. Joint victory concerts cement all three as legends. The war plants seeds of the gay rights movement — Metro's distant origins.
Late 1940s–1950s
Ragstep electrifies into Hopstep. Xavier Drake pioneers the transition, dies 1955 at the moment of breakthrough. Dean Merritt and Loretta Hollis define Hopstep's golden age. Bolt, Metro, Zinga, and Brisa all emerging simultaneously as distinct streams.
1964 — Redstone, Minnesota
The Redstone Festival. The public collision of Bolt, Metro, Zinga, Brisa, and Hopstep. A cultural exhale at the end of Lincoln's presidency. Not the birth of something new — the moment when everything already developing becomes undeniable and visible. The Redstone Generation defined by this moment.
Late 1960s
The Experimental Period. Joyful chaos following Redstone. Cross-pollination across all streams. No dominant synthesis — musicians moving freely between traditions. The Schultz era's Choson War adds displaced protest energy that feeds the experimentation.
Early–Mid 1970s
Langston era crystallization. Expansive, continental, ambitious mainstream sound emerging. NAU Assembly establishment, moon landing, and bicentennial anticipation shaping cultural confidence. Counter-currents: impatient Metro, harder Bolt, anti-NAU nationalist sounds, artistic avant-garde.
1976
The Bicentennial. 200th anniversary of American independence. Major cultural touchstone. Country has its Americana boom moment. National reflection on the full sweep of WBT history — different from OTL, genuinely complicated and proud. Music, film, and literature all engage with American identity.
1983–1989
The Pike Presidency and Long Boom. Metro's triumphant moment — fully out of coded underground, decades of suppressed openness pouring into joyful music. Zinga and Brisa become genuinely mainstream. Hop enters nostalgic territory. The Long Boom produces excellence within existing forms rather than new genres.
Mid-1990s–2007
Post-Long Boom / Liu-Mercer era. Ottoman collapse ends the Long Boom. Anxious and melancholy cultural register. Thunder (hard electric Bolt) and Aether-Metro (synth/ethereal Metro) emerge as the era's signature subgenres. Music processing instability and the weight of complicated world events.
2007–2013
Sinclair era — Pulsar emerges. Space-influenced, electronic, cosmic. First Interlink-native genre. Gestating under Mercer, blooming under Sinclair. A generation that grew up with a functioning lunar base reaching for a sound that reflects that reality.
~2019
Chinese Spring. HuaWang dismantled, Chinese cultural production enters global Interlink. New sonic influences entering Brisa (Pacific Continental) especially. Pulsar absorbing new textures. Contemporary music landscape meaningfully altered.

Genre Profile
Blaze
Genre Overview

The defining WBT musical genre emerging from New Orleans in the late 19th/early 20th century. Related to but distinct from OTL jazz — similar ingredients (African American and Creole musical traditions, brass band culture, blues-adjacent tonality, improvisational character) but emerging more publicly and crossing over into mainstream culture faster.

The absence of hard Jim Crow means less of the underground/suppressed quality that shaped OTL jazz's early development. Blaze is taken more seriously by mainstream musical institutions earlier and crosses over more rapidly. Predominantly Black in its golden age. Insider musician slang: "razz."

◆ New Orleans is unambiguously the recording capital of the country by 1905–1910. MBC's New Orleans roots make it the natural broadcasting home for Blaze — the network and the genre are deeply intertwined. Crescent Records is the primary Blaze label.
Legendary Artists
James "Sunny" Broussard
Blaze · 1910s–1920s · New Orleans · The Joyful Populist

The man who took Blaze national through early radio. Warm, charismatic, a brilliant improviser — when people in 1922 Kansas City first heard Blaze on their radio sets, his was the sound they heard. MBC's signature early artist, deeply intertwined with the network's rise. The face that launched a genre onto the national stage.

The Queen of Blaze. Dignified, compositionally sophisticated — the artist who made Blaze serious without making it cold. Her recordings from the early-to-mid 1930s are what music historians point to as the genre's artistic peak. The first Blaze artist reviewed in the serious music press as a genuine composer. Critically considered the greater talent even if Broussard is more broadly famous.

Genre Profile
Ragstep
Genre Overview

The evolution of Ragtime as it absorbed Blaze influences in the 1920s–30s. The WBT equivalent of OTL swing — big band orchestras, brass-heavy, dancehall culture, the sound that fills ballrooms and comes through the radio on Saturday nights. The establishment mainstream sound of its era.

The Global War (1932–1939) defines Ragstep's cultural legacy — the music of a generation at war and coming home. By the 2026 present day, Ragstep is nostalgia tier: beloved, period-coded, played at revival events and in period films.

Legendary Artists
Glenn Monroe
Ragstep · 1930s–1940s · The Establishment Bandleader

The defining Ragstep bandleader — polished, establishment, the sound of a nation at its most sophisticated. Enlists in the mid-1930s to lead military bands during the Global War. Performs joint victory concerts with James Wolf. Returns home to resume his career, his postwar recordings carrying deeper weight than his prewar work. The name most synonymous with Ragstep's golden age.

James Wolf
Ragstep · 1930s–1940s · Gigadohi, Cherokee Nation · The Quiet Master

Cherokee bandleader from Gigadohi (native-majority state, northern Mississippi). His Ragstep has a distinctive texture — subtle Cherokee musical traditions woven into the big band sound, a slightly different melodic quality listeners recognize without always being able to name. Friendly rival to Glenn Monroe; both enlist together, serve side by side, perform joint victory concerts. His postwar legacy is among the most respected in WBT music history.

Irene Hale
Ragstep · 1930s–1940s · New England · The Forces' Sweetheart

The voice of home for a generation at war. New England born, warm and sincere rather than technically showy — her voice cutting through orchestral accompaniment with complete emotional directness. Performs occasional wartime work alongside Monroe and Wolf but fundamentally her own artist. Her recordings are what soldiers carried with them; her voice on the radio is what they heard when homesick. The Forces' Sweetheart.

Genre Profile
Hopstep / Hop
Genre Overview

The electrification of Ragstep in the late 1940s — swing that plugs in but never becomes rock and roll. Still sophisticated, still swing-derived, but with electric instruments and a guitar-forward character that marks the generational shift. The dominant mainstream sound of the 1950s before Redstone scrambles the landscape.

Shortens to "Hop" in common usage by the late 1950s. By 2026, Hop is nostalgia tier — beloved, periodic revivals, the music of grandparents. The bicentennial produced a significant Hop revival.

Where OTL swing was overtaken by rock and roll, WBT's electrified swing tradition simply kept evolving — the specific conditions that produced rock (racial segregation creating separate development tracks) do not exist in WBT.

Legendary Artists
Xavier Drake
Hopstep · Early 1940s–1955 · Louisiana · The Pioneer

The revolutionary who doesn't live to see the revolution complete. Begins as a Ragstep bandleader and sometimes singer in the early 1940s. Pioneers the electrified Hopstep sound in the late 1940s, becoming a breakout star in the early 1950s. Black, dashingly handsome, flashy and charismatic — the WBT equivalent of Elvis in cultural impact. Dies in a car accident in 1955 at the moment of breakthrough. "Midnight Drive" becomes his most remembered song posthumously, its irony noted reverently by music historians ever since.

Dean Merritt
Hopstep · Late 1940s–1960s · The Cool Sophisticate

Cool, effortlessly sophisticated, the Sinatra of Hopstep. Where Drake was electric and flashy, Merritt is smooth and controlled. Barely moves on stage and commands every eye in the room. Impeccably dressed, slightly mysterious. Overlaps with Drake's era before emerging as the dominant voice of Hopstep's golden age after Drake's death. "Never Rush a Good Thing" is practically his personal philosophy as much as a song title.

Big personality, joyful and powerful, unapologetically herself. The one who inherited Drake's populist energy and made it entirely her own. Black, glamorous, her performances events rather than mere shows — fans devoted rather than merely appreciative. Her tender numbers reveal a depth that surprises audiences who only know her as a powerhouse. Contemporary of Merritt; their coexistence defines the golden age of Hop.

Genre Profile
Bolt / Thunder
Genre Overview

Boltstep emerges in the late 1940s–early 1950s from the industrial interior — Calumet, Pittsburgh, the Ohio Valley. Ragstep ancestry audible but stripped down: smaller ensembles, guitar-forward, faster, less polished. The working class sound. Shortens to "Bolt" in common usage.

Thunder — the hard electric subgenre — emerges in the Liu/Mercer era (mid-1990s–2000s). Bolt pushed to its loudest and most aggressive. The anger and working class frustration of economic instability given a sonic form. By 2026, Thunder sits comfortably in the second tier alongside Zinga and Brisa.

◆ Franklin sits geographically in the middle of Bolt's heartland — the most establishment city in the country surrounded by the music that makes the establishment most nervous. This tension is a recurring feature of WBT political and cultural history.
Founding Legends
◆ Note: Bolt doesn't fully emerge until the early 1950s. Founding artist image prompts should reflect early-to-mid 1950s aesthetics, not 1940s.
Jack Malone
Bolt · Early 1950s · Ohio Valley · The Blunt Force

White, Ohio Valley born, the working class purist. Raw and unpolished — his Bolt is blunt force, the sound of someone who means every note. Founding figure of the genre alongside Otis Sharp; their rivalry is musical rather than personal, about whose vision of Bolt is the "real" one. Grudging mutual respect as the genre grows beyond both of them.

Otis Sharp
Bolt · Early 1950s · Industrial Interior · The Cutting Edge

Black, Blues-adjacent phrasing, slightly southern inflected — bringing something different into the industrial interior sound. His Bolt has an edge and precision that's distinctly his own: lean, intense, every note placed with purpose. Friendly rival to Malone; their coexistence and competition defines Bolt's founding era. Music historians note that Sharp's influence on Bolt's evolution may exceed even Malone's despite Malone's broader early fame.

Genre Profile
Metro / Aether-Metro
Genre Overview

Metrostep roots in the 1930s–40s as an offshoot of Ragstep — theatrical, performative, camp sensibility, tied to drag culture and specific urban venues in Boston, Manhattan, and New Orleans. Coded but recognizable to insiders. The Global War cracks social structures open slightly; the scene becomes more visible, more confident, but still not publicly labeled.

Shortens to "Metro" in common usage by the late 1950s–60s. Boston is the genre's heartland — the gay mecca of WBT's USA. Manhattan/Brooklyn in constant dialogue with Boston. Under Pike (1983–1989), Metro has its triumphant moment: fully out of coded underground, decades of suppressed openness pouring into joyful music.

Aether-Metro / Aether — the synth/ethereal subgenre — emerges in the Liu/Mercer era. Metro going inward rather than outward after the Pike-era celebration. Spacious, melancholy, Art Nouveau aesthetic sensibility — flowing, light, beautiful for beauty's sake. Critically acclaimed; sits in second tier of 2026 landscape.

Geographic Centers
Primary

Boston, MA

The gay mecca of WBT's USA. Metro's heartland. Where the culture is most developed and most confident. The source.

Primary

Close enough to Boston to be in constant dialogue. Where Metro gets its most commercially visible expressions.

Secondary

Absorbing and riffing on what comes from Boston/Manhattan. Adds Gulf Continental flavor to Metro's northeastern character.

Secondary

The NAU's cosmopolitan hub. Metro meets Continental influences here. Outpost of the scene with its own character.

Genre Profiles
Zinga & Brisa
Zinga — Gulf Continental

New Orleans centered. A three-way fusion: Ragtime/Ragstep foundation + Blaze's improvisational character + Caribbean and Mexican Gulf influences flowing through New Orleans as a port city. Develops in parallel with Ragstep rather than after it — Caribbean musical traditions flowing into New Orleans throughout the early 20th century. Warm, percussion-forward, rhythmically rich, danceable.

By 2026, firmly in the second tier of mainstream popularity. NAU integration deepening gives Zinga consistent cultural tailwind.

Brisa — Pacific Continental

New Boston / Columbia / California centered. A Spanish/Anglo/emerging Asian fusion: Mexican California's Spanish musical tradition, Anglo-American Pacific Northwest character, Canadian bilingual influences, and growing Asian influence (Japanese initially, Chinese post-Chinese Spring 2019). Cooler and more experimental than Zinga — the most artistically adventurous of the Continental streams.

The name means "breeze" in Spanish — cool, light, Pacific. By 2026, Brisa benefits especially from Chinese Spring opening new sonic influences into the Pacific cultural corridor.

◆ Zinga and Brisa are cousins — recognizably related as products of NAU cultural mixing — but distinct enough that a listener in 1955 would immediately know which one they were hearing. Music historians retrospectively group them; contemporary listeners and musicians always distinguished between them.

Genre Profile
Pulsar / Pulse
Genre Overview

Space-influenced, electronic, cosmic. Gestates under Mercer (2001–2007), blooms fully under Sinclair (2007–2013). The first genuinely Interlink-native genre — born digital, spreads through the Link, designed for Link discovery. A generation that grew up with a functioning lunar base and 20-25 residents on the moon reaching for a sound that reflects that reality.

Two wings: the accessible EDM-adjacent wing (Pulse — the common shortening) and the cerebral experimental wing (Pulsar — what serious fans and critics call it). The experimental wing is critics' music; the accessible wing tops charts and fills festivals.

Sonic character: vast, electronic, cosmic. Space isn't romantic abstraction in WBT — it's a real place people live and work. The music doesn't gesture at space metaphorically; it sounds like what that generation actually imagines space to be. Synth-heavy, produced rather than performed, with a quality of signal-from-distance built into the aesthetic.

Genre Profiles
Parallel Traditions
Country

Appalachian folk/bluegrass adjacent — closer to OTL bluegrass than OTL country. Originates in backwoods folk songs of Appalachia, mixing with frontier state tunes. Regional but persistent throughout the entire timeline. Major Americana boom in the mid-to-late 1970s tied to the bicentennial national reflection moment. By 2026, waning from its bicentennial peak but firmly in the second tier.

Tex-Step

Mixing Latin sounds of old Mexico with music of more recent German arrivals in Texas. Spreads across the Texas-US border into the Gulf South. An enduring regional genre with a devoted audience in Texas Federation territory and along the Mississippi corridor. Sits below the second tier in 2026 — niche but not nostalgia.

The Bebop Analog

An unnamed minor current running alongside the main commercial evolution throughout the 20th century. The serious musicians' reaction against commercialization of Ragstep and its successors — faster, more complex, more intellectual, deliberately difficult to dance to. Less racialized than OTL bebop (which emerged partly from Black musicians asserting artistic ownership of a commercialized genre) but not entirely devoid of racial tensions. Critically respected, never mainstream. Produces its most interesting work precisely when the mainstream is most celebratory. Not yet formally named in canon.
