Track Three
Key Cultural Moments
Late 19th C — Early 1900s
Ragtime and Blaze emerge. New Orleans unambiguously the recording capital by 1905–1910. Crescent Records and MBC's New Orleans roots deeply intertwined with Blaze's rise.
1910s–1920s
Radio era begins. Blaze goes national through MBC. Ragstep emerges as Ragtime absorbs Blaze influence. James "Sunny" Broussard defines the Blaze golden age on radio.
1920s–1930s
Ragstep golden age. Glenn Monroe, James Wolf, and Irene Hale define the era. Big band orchestras, dancehall culture. Celestine Beaumont reaches the artistic peak of Blaze.
1932–1939
The Global War. Monroe and Wolf both enlist, lead military bands. Irene Hale becomes the Forces' Sweetheart. Joint victory concerts cement all three as legends. Seeds of gay rights movement planted — Metro's distant origins.
Late 1940s–1950s
Ragstep electrifies into Hopstep. Xavier Drake pioneers the transition, dies 1955. Dean Merritt and Loretta Hollis define Hopstep's golden age. Bolt, Metro, Zinga, and Brisa all emerging simultaneously.
1964 — Redstone, Minnesota
The Redstone Festival. The public collision of Bolt, Metro, Zinga, Brisa, and Hopstep. A cultural exhale at the end of Lincoln's presidency. The Redstone Generation defined by this moment.
Late 1960s
The Experimental Period. Joyful chaos following Redstone. Cross-pollination across all streams. No dominant synthesis — musicians moving freely between traditions.
Early–Mid 1970s
Langston era crystallization. Expansive, continental, ambitious mainstream sound. NAU Assembly, moon landing, bicentennial anticipation shaping cultural confidence.
1976
The Bicentennial. 200th anniversary of American independence. Country has its Americana boom. Major cultural touchstone across all genres.
1983–1989
The Pike Presidency and Long Boom. Metro's triumphant moment — fully out of coded underground. Zinga and Brisa become genuinely mainstream. Hop enters nostalgic territory.
Mid-1990s–2007
Post-Long Boom / Liu-Mercer era. Ottoman collapse ends the Long Boom. Anxious, melancholy register. Thunder and Aether-Metro emerge as the era's signature subgenres.
2007–2013
Sinclair era — Pulsar emerges. Space-influenced, electronic, cosmic. First Interlink-native genre. A generation that grew up with a functioning lunar base.
~2019
Chinese Spring. HuaWang dismantled. New sonic influences entering Brisa and Pulsar especially. Contemporary music landscape meaningfully altered.

Genre Profile
Blaze
Genre Overview

The defining WBT musical genre emerging from New Orleans in the late 19th/early 20th century. Related to but distinct from OTL jazz — similar ingredients (African American and Creole musical traditions, brass band culture, improvisational character) but crossing into mainstream culture faster due to the absence of hard Jim Crow. Predominantly Black in its golden age. Insider musician slang: "razz."

◆ New Orleans is unambiguously the recording capital of the country by 1905–1910. MBC's New Orleans roots make it the natural broadcasting home for Blaze. Crescent Records is the primary Blaze label.
Legendary Artists
Blaze · 1910s–1920s · New Orleans · The Joyful Populist
James "Sunny" Broussard

The man who took Blaze national through early radio. Warm, charismatic, a brilliant improviser — when people in 1922 Kansas City first heard Blaze on their radio sets, his was the sound they heard. MBC's signature early artist, deeply intertwined with the network's rise.

Genre Profile
Ragstep
Genre Overview

The evolution of Ragtime as it absorbed Blaze influences in the 1920s–30s. The WBT equivalent of OTL swing — big band orchestras, brass-heavy, dancehall culture, the sound that fills ballrooms and comes through the radio on Saturday nights. The Global War (1932–1939) defines Ragstep's cultural legacy. By 2026, Ragstep is nostalgia tier: beloved, period-coded, played at revival events and in period films.

Legendary Artists
Ragstep · 1930s–1940s · The Establishment Bandleader
Glenn Monroe

The defining Ragstep bandleader — polished, establishment, the sound of a nation at its most sophisticated. Enlists in the mid-1930s to lead military bands during the Global War. Friendly rival to James Wolf; both perform joint victory concerts. Returns home to resume a career deepened by wartime experience.

Genre Profile
Hopstep / Hop
Genre Overview

The electrification of Ragstep in the late 1940s — swing that plugs in but never becomes rock and roll. Still sophisticated, still swing-derived, but electric instruments and a guitar-forward character mark the generational shift. Dominant mainstream sound of the 1950s. Shortens to "Hop" in common usage. By 2026, nostalgia tier — beloved, periodic revivals.

Where OTL swing was overtaken by rock and roll, WBT's electrified swing tradition simply kept evolving — the specific conditions that produced rock (racial segregation creating separate development tracks) do not exist in WBT.

Legendary Artists
Hopstep · Early 1940s–1955 · Louisiana · The Pioneer
Xavier Drake

The revolutionary who doesn't live to see the revolution complete. Begins as a Ragstep bandleader in the early 1940s, pioneers the electrified Hopstep sound in the late 1940s, becomes a breakout star by the early 1950s. Black, dashingly handsome, flashy and charismatic. Dies in a car accident in 1955. "Midnight Drive" becomes his most remembered song posthumously — its irony noted reverently by music historians ever since.

Genre Profile
Bolt / Thunder
Genre Overview

Boltstep emerges in the early 1950s from the industrial interior — Calumet, Pittsburgh, the Ohio Valley. Ragstep ancestry audible but stripped down: smaller ensembles, guitar-forward, faster, less polished. The working class sound. Shortens to "Bolt" in common usage. By 2026, one of the two dominant mainstream genres.

Thunder — the hard electric subgenre — emerges in the Liu/Mercer era (mid-1990s–2000s). Bolt pushed to its loudest and most aggressive. By 2026, sits comfortably in the second tier.

◆ Franklin sits geographically in the middle of Bolt's heartland — the most establishment city in the country surrounded by the music that makes the establishment most nervous.

Genre Profile
Metro / Aether-Metro
Genre Overview

Metrostep roots in the 1930s–40s as an offshoot of Ragstep — theatrical, performative, camp sensibility, tied to drag culture and specific urban venues in Boston, Manhattan, and New Orleans. Coded but recognizable to insiders. Shortens to "Metro" in common usage by the late 1950s–60s. Boston is the genre's heartland — the gay mecca of WBT's USA.

Two distinct artistic traditions run through Metro's history: the theatrical/camp tradition (Sterling, Lumière, Divina, Corazón) and the satirical tradition (Goldman, DeLuca, Washington, Chevalier). Many artists blend both. Under Pike (1983–1989), Metro has its triumphant moment — fully out of coded underground, decades of suppressed openness pouring into joyful music.

Aether-Metro / Aether — the synth/ethereal subgenre — emerges in the Liu/Mercer era. Metro going inward rather than outward after the Pike-era celebration. Spacious, melancholy, Art Nouveau aesthetic sensibility. By 2026, Metro remains one of the two dominant mainstream genres alongside Bolt, with trans/non-binary rights as the next frontier in its ongoing political conversation.

Geographic Centers
Primary

Boston, MA

The gay mecca of WBT's USA. Metro's heartland. Where the culture is most developed and confident. The source of the theatrical/camp tradition.

Primary

Manhattan / Brooklyn

Close enough to Boston to be in constant dialogue. Home of Metro's satirical tradition. Where Metro gets its most commercially visible expressions.

Secondary

New Orleans

Absorbing and riffing on what comes from Boston/Manhattan. Adds Gulf Continental warmth to Metro's northeastern character.

Secondary

Calumet

The NAU's cosmopolitan hub. Metro meets Continental influences here. Home to Roland Washington and Jae Park.

Jewish, Boston. His songs have a second meaning operating on a completely different level from the surface reading. The bridge builder — moves in mainstream spaces while being unmistakably Metro to insiders.

Genre Profiles
Zinga & Brisa
Zinga — Gulf Continental

New Orleans centered. A three-way fusion: Ragtime/Ragstep foundation + Blaze's improvisational character + Caribbean and Mexican Gulf influences. Warm, percussion-forward, rhythmically rich, danceable. By 2026, firmly in the second tier. NAU integration deepening gives Zinga consistent cultural tailwind.

Brisa — Pacific Continental

New Boston / Columbia / California centered. Spanish/Anglo/emerging Asian fusion. Cooler and more experimental than Zinga. The name means "breeze" in Spanish. By 2026, benefits especially from Chinese Spring (2019) opening new sonic influences into the Pacific cultural corridor.

◆ Zinga and Brisa are cousins — recognizably related as products of NAU cultural mixing — but a listener in 1955 would immediately know which one they were hearing.

Pulsar / Pulse
Genre Overview

Space-influenced, electronic, cosmic. Gestates under Mercer (2001–2007), blooms fully under Sinclair (2007–2013). The first genuinely Interlink-native genre. Two wings: accessible EDM-adjacent (Pulse) and cerebral experimental (Pulsar). Sonic character: vast, electronic, cosmic — space as a real place people live and work, not romantic abstraction.

Parallel Traditions
Country

Appalachian folk/bluegrass adjacent — closer to OTL bluegrass than OTL country. Regional but persistent. Major Americana boom mid-to-late 1970s tied to the bicentennial. By 2026, waning from that peak but firmly in the second tier.

Tex-Step

Latin/German Texian fusion. Enduring regional genre with devoted audience in Texas Federation territory and along the Mississippi corridor. Sits below the second tier in 2026 — niche but not nostalgia.

The Bebop Analog

An unnamed minor current running alongside the main commercial evolution throughout the 20th century. The serious musicians' reaction against commercialization — faster, more complex, more intellectual, deliberately difficult to dance to. Less racialized than OTL bebop but not entirely devoid of racial tensions. Critically respected, never mainstream. Not yet formally named in canon.
