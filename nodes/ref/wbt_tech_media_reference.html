Track One
The Telephone & the USP&T
The Inventors
Co-Inventor

Harlan Voss

P&T line supervisor — the network mind. Deep understanding of how the national telegraph infrastructure functions at ground level. Based in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania.

Co-Inventor

Silas Dunbar

Electrician and instrument maker — the mechanical mind. Close personal connection to Voss (friends or brothers-in-law, TBD). Also Pittsburgh-based.

First successful voice transmission: 1879. Patent filed early 1880. Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania is the base of operations — already home to Julius Lowry's steammobile work, establishing western Pennsylvania as a significant innovation corridor.

The Voss-Dunbar Settlement (1880–1882)

The USP&T contests the patent on grounds of Voss's P&T employment. Resolved through the courts. Outcome: private parties control manufacturing and equipment; USP&T controls exchange operation and trunk line infrastructure. This settlement is the legal foundation of the American telephone industry — the hybrid model.

The USP&T — Institutional Name Evolution
Pre-1882
United States Post & Telegraph Office — the name established in the Littlefield era. Abbreviated USP&T or just P&T.
1882 onward
Name unchanged. "T" informally understood to cover telephone as well as telegraph. USP&T offices serve both telegraph and telephone functions simultaneously through the transition period.
1930s
Formal quiet rename to United States Post & Telephone Office. Abbreviation USP&T unchanged. "T" now stands for Telephone rather than Telegraph. Most people barely notice.
Telegraph Fade-Out

Slightly slower than OTL due to government absorption of operating costs — no profit motive to kill the service artificially early. Mass medium death: early 1930s. Specialized survival (maritime, military, remote areas) into the 1940s. Telegraph remains viable for formal written-record communication longer than telephone for personal use.

Rollout Timeline
Early 1880s
Wealthy businesses in major cities only. Drake-era budget cuts slow USP&T exchange construction — meaningful drag on rollout.
Late 1880s
Post-Drake rebuilding accelerates exchange infrastructure. Mid-sized businesses as primary market.
1890s
Standard in business and government. USP&T operators (predominantly women) become a familiar cultural institution.
~1900
Residential service begins in major cities for middle and upper classes. Rural access essentially nonexistent.
Payphone Rollout
Late 1880s–Early 1890s
Telephone service added at existing USP&T office counters alongside telegraph. Natural extension of existing foot traffic.
Mid-1890s
Booths at USRC train stations, post offices, and major transit hubs.
Early 1900s
Street coin-operated booths in major cities. "Finding a P&T booth" becomes a normal urban activity.
Private Sector

Four major companies from the telegraph era all move into telephone service. New manufacturing companies emerge, with Pittsburgh a natural hub. Regional operators connect to USP&T trunk lines under licensing arrangements.

Atlantic Signal & Dispatch

The most aggressive and litigious. Fought the 1880 Telegraph Standardization Act in court and lost. Pushes hardest into telephone service, constantly testing the Settlement's limits.

Pacific Signal Company

Welcomed the standardization act as leveling the playing field. Most cooperative with the hybrid model. Strong in the West.

Continental Telegraph & Wire

Mid-sized, steady. Pivots into manufacturing handsets and exchange equipment as much as operating service.

Southern Wire

Regional strength in the South and Gulf Coast. Last major holdout of pure telegraph service before the medium fades.

Terminology
"telephone"
Formal name. Universal from the start.
"phone"
Standard casual shorthand. Wins quickly over competing terms.
"ring" (verb)
Standard verb for making a call. "Ring me when you arrive."
"wire" (telegraph only)
Exclusively telegraph language. Never applied to telephone. The distinction is absolute.
"the operator"
Colloquial for USP&T exchange operators. Formally "P&T operators."
North American Context

Canada

Mirrors USP&T model closely with its own federal Post & Telegraph equivalent. Easiest NAU integration partner — compatible institutional structure.

Texas Federation

Private patchwork — strong urban coverage, weak rural, less centralized regulation. Politically contentious NAU integration. Texas's laissez-faire instincts clash with the standardized model.

Mexico

Lags in buildout. Significant foreign (American and British) company presence in telephone infrastructure. Complicated political dimensions as NAU develops.

◆ Cross-border trunk line authority becomes a genuine NAU friction point when the North American Conference framework develops. At least three substantially different telephone regulatory models must eventually talk to each other.

Track Two
Audio Recording, Radio & Broadcast Media
The Audiograph — Gideon Macon
Inventor

Gideon Macon

Richmond, Virginia. Working/middle class, old Richmond family without old money. Printing and mechanical trades background. Single inventor — not a partnership.

The Invention

First Successful Recording: 1882

Disc-based format from the start — no cylinder phase. The device is called the audiograph; the practice and industry are called audiography.

1882–1883 is remembered in-universe as "the miracle years" or "the great invention window" — Macon's audiograph (1882), Peterson's electric light (1883), and Lowry's steammobile (1883) all arriving within roughly eighteen months of each other.

Macon Audiograph Company (MAC)

Incorporated in Richmond, 1883. Philadelphia investor — printing industry or electrical manufacturing background. Macon serves as technical director and public face initially. Gradually pushed out through the late 1880s–early 1890s as professional management takes over and ownership dilutes through successive funding rounds.

Macon returns to Richmond — celebrated locally as the city's most famous son, financially comfortable but peripheral to the industry he created. Dies as a respected but somewhat melancholy figure.

MAC eventually expands into radio receiver manufacturing during the 1920s boom. "Buying a MAC" becomes standard consumer shorthand for both audiograph players and radio sets. MAC sets known for clean industrial design.

The Record Labels
Long-Term Dominant

Crescent Records

New Orleans anchored. Most artistically prestigious label long-term. Primary label home of Blaze music. Founded when local businessmen realized major labels weren't capturing the city's scene.

Long-Term Dominant

Republic Audiograph Company

The establishment label. Philadelphia or Manhattan headquartered. Orchestral recordings, popular vocal performers, the respectable mainstream middle. "The label your grandmother buys."

Long-Term Dominant

Macon Records

Carries the inventor's name. Starts as a MAC house label, eventually spun off or sold. Macon himself has little connection to it by the time it matters — a melancholy irony.

Long-Term Dominant

American Audiograph Company

The populist label. Broad catalogue, aggressive distribution. Most associated with working class musical tastes. Willing to record genres Republic turns its nose up at.

Significant Early / Later Fades

Continental Records

Significant early player. Fades as industry consolidates around the dominant four.

Significant Early / Later Fades

Liberty Records

Significant early player. Fades as industry consolidates around the dominant four.

Blaze — The New Orleans Sound

The defining WBT musical genre emerging from New Orleans in the late 19th/early 20th century. Related to but distinct from OTL jazz — similar ingredients (African American and Creole musical traditions, brass band culture, blues-adjacent tonality, improvisational character) but emerging more publicly and crossing over into mainstream culture faster.

The absence of hard Jim Crow means less of the underground/suppressed quality that shaped OTL jazz's early development. Blaze is taken more seriously by mainstream musical institutions earlier and crosses over more rapidly.

"blaze"
The mainstream and historical term. What the history books, record labels, and radio announcers call it.
"razz"
Older insider musician slang. Still used by musicians among themselves even after "blaze" wins as the public term.
◆ New Orleans is unambiguously the recording capital of the country by 1905–1910. MBC's New Orleans roots make it the natural broadcasting home for blaze music — the network and the genre are deeply intertwined.
Radio — Pioneer Figures
The Voice Breakthrough

Edmund Cray

Physicist, Columbia University, Brooklyn. First successful voice transmission over radio waves: 1903. First true radio broadcast (small invited audience): 1904. Celebrated public face of radio's invention. Career academic — content with legacy in contrast to Macon's more melancholy relationship with his own.

The Naval Engineer

Josiah Pruett

Navy engineer, Franklin naval research installation. Makes shipboard wireless operational through the 1890s. Work classified during development. Becomes chief FBA technical advisor under Hannah — first major public recognition.

The Commercial Bridge

Walter Greer

Manhattan engineer-entrepreneur. Reads Cray's papers in 1904, immediately sees commercial potential. Founds one of earliest commercial stations in Manhattan, 1912–1913. Seed of what becomes ABN. Better known in business history than public memory.

Radio Development Timeline
1890s
Naval development, classified. Pruett and colleagues working invisibly. Atlantic Signal & Dispatch monitoring nervously. No public hobbyist community.
1900–1904
Cray's breakthrough. Voice transmission (1903), demonstration broadcast (1904). Small but growing civilian hobbyist market forming around wireless telegraphy. Naval radio operational and routine (Harrington incident, 1904). MAC begins quietly developing radio receiver components.
1905–1910
The Hobbyist Decade. Radio clubs form in most major cities. Technical magazines publish receiver building instructions. Newspaper experimental stations launch (Franklin Courier, Brooklyn Eagle among first). NBS concept discussed in government circles by 1908–1909.
Dec 31, 1910
Roosevelt's New Year's Address — first presidential radio broadcast. Reaches "a few hundred" receivers in the Franklin-Dayton corridor. Those who hear it live feel like witnesses to history.
1911–1912
FBA and NBS established under Hannah. FBA dual mandate: license private broadcasters, oversee NBS. NBS flagship Franklin station launches. JCA reaction immediate — "Franklin telling you what to think over the wireless." Feeds 1914 midterm JCA wave.
1913
Hyperion sinking — 24-hour maritime radio operator regulations mandated. Broad bipartisan support. Creates significant professional class of trained radio workers.
1910s–1920s
Organic transition from hobby to commercial. No mass military training pipeline as catalyst (no WWI equivalent). Driven by falling receiver prices, improving programming, advertising money, FBA framework giving operators investment confidence.
1918–1928
The Boom. Receiver prices drop dramatically. Blaze music drives listenership. Live sports broadcasting significant by mid-1920s. Regional networks forming and consolidating. NBS finds its identity as broadcaster of record.
Late 1920s–Early 1930s
Network consolidation. ABN, MBC, CBC, PBN, SBC fully established. Broadcasting technology improving — radio genuinely pleasurable rather than a technical achievement.
By 1932
Radio as embedded in daily life as the telephone. Palmer's armistice address (1939/1940) one of the most-listened broadcasts in WBT history.
The Broadcasting Landscape
Regulatory Body
Regulator

Federal Broadcasting Authority (FBA)

Established under Hannah, 1911/1912. Dual mandate: licenses private broadcasters and oversees the NBS. Early years mostly technical standards and licensing. One of Hannah's initiatives that feeds the JCA's federal overreach narrative in the 1914 midterms.

Public Broadcaster
Public / Franklin

National Broadcasting Service (NBS)

Headquartered in Franklin. News, government addresses, educational content, cultural programming. Funded through federal appropriation. Broadcaster of record for major national events. All commercial networks maintain Franklin bureaus alongside it. Structurally independent but financially vulnerable to congressional budget pressure — JCA uses NBS funding fights as a recurring political tool.

Commercial Networks
National Giant / Manhattan

American Broadcasting Network (ABN)

The dominant commercial giant. Corporate, advertising-driven, national ambitions from the start. Manhattan-based. Absorbs the Southern Broadcasting Company (SBC) in the late 1940s/early 1950s as telecinema economics make SBC's independence untenable. SBC's western fringe markets go to MBC; the SBC name retires, felt as a genuine cultural loss in the South.

National Giant / New Orleans

Meridian Broadcasting Company (MBC)

The culturally distinct national giant. New Orleans-based. Wears its Mississippi/Gulf South identity as a deliberate brand choice — "the emotional halfway point of the country." Deeply tied to the recorded music industry and blaze music. Close (occasionally controversial) relationship with Crescent Records. Picks up SBC's western fringe markets on ABN absorption.

Strong Second-Tier / Heartland

Continental Broadcasting Company (CBC)

Calumet/Hugosville co-anchored. The heartland network serving industrial Midwest through interior West. Positions itself against both coastal giants. Note: "CBC" also used for Canadian Broadcasting equivalent — occasional in-universe confusion, especially as NAU integration develops.

Pacific Regional / New Boston

Pacific Broadcasting Network (PBN)

New Boston-anchored. Serves Oregon, Clark, and Washingtonia. Significant within its territory. Affiliated with one of the nationals for programming it can't produce independently but maintains distinct regional identity.

Southern Regional (absorbed ~1950s)

Southern Broadcasting Company (SBC)

Atlanta-anchored. Upper South and Appalachian territory. The proud independent Southern voice. Holds out through the radio era and into early telecinema before ABN absorption. Its disappearance felt as a genuine cultural loss in the South.

Broadcast News & Regulatory Framework

The NBS is not the only news broadcaster — all commercial networks develop their own news divisions, driven by competitive instinct and direct ties to the newspaper industry that seeded early radio stations. The same newspaper groups that launched experimental stations develop integrated news organizations combining print and broadcast speed.

NBS News

Nonpartisan public broadcaster. Deep international coverage. Maintains its own independent correspondent network — genuinely editorially independent from wire services. The broadcaster of record for major national events.

ABN News

Most powerful commercial operation. Manhattan location, closest ties to northeastern newspaper establishment. Centrist-establishment perspective. Breaks national stories first most often.

MBC News

Southern and Gulf Coast perspective. Strong regional coverage. Most consistent coverage of civil rights and racial politics, reflecting its New Orleans audience base.

CBC News

Heartland perspective. Strong agricultural and industrial coverage. Trusted by farmers and factory workers. Skeptical of coastal establishment narratives.

PBN News

Pacific coast perspective. Strong on Pacific trade, California relations, NAU integration stories. The network most likely to cover NAU integration with genuine depth.

Regulatory Timeline
1911–1912
Public Interest Standard baked into FBA licensing from day one. License conditions require minimum public affairs airtime, news/opinion/entertainment distinction, community needs response. License renewal is the enforcement mechanism.
1920s
Fairness Doctrine equivalent. FBA formalizes requirement to present contrasting viewpoints on matters of public controversy. Not equal time — just multiple perspectives represented. Generates JCA opposition but passes.
1930s
Structural Separation Requirements. Added as Global War-era conflict-of-interest prevention. News divisions must have independent budgets and editorial leadership. Advertisers cannot have editorial input. Framed as organizational structure regulation, not content regulation.
1950s–1960s
Single-channel era. News-entertainment separation exists organizationally within one broadcast operation. Opinion shows exist but are contained — fifteen-minute commentary segments, clearly labeled.
Late 1950s–1960s
Multi-channel TC era begins. Networks launch dedicated news and entertainment channels. Opinion programming expands dramatically on news channels. Legal challenge builds.
1969
Public Broadcasting Alliance v. American Broadcasting Network filed. ABN's dedicated news channel fills significant airtime with opinion programming during the emotionally charged Choson War period. PBA files formal license renewal challenge.
1970
The Opinion Ruling. Late Schultz presidency (Federalist, 1965–1971). Court finds opinion programming cannot be classified as news under FBA structural separation requirements. Opinion shows must air on entertainment channels. Two-channel model required. Major JCA controversy — framed as government censorship. Becomes a 1970 election issue. Langston administration implements framework.
Early 1970s onward
Two-channel model standard across all major networks. Opinion-news distinction culturally understood and legally enforced. Framework holds under continuous political pressure — imperfect but meaningfully better than OTL.
Daily Life Texture — Radio Era

The Radio as Furniture

By the late 1920s, the home radio set is a piece of furniture as much as an appliance. MAC sets known for clean industrial design; other manufacturers favor ornate wooden cabinets for the parlor.

Programming Culture

Families organize evenings around schedules. MBC's blaze music hour, ABN's nightly news, NBS Sunday cultural programming become social institutions.

Language

"Tuning in" and "tuning out" enter common speech as metaphors almost immediately. "What are you tuned to?" is a standard social question by the mid-1920s. NBS listeners sometimes called "public sets" by commercial fans — mild affectionate insult.

Rural Lag

Rural adoption lags urban significantly through the 1920s due to incomplete electrification. The Global War is the first major national event genuinely reaching everyone simultaneously — rural electrification is sufficiently complete by the 1930s.

Track Three
Cinema & Telecinema
Cinema — The Inventors

Moving image technology invented nearly simultaneously in three nations in the early-to-mid 1890s. First public exhibitions: 1895–1897. Cinemagraphic halls multiplying through the early 1900s with a meaningful exhibition network by 1906.

American Inventor

Vera Hollis

Brooklyn-born, white, working-class to middle-class background. Connected to Brooklyn's commercial photography and optical instrument culture. First projection to a small private audience: 1894 or 1895. Builds the independent Hollis Studio. Relocates to St. Augustine, Florida ~1912–1915 for weather. Other filmmakers follow — Hollis plants the flag. Celebrated as the mother of American cinema.

Prussian Inventor

Otto Haas

Frankfurt am Main based. Technical university tradition — formally trained in optical physics and mechanical engineering. More theoretically oriented. Prussian/German cinema develops reputation for technical precision and artistic seriousness.

Italian Inventor

Marco Conti

Naples based. Theater and performance background — a showman first, technician second. Italian cinema emphasizes spectacle and emotional storytelling rooted in Naples's operatic and theatrical traditions.

Film Industry Geography

St. Augustine, Florida

American film center — WBT's Hollywood. Year-round sunshine, diverse landscapes, national rail access. Established by Vera Hollis's relocation decision. "Cinema" and "film" terminology standard.

Frankfurt am Main

Primary European cinema center. Prussian technical and commercial infrastructure. German cinema known for precision and artistic seriousness.

Naples

Secondary European center. Distinct Italian cinema tradition — melodramatic, visually spectacular, operatically influenced.

San Diego, Union of the Californias

Californian film center. Eventually competes with St. Augustine as NAU integration opens borders. SoCal "movies" terminology creates stylistic distinction from St. Augustine's "cinema" tradition.

Synchronized Sound

The technical pieces are available earlier in WBT than OTL due to the more mature audiography infrastructure — Gideon Macon's disc-based recording technology has been developing since 1882. The challenge of synchronization is solved in the late 1910s to early 1920s, possibly as early as 1919 — roughly a decade ahead of OTL. The talkie transition in WBT cinema is significantly earlier.

Telecinema — The Inventors

Telecinema emerges from collaboration between radio engineering and cinema — it inherits the culture and infrastructure of both industries simultaneously. The name "telecinema" reflects this dual origin: cinema transmitted at a distance.

Radio Engineering Side

Arthur Cade

Brooklyn-based. Worked at or near ABN or predecessor stations in the early 1920s. Technically rigorous, thinks in terms of signal transmission and frequency. His contribution: the transmission side — how to get a moving image through a radio wave reliably and with sufficient resolution.

Cinema Side

Joseph Wren

Brooklyn-based. Trained in the Brooklyn film world before Hollis's departure thinned the local industry. His contribution: the imaging side — how to capture a moving image electronically rather than photographically so it can be converted into a transmittable signal.

◆ Cade and Wren meet through overlapping professional circles in Brooklyn in the early-to-mid 1920s. The Cade-Wren demonstration — first public showing of moving image transmission — occurs approximately 1929 or 1930, just as Global War clouds are gathering.
Telecinema Development Timeline
Late 1920s
Laboratory experiments. First successful transmission — grainy, low-resolution, sent across a Brooklyn rooftop. Unmistakably moving. Reported in scientific press and specialist publications.
Early–Mid 1930s
Global War period. Military applications drive some development. War simultaneously slows consumer development (manufacturing redirected) and accelerates technical aspects (military funding). Limited experimental broadcasts in major cities.
Late 1930s
Postwar launch. Peace returns, manufacturing capacity frees up. Experimental public broadcasts begin in Franklin, Brooklyn, and other major cities. NBS launches experimental TC service. Commercial networks begin TC operations.
Early 1940s
Limited consumer sets. Sparse programming — a few hours per day, mostly news and special events. Sets expensive — upper-middle class and wealthy households only.
1946
First election broadcast simultaneously on radio and telecinema. Still new enough that a candidate's "instinctive command of the new telecinema medium" is a meaningful differentiating skill.
Late 1940s–1950s
Mass market adoption. TC sets become affordable for middle class families. Programming expands dramatically. SBC's inability to compete in TC era drives its ABN absorption.
Telecinema Terminology & Daily Life
"telecinema"
The technology and industry name. Understood as cinema transmitted at a distance — reflects dual origins.
"TC set"
The home receiver. Significant household purchase — families save up for one. Living room centerpiece.
"watching the TC"
Standard phrase. "What's on the TC tonight?" enters common speech.
◆ Political candidates begin being coached on TC presentation by the late 1940s — the medium rewards a different kind of performance than radio. The 1946 election broadcast is remembered as a watershed moment — the first time most TC-owning Americans watched a political event unfold in moving images in their own homes.

Track Four
Computing & The Interlink
The First Computers
First Machine

The UER Computer (~1943–1945)

Primary motivation: codebreaking during the Global War (1932–1939). Secondary: economic planning for a complex planned economy. Vacuum tubes, room-filling, team of operators required. Classified for several years. Western intelligence pieces together what the UER has built through the mid-to-late 1940s.

American Response

The American Machine (Early–Mid 1950s)

Nolan administration (1947–1953) launches emergency computing program once UER intelligence becomes clear. "The UER has a thinking machine and we don't" fits Nolan-era paranoia perfectly. Sun bomb calculations (NAU test autumn 1951, China early 1952) are a parallel driver. First working machine arrives end of Nolan or early Priest. Institutional credit goes to Priest even though Nolan started it.

American Computing Geography
Primary Cluster — Northeast

Philadelphia (Center of Gravity)

University of Pennsylvania is the primary early American computing center. Strongest historical parallel. Existing scientific infrastructure.

Primary Cluster — Northeast

Brooklyn & Boston

Columbia University (Brooklyn) — already established WBT innovation hub. Harvard (Boston) — strong mathematical tradition.

Secondary Cluster — Midwest

Calumet

Hardware manufacturing hub. University of Illinois computing research. Oliver Allen's electrical engineering legacy makes it the natural center for computing hardware production.

Secondary Cluster — Midwest

Mahkato, Minnesota

The distinctive WBT choice. University of Minnesota gives Minnesota a significant computing history role it does not have in OTL. An unexpected location that becomes a key early research center.

◆ No Silicon Valley equivalent within US borders — the Union of the Californias is not part of the USA. California develops its own computing industry independently, centered on San Francisco. The question of whether Philadelphia/Brooklyn or San Francisco is the commercial center of gravity is a recurring NAU tension through the 1990s–2000s.
Computing Terminology
Same as OTL
computer
Straightforwardly descriptive. Emerges naturally.
hardware
Too good a metaphor to lose. Wins in WBT as in OTL.
bug
Universal computing error term.
program
General term for computer instructions. Competes with "script" early on.
WBT-Specific or Divergent
script
Early term for a computer program, drawn from broadcasting culture. Used particularly 1940s–1960s before "program" fully dominates. Survives in specific contexts (automated scripts) even after "program" wins.
apparatus / plant
Formal/technical terms for a complete computer installation. "The apparatus is down." Used by engineers and institutions talking about whole systems.
kit
Informal everyday term for a personal computing setup, particularly in Canada and among younger users. Reflects NAU's British-influenced vocabulary. "What kit are you running?"
works
Casual shorthand for physical components specifically. "Something's wrong with the works." A technician's term when diagnosing a physical problem.

Track Four — Continued
The Interlink
What Is the Interlink?

The Interlink — colloquially "The Link" — is not a single unified network like OTL's internet. It is a network of networks: separate national and bloc networks connected through agreed protocols and treaties. Each network develops independently with its own architecture, addressing conventions, and organizational assumptions. The Interlink is the meta-layer connecting them.

The protocol identifier in all Interlink addresses is link:// — referencing the Interlink itself.

The Interlink
Formal name for the global network of networks.
The Link
Colloquial shorthand. "Are you on The Link?"
bridge
A clickable hyperlink — the thing you click to navigate to another node. Completely distinct from "link" despite sharing a root concept. "Send me the bridge," "click the bridge," "I'll bridge you that node."
node
An individual destination or computer on the Interlink. Already a living term in network engineering, adopted naturally into public vocabulary.
on the Link / off the Link
Connected / not connected to the Interlink.
Interlink Development Timeline
1980s
National/bloc networks developing independently — no international connection. Each network is a silo. A NANet user cannot reach a ResEu node. Mass consumer adoption within national networks. The Long Boom (from Pike presidency, 1983) creates economic conditions for commercial expansion.
Late 1980s–1990s
Cross-network pressure builds. Businesses operating across borders need electronic communication. Academics want to share data. Workarounds proliferate: dedicated bilateral links between specific institutions, physical media exchange, early email-adjacent systems through diplomatic channels.
1998–2000
Interlink negotiations begin. Multilateral process hosted through GPC framework and bilateral diplomatic channels. Covers protocol standards, addressing compatibility, governance structure, security, and cost-sharing.
2002–2004
Interlink Treaty signed. Initial connections go live between founding members: NANet, ResEu, UKNet, BharatNet, RedSur, AfricaNet, AnZacNet, APN. The Interlink exists. Most of the democratic world is now connected.
2005–2006
China officially joins — formal two-tier system. Government and approved commercial nodes fully accessible internationally. Academic institutions have controlled access. Ordinary consumer access heavily filtered through government-controlled gateways. Most Chinese households experience a closed national network despite formal Interlink membership. Becomes a recurring human rights and trade issue.
2007–2009
RusSet joins. Informal rather than formal restrictions — state pressure on operators produces practical content control without China's systematic architecture. Messier but real in effect.
2010s
Full global integration. Interlink becomes critical infrastructure worldwide. Mobile access transforms usage patterns. Governance disputes intensify. UKNet integration debate begins — ResEu or NANet?
2019
Chinese Spring. Commitment to genuine Interlink access as part of reform settlement. Two-tier HuaWang system begins formal dismantling.
2020s (Nakamura era)
HuaWang dismantling underway but incomplete. NAU policy toward China's network opening is active diplomatic question. UKNet integration debate unresolved. NAU Constitution ratification includes Interlink governance provisions.
The UKNet Integration Debate

The ResEu Option

Geographic logic strong but cultural fit poor. ResEu's architecture reflects French and German institutional priorities. Organization codes alone (.com vs .ent, .gov vs .gouv) would require massive retrofitting of existing British nodes.

The NANet Option

Anglophone argument compelling — shared language, shared conventions (.com and .gov already compatible). Significant geopolitical signal: Britain formally aligning with NAU rather than Europe. Some Commonwealth nations (Australia via AnZacNet) would likely follow.

The Independence Option

UKNet stays independent, negotiates bilateral agreements with both. Smaller Commonwealth-adjacent nations prefer this. Probably the path of least resistance — default position. Unresolved in the current timeline endpoint.

Interlink Address Guide
Address Structure

General format: link://[network].[sitename].[orgcode].[countrycode]

Country codes absent where the network is purely national (UKNet, HuaWang). Some networks treat country codes as afterthoughts — built as purely national infrastructure before expanding or fragmenting.

Networks With Country Codes as Afterthoughts

Networks built as purely national infrastructure have different architectural relationships to country codes. HuaWang and BharatNet were built as single-nation networks — country codes are redundant and rarely used internally, only appearing in Interlink-facing addresses for external navigation. Early UER nodes in ResEu often lack country codes entirely, added only as non-UER European states joined the network.
