The Museum of the United States is the national museum of American history, dedicated to preserving the history of the republic and sharing it with the people to whom it belongs. Admission has been free since opening day.

Founding, 1879

The Museum opened its doors on February 22, 1879 — George Washington's Birthday — with President Littlefield presiding over the dedication ceremony. The founding came at a moment of national reflection: the republic had just celebrated its centennial, the generation that remembered the founding era was passing from living memory, and there was a growing conviction that the narratives of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries — and the recent War Between the States — must be gathered and preserved before they were lost.

From its first day, the Museum's core exhibit presented artifacts of the early founding, including an original copy of the Declaration of Independence, copies of both the 1787 and 1861 Constitutions, and items belonging to several former presidents.

From the Smithsonian Research Institute

The Museum began as an outgrowth of the Smithsonian Research Institute, which had originally embraced all aspects of academic research and education. As the Institute turned increasingly toward the sciences under its director, Peter Talbot, Talbot himself became the leading advocate for a separate institution devoted to the nation's history. Working closely with the University of the United States and with Congress, he secured the establishment of the new museum — ensuring that the history of the republic would have a dedicated organization of its own.

The Building & the Great Hall

The Museum occupies a three-story domed building on Union Avenue in Franklin, just east of Congress Hall and directly across the street from the University of the United States — placing it at the heart of the capital's civic and academic life.

Beneath the dome, the central atrium — the Great Hall of the Republic — has welcomed every visitor since opening day. The Great Hall is the permanent home of John Trumbull's most celebrated works of patriotic American art: Declaration of Independence, Burning of the Federal City, and Victory at Yorktown. Below the public galleries, the Lower Level houses the National Archives Gallery and the preservation vaults that safeguard the nation's founding documents.

The Museum Today

Nearly a century and a half after its founding, the Museum continues the mission set out in 1879. It maintains its close partnership with the University of the United States to promote historical research nationwide, cares for a National Collection of well over a million objects and documents, and remains free to all visitors. In this semiquincentennial year, the Museum is preparing its landmark exhibition The Republic at 250, opening in the autumn of 2026.

Museum Milestones
1879The Museum of the United States opens on Washington's Birthday, February 22, with President Littlefield presiding.
1902The original parchments of the Declaration of Independence and both Constitutions are transferred to purpose-built preservation vaults on the Lower Level.
1926The sesquicentennial exhibition marks one hundred and fifty years of American independence.
1941The Global War gallery opens, documenting the conflict concluded at Geneva the year before.
1979The Museum marks its own centennial with a major restoration of the Great Hall of the Republic.
2026The semiquincentennial year. The Republic at 250 opens in the autumn.
Visiting the Museum The Museum is open daily except Mondays, 10 a.m. to 5:30 p.m., on Union Avenue in Franklin, D.W. Admission is free. The National Archives Gallery and special exhibitions require free timed entry passes.
