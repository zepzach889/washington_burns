Alright, I know this one's a warhorse, but I drove past First Capital Park last weekend and I can't stop thinking about it, so humor me.

August 1814. Ross and Cockburn are ashore in Maryland. Say Bladensburg goes differently — the militia actually holds the bridge for a few hours, or Ross decides an inland march against a defended city isn't worth the casualties and sticks to raiding the Chesapeake. Either way: Washington City is never burned. The Capitol wings, the President's House, the department buildings — all still standing when the war ends.

Questions for the board:

1. Does the capital stay on the Potomac? The abandonment only really snowballed after the burning — that 1817 congressional report about the city being derelict describes a city nobody wanted to rebuild, not a city nobody wanted, full stop.
2. Without a capital fight, is there even a Grand Compromise? And if there's no Compromise, there's no single six-year term. Presidents running for reelection. Sit with that one.
3. Longer term — does a Potomac capital change the sectional math at all when things fall apart in the 1850s?

Ground rules: keep it to the first few decades, and if your answer involves the British apologizing and going home, that's a ZLI post, take it next door.

The Ross-declines version is honestly the more plausible lever. Bladensburg holding takes a lot of hand-waving — that militia was never going to stand against peninsular regulars. But Ross was genuinely reluctant about the whole expedition; Cockburn talked him into it. One bout of dysentery in the right tent and the fleet stays in the Chesapeake burning tobacco warehouses instead.

Immediate consequence people always skip: no Philadelphia interregnum. The government spent thirteen years as a lodger in someone else's city, and that experience did a lot of quiet work on the national psyche. A government that never leaves the Potomac never develops the "temporary capital" mindset that made moving west thinkable in the first place.

This premise is older than the board. I was arguing it in the POD letters column when postage was cheaper than a modem, and the argument has not materially improved, so let me save everyone four pages:

The capital question is the boring half. The interesting half is constitutional. The Grand Compromise wasn't really about the capital — the capital was the sweetener that got the Western wing to swallow the Wisconsin Treaty. Crawford still has a dead vice president to replace, the Federalists still want their Hartford wishlist, and Madison's treaty still has to get through the Senate somehow. So you probably still get a compromise. But without the capital on the table, the Westerners have nothing to gain, and the whole package is smaller. Maybe the 13th Amendment passes alone.

Which means: no 14th. No single six-year term. Presidents seeking reelection, incumbents building machines, and no "next president can't be from the same state" rule quietly shaping every party's ticket for two centuries. That's the divergence worth chasing. Where the government happens to sit is downstream of it.

Obligatory cold water: the war is still a humiliation with or without the fires. Ghent still restricts the Mississippi. The Wisconsin Treaty cession probably still happens in some form, because the boundary mess exists independently of one burned city. The burning is the symbol of the bad war, not the substance of it.

And westward pressure doesn't evaporate. The population center was already sliding over the mountains. Even in your timeline, I'd bet money somebody introduces a capital-relocation bill by the 1840s. It probably fails without a burned city to point at — but the question stays on the table for decades, and Western politicians get a grievance to campaign on every time an appropriation for Potomac public buildings comes up.

It's not ZLI. It's just unlikely, and you should feel bad.

PlausibilityPolice said:
Even in your timeline, I'd bet money somebody introduces a capital-relocation bill by the 1840s.

Introduced, sure. Passed? Governments don't abandon functioning capitals — they abandon ruins. The 1817 report everyone cites describes decay because the burning emptied the city; private citizens left because the government's return kept getting less likely, not the other way around. Take away the fires and Washington City in 1817 is a shabby but working town with a sunk-cost Capitol two wings deep. Congress votes to finish the rotunda, grumbles about the swamp, and stays. Sunk cost is the most underrated force in this hobby.

Toast4Breakfast said:
is that inversion actually defensible, or is it just the screenwriters wanting the government to be the good guys for once?

We run this thread roughly once a decade — the 1998 iteration got so heated it's part of the reason the old scenario boards were reorganized, which the veterans will remember and the rest of you can go dig out of the archive.

The film's inversion is more defensible than people give it credit for. "Union" isn't a moral category, it's just whoever holds the federal machinery when the crisis breaks. In the real 1850s that was Lee and the Republicans, so abolition had to walk out; the film only needs a Federalist to win one presidential election instead, and suddenly it's the slave states doing the walking. Contingent, not ZLI.

Geography's the part the film doesn't think through. In the real war, the capital sat on the Ohio — right on the seam — which is how Brandt's forces could take it in '59, and Hawthorne had already decamped to Atlanta by then, which cost the government the initiative and, arguably, the legitimacy that came with the city. A Potomac capital is buried deep in Maryland and Virginia — loyal country in either version of the war. Northern secessionists could never have touched it. So the government side keeps its capital, keeps its nerve, and the war probably runs longer than the one we got. The film hand-waves that entirely.

And before anyone starts: yes, the Devil's Bargain of 1826 probably still happens in some form. Ambitious men find each other in any timeline.

Welcome aboard. More than you'd think — the north and south wing walls of the old Capitol, the paved plaza with the flagpole where the rotunda would have gone, and the restored President's House (it spent most of a century as a hotel, which is its own strange story). The Federal Parks interpretation is solid; go on a quiet weekday if you can.

If you can't make it east, the Museum of the United States here in Franklin has a special exhibition running right now on the burning and the move — The Burning and the Building — that covers the same ground with the actual documents. Decent primer for this thread, honestly.
